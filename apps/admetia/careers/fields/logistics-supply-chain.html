1. What this branch is

Logistics and supply chain work is about getting the right goods, in the right quantity, to the right place at the right cost: buying materials and products (procurement), deciding how much to make and stock (planning), moving things by road, rail, air and sea (transport and shipping), storing and handling them (warehousing), and making them (manufacturing operations). It is a large, mostly practical branch with many entry points and a lot of graduate openings compared with finance or consulting, but pay at entry is usually moderate and a good share of jobs are operational (shifts, sites, firefighting). Since 2020 the field has been in the news constantly: pandemic shortages, the Red Sea and Suez diversions from late 2023, US tariff turbulence, and in 2026 a new Gulf and Strait of Hormuz crisis. That makes the work more visible and more stressful, and it has raised the value of people who can plan around disruption. Technology (planning software, warehouse robots, AI "agents") is changing the entry-level jobs fastest, so the safest entrants are those who combine operational understanding with data skills.

Scope notes: Global view with explicit US, UK, continental Europe (Italy always called out) and Asia/Middle East (Singapore, Dubai) comparisons; "entry" means a graduate with a bachelor's or master's and no MBA; figures are dated and sourced, and where a figure is from a job posting, recruiter blog or aggregator it is labelled as a weak signal; no reliable Italian recruiter salary guide for 2025-26 was found, so Italian pay rests on a few postings and a training-site table and is marked indicative; generic operations/supply-chain analytics is covered in the data analytics report, general operations in the management report, consulting in the management consulting report, and physical commodity trading (which shares chartering skills) in the finance report; facts that come from my background knowledge rather than a source I opened are marked "unverified".

2. Map of areas and sectors

2026 context that shapes every area (dated).

  • Red Sea/Suez and Hormuz. Most container lines left the Red Sea route from late 2023 and sailed round the Cape of Good Hope, adding up to about two weeks to Asia-Europe voyages and absorbing roughly 2.5 million TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units, the standard container measure) of capacity out of a 34 million TEU fleet, about 7% (Xeneta, 2026, via Maritime Executive). Early 2026 saw a cautious return: large containership Suez transits rose 215% in the first half of 2026 versus a year earlier and Maersk restored a second Suez service (AGBI, July 2026). That was disrupted by the February 2026 US-Israel strikes on Iran and the Strait of Hormuz crisis that followed: Shanghai-Jebel Ali spot rates went from about US$1,800 per container in January 2026 to over US$7,000 by late March (Freightos data cited by AGBI/trade press), carriers added war-risk surcharges (Hapag-Lloyd: US$3,000 per 40-foot box, reported), and Maersk introduced an Emergency Freight Rate for several Gulf states in September 2026. Xeneta said in early October 2026 that rates had probably peaked, but Far East-US West Coast spot rates were still about 344% above 28 February levels and would stay elevated for the rest of the year (Xeneta via AJOT, 2 Oct 2026). These are trade-press summaries; check rates before using them for anything quantitative.
  • Tariffs. The US Supreme Court ruled against the IEEPA tariffs in February 2026; a 10% Section 122 surcharge ran until 24 July 2026 and was replaced the same day by Section 301 tariffs of roughly 10-12.5% by country (USMCA-qualifying goods exempt), with Section 232 tariffs still in force and a Section 301 "excess capacity" action against 16 economies still pending in early October 2026; refund claims and appeals are ongoing (law-firm and trade-press summaries, July-October 2026; verify with USTR/CBP). Practical effect: more work for customs, trade compliance, sourcing and planning teams.
  • Nearshoring. Surveys point to firms moving some sourcing from China to Mexico and Southeast Asia (Fictiv 2024: 54% of North American manufacturers had adopted nearshoring strategies, via a secondary write-up), but mid-2026 reporting says Mexico's advantage is being eroded by US policy on USMCA and component tariffs. Treat the trend as real but unstable.
  • Automation and AI. Amazon passed one million warehouse robots in June 2025 and in 2026 cut about 30,000 corporate jobs (reported), mostly not warehouse staff. Gartner reports that supply chain job postings requiring AI skills rose 387% between Q1 2023 and Q1 2026 (35 million postings analysed, via Supply Chain 24/7). Kuehne+Nagel is cutting more than 2,000 roles, mainly attributed to technology-enabled efficiency, and DSV says it has removed over 8,000 white-collar positions since integrating Schenker (trade press, 2026). A job-risk website cites Gartner (February 2026) as saying 55% of supply chain leaders expect agentic AI to reduce entry-level hiring (could not confirm at source: unverified). Net: demand for planners and analysts is not collapsing, but routine coordination and data-entry work is the first to be automated, which makes entry-level back-office roles less safe than before.

2.1 Procurement and purchasing

  • Definition. Buying what an organisation needs. Direct procurement buys what goes into the product (steel, chemicals, components, packaging); indirect procurement buys everything else (IT, marketing, facilities, travel, professional services). Sourcing finds and qualifies suppliers and runs tenders; category management owns a whole spend category (for example plastics resins) with a strategy; supplier management handles performance, risk and relationships.
  • In practice. You compare quotes, negotiate price and terms, run a request for proposal, check supplier financial and sustainability risk, and chase late deliveries. In 2025-26 it increasingly means re-sourcing around tariffs and switching suppliers at short notice.
  • Employers. Manufacturers (automotive, FMCG, pharma, electronics, aerospace and defence), retailers, energy and utilities, the public sector (NHS, defence, ministries), and procurement outsourcers. In the US, BLS counts 516,100 buyers and purchasing agents (2025), 22% in manufacturing, 14% wholesale, 13% government (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, May 2025 data).

2.2 Supply chain planning

  • Definition. Demand planning forecasts what customers will buy; supply planning works out what to produce or buy to meet that; S&OP (sales and operations planning) or IBP (integrated business planning) is the monthly cycle where sales, finance, operations and planning agree one plan; inventory management sets safety stock (buffer inventory held against uncertainty) and reorder rules.
  • In practice. You own a forecast in a system, explain forecast errors, balance factory capacity against sales promotions, and decide trade-offs such as air-freighting a shortfall. It is spreadsheet and system heavy, meeting heavy, and the first function blamed when stock is wrong.
  • Employers. FMCG and consumer goods (P&G, Unilever), pharma, retail and e-commerce, electronics, automotive, and planning-focused consultancies. P&G's European planning centre in Warsaw is described by P&G as the region's largest planning hub (P&G careers, 2025-26).

2.3 Logistics and transportation (freight forwarding, 3PL, customs)

  • Definition. Freight forwarders arrange cargo moves for shippers without necessarily owning ships, planes or trucks (they buy space from carriers and add documents, customs and consolidation). Third-party logistics (3PL) firms run warehousing and transport on contract for a client. Customs and trade compliance covers classification (HS codes), duty, origin rules, sanctions and licences, and Incoterms (standard trade terms that say who pays for and bears risk on each leg, for example FOB or DAP). Last-mile is final delivery to the customer.
  • In practice. A forwarder's day is quotes, bookings, documents and exception handling (a delayed vessel, a missing certificate, a customs hold). Peak periods and disruptions raise workload sharply.
  • Employers. By Armstrong & Associates' 2025 ranking of the top 25 forwarders, Kuehne+Nagel and DSV share first, then DHL, Sinotrans, Nippon Express and CEVA (revenue: DSV about US$37.4bn, DHL Supply Chain & Global Forwarding about US$35.5bn, K+N about US$33.8bn; Sinotrans led ocean volume at 4.93m TEU; via Supply Chain 24/7, one secondary source). Also integrators (UPS, FedEx, DHL Express), 3PLs such as GXO, DB Schenker (now part of DSV), Geodis and CEVA, plus many regional firms. In Italy: Savino Del Bene, Fercam, Arcese, BRT and DHL Italy appeared at the top of the older Top 1000 ranking (2018 data, published 2020; dated, treat as a list of names not a current ranking). The Italian contract-logistics market was forecast at about EUR 112.4bn in 2025 (+1.9%) with roughly 79,000 firms (Politecnico di Milano Contract Logistics Observatory via Shipping Italy, Nov 2025).

2.4 Warehousing and distribution operations

  • Definition. Receiving, storing, picking, packing and dispatching goods in warehouses, fulfilment centres and cross-docks, usually managed through a WMS (warehouse management system).
  • In practice. A graduate "area manager" or shift supervisor runs 20-100 operatives, tracks productivity and safety, and fixes flow problems, usually on a shift pattern. It is physical, metric-driven and hands-on.
  • Employers. Retail and e-commerce (Amazon, Zalando, Tesco, Walmart), 3PLs (DHL Supply Chain, GXO, CEVA, Kuehne+Nagel contract logistics), grocery wholesalers, and manufacturers' distribution arms. Amazon's own listings describe the entry-level Area Manager as on-site, with 10-12 hour shifts and weekends/nights common (Amazon postings, 2025-26).

2.5 Manufacturing and production operations / lean

  • Definition. Running a plant: production scheduling, shift management, quality, maintenance coordination, continuous improvement. Lean is a method for removing waste from processes; Six Sigma uses statistics to cut defects; the two are often combined as Lean Six Sigma (belt levels: yellow, green, black).
  • In practice. Early career means supervising a line, owning daily output and safety targets, and running small improvement projects.
  • Employers. FMCG, automotive, pharma, food, chemicals, aerospace and defence. For Italy this is a core part of the economy (northern manufacturing districts), though the 2025-26 industrial picture is mixed (see the separate industrial-automotive-defence research note for the automotive side).

2.6 Shipping and maritime

  • Definition. Container lines (liner shipping) run scheduled services; tramp/bulk and tanker shipping is chartered on demand. Ship brokers intermediate between shipowners and charterers (companies hiring a ship); chartering is hiring a ship, on a voyage or time basis; port operators run terminals. Laytime is the time allowed to load or discharge, and demurrage is the penalty charged when it is exceeded.
  • In practice. Liner companies employ customer service, pricing, network planning, equipment (container repositioning) and port-operations staff; brokerages employ small desks of brokers who live on phone, chat and market information.
  • Employers. Alphaliner's mid-2026 data (secondary reports) put MSC first with about 7.33m TEU (21.6% of global capacity), Maersk about 4.65m and CMA CGM about 4.29m, followed by COSCO, Hapag-Lloyd, ONE, Evergreen, HMM, Yang Ming and ZIM. Ports: Rotterdam 14.2m TEU in 2025, Antwerp-Bruges 13.6m, Hamburg 8.3m (US-bound trade down 25.6% on US customs measures); Singapore's PSA terminals 44.5m TEU (record, +8%); Jebel Ali about 15.55m TEU (flat, +0.1%), with DP World noting reduced inbound vessel traffic due to the Iran conflict (port authorities and trade press, 2025-26). Italy: Italian ports handled about 12.8m TEU in 2025 (+7.1%), with Gioia Tauro above 4.5m TEU (+14%, MSC transhipment hub); Trieste was Italy's largest port by tonnage at nearly 60m tonnes; Genoa is the main gateway for northern Italian cargo (Ports Europe / Shipping Italy, 2026). Ship brokers: Clarksons, Braemar, SSY (Simpson Spence Young), Howe Robinson, and many Nordic and Greek brokers.

2.7 Supply chain technology and analytics

  • Definition. Implementing and running the systems: ERP (SAP, Oracle), advanced planning systems/APS (SAP IBP, Kinaxis, o9, Blue Yonder), transport and warehouse systems (TMS, WMS), control towers and analytics.
  • In practice. You configure planning parameters, clean master data, build dashboards (SQL, Power BI/Tableau, Python), and train users. Vendors are all pushing "agentic AI": Kinaxis announced Maestro Agent Studio, SAP has added AI agents for planning (vendor claims, 2026).
  • Employers. Software vendors, system integrators (Accenture, Capgemini, Deloitte and boutique SAP/Kinaxis/o9 partners), large manufacturers and retailers' IT/digital supply chain teams.

2.8 Supply chain consulting (brief)

  • Operations and supply chain practices exist at Kearney, McKinsey Operations, BCG, Bain, A&M, AlixPartners, the Big 4 and Accenture, plus boutiques. See the management consulting report for entry routes and pay. Typical projects: network design, inventory reduction, procurement savings, S&OP transformation, tariff-response sourcing studies, ERP/planning system implementations.

Regional notes.

  • US: Largest wage numbers (ASCM median US$103k all levels, 2025), strong role definition, ASCM certifications are valued; federal government and defence pay well for logisticians; tariff policy is a daily topic.
  • UK: Lower entry pay (GBP 25-37k), CIPS and CILT chartership matter; graduate schemes often exclude visa sponsorship (Maersk, DHL, K+N, Unilever, Hagland); the City of London is the centre of ship broking.
  • Italy: Strong manufacturing and port base (about 12.8m TEU in 2025), but entry pay is low (roughly EUR 22-35k gross, weak data); many jobs sit under national collective agreements (CCNL; unverified detail), a 13th month is common, most graduate jobs are in the north (Milan, Verona, Genoa, Trieste), and Italian is expected in local roles.
  • Northern European ports (Rotterdam, Antwerp-Bruges, Hamburg): Large forwarder, terminal and carrier staffs, English plus Dutch/German useful; Hamburg's US trade fell 25.6% in 2025; Rotterdam handled 14.2m TEU; Unilever's Dutch track requires a Dutch WO master's.
  • Singapore and Dubai: English-language hubs for commodity shipping, brokerage and regional forwarding; Singapore total-pay estimates are higher (Supply chain analyst about S$65k; manager about S$160k, Morgan McKinley 2026) but the labour market is competitive; Jebel Ali is exposed to Hormuz risk, with volumes flat in 2025 and sharply higher surcharges in 2026; Dubai forwarders get overtime complaints in reviews (weak signal).
  • Hong Kong, Shanghai/Shenzhen, Tokyo: major sourcing hubs where Chinese or Japanese language matters; no reliable pay data found.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
