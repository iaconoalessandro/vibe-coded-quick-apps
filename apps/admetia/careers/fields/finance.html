1. What this branch is

Finance is the work of moving, pricing, investing and protecting money for companies, governments, institutions and individuals. It splits into four worlds that feel very different from the inside: the sell side (investment banks that advise on deals, raise capital and make markets, paid through fees and trading spreads), the buy side (asset managers, hedge funds, private equity, venture capital and private credit funds that invest money and are paid management and performance fees), corporate and risk finance (finance teams inside companies, risk and compliance at financial institutions, paid a salary to plan, control and protect), and quant and fintech (mathematicians, programmers and technology firms that model markets or rebuild financial services as software). Pay at the top of finance is among the highest any graduate can reach, but it is concentrated in a few very selective, long-hours seats; most finance jobs (FP&A, risk, compliance, wealth management, corporate banking) are normal-hours, well-paid professional careers with far easier entry. Region matters more than in most branches: New York and London pay and recruit very differently from Milan, Hong Kong, Singapore, Tokyo or Dubai, and the reports below say where.

Scope notes: this branch was researched in four parts (investment banking and advisory; sales & trading, markets and research; the buy side; corporate finance, risk, quant and fintech). Accounting, management consulting and economics careers have their own branch reports.

Part 1: Investment banking and advisory

Investment banking and advisory is the part of finance where professionals help companies, governments and investors do large, infrequent financial transactions: selling a business, buying one, going public on a stock exchange, borrowing through bonds or loans, or fixing a balance sheet that has run into trouble. Banks and advisory firms are paid mainly through fees tied to those deals, not by trading or by managing money. This is the "sell side" of the industry (it serves issuers and corporate clients; the "buy side" means investors such as private equity funds and asset managers, which are covered in another part of this finance branch). The work is among the best paid and the most demanding in early careers, and it is also one of the most selective: junior pay is high, but hours are long and the entry funnel is narrow, so it pays to understand the whole map, including the adjacent and lower-intensity doors (corporate banking, Big 4 deal advisory) before committing.

P1-2.1 What investment banking is, and where it sits

An investment bank (or the investment banking division, IBD, of a larger bank) is an adviser and fundraising intermediary for large organisations. Three broad things happen in a bank that people loosely call "investment banking", and it matters which one you mean:

  • IBD / "banking" (the "private side"): bankers who advise clients on mergers and acquisitions (M&A), restructurings, and on raising equity and debt. They work with confidential information, which is why this is the "private side" of the bank. This report is about this side. IBD is organised into coverage groups (relationships by industry or region) and product groups (execution expertise: M&A, equity capital markets, debt capital markets, leveraged finance, restructuring).
  • Markets / "sales & trading" and research (the "public side"): traders and salespeople who buy and sell securities for clients, and analysts who publish research. Revenue comes from spreads, commissions and financing. A deal launched by IBD (a share offering, a bond) is sold to investors through the markets side (the "syndicate" desk is the bridge). Covered in another part of this branch.
  • Corporate and transaction banking: the lending, cash management and trade finance relationships a bank has with companies (section 2.7). It is bank-balance-sheet business, not deal advisory, but it sits next to IBD and is a common alternative entry point.

Sell side vs buy side. The sell side (banks, advisers) creates, advises on and distributes financial products and transactions and earns fees or spreads. The buy side (private equity, hedge funds, asset managers, pension funds, insurers) invests capital. In deals, bankers frequently sit across the table from private equity buyers. IB analysts commonly exit to the buy side after two to three years (sections 3 and 4).

P1-2.2 M&A advisory: sell-side, buy-side, fairness opinions

  • Definition. M&A bankers advise on buying, selling, merging or splitting companies and business units.
  • Sell-side mandate. The client wants to sell. The bank prepares the business for sale (a financial model, an information memorandum), approaches potential buyers, runs a competitive process, and helps negotiate price and terms. The sell-side process is the "bread and butter" of boutique and mid-market banks.
  • Buy-side mandate. The client wants to buy. The bank helps find targets, values them, structures the offer (cash, shares, debt), and helps negotiate. Often runs alongside a financing from the same bank's DCM/LevFin teams.
  • Defence and activism work. Advising a company that is being approached by an unwanted bidder or an activist shareholder (common at the elite boutiques).
  • Fairness opinion. A formal letter in which a bank states that a deal price is fair, "from a financial point of view", to the shareholders or the board. It exists because directors want independent support for a decision, and it is often a separate fee line (a bank can be paid for it even if it is not the lead adviser). The analyst work behind it is valuation: discounted cash flow (DCF), comparable companies ("comps"), precedent transactions.
  • Who the clients are. Large corporates, private equity firms (financial sponsors) buying and selling portfolio companies, families and founders selling businesses, governments (privatisations), and sovereign/state-backed investors.
  • In Italy. The M&A market is concentrated in a handful of very large deals each year. In the first nine months of 2026 Italian-involved announced M&A was US$168.7 billion, up 122% on a year earlier, across 1,571 deals, up 9% (LSEG via Teleborsa, 6 October 2026). By value, the top ten advisers were Bank of America, Vitale&Co-IMAP, UBS, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, BNP Paribas, Goldman Sachs, Deutsche Bank, Lazard and Stifel/KBW; by number of deals among them, Lazard led with 26, followed by JPMorgan with 21 and Vitale&Co-IMAP with 19. Each deal's full value is credited to every adviser, so shares sum to well above 100%; the near-identical shares of the leaders imply that a few very large deals are shared by the same advisers (my inference). Vitale&Co-IMAP is the only Italian independent boutique in the top ten.

P1-2.3 Capital markets and product groups

ECM (equity capital markets) helps companies raise money by selling shares: IPOs (first listing), follow-on offerings (a listed company sells more shares, often an overnight "accelerated bookbuild"), rights issues, and convertible bonds (bonds that can turn into shares, a hybrid of debt and equity). ECM bankers sit between the issuer (via the coverage banker) and investors (via the sales and trading floor's syndicate and sales desks). They advise on timing, valuation, size, investor targeting and structure, and lead the legal/marketing process. Fee: gross spread on proceeds. Clients: listed companies, companies preparing to list, private equity sponsors selling shares post-IPO, governments privatising assets. 2026 context: record US dollar volume but concentrated in a few huge deals; Dealogic reports 1H26 global ECM issuance of US$729.4 billion (+73% y/y), global IPO volume US$207 billion, and record first-half convertible volumes (ION Analytics/Dealogic, 1 July 2026).

DCM (debt capital markets) advises borrowers with strong credit ratings ("investment grade", rated BBB-/Baa3 and above) on issuing bonds, and on loans and other debt products. Bankers advise on timing, maturity, currency, size, price ("spread" over a government benchmark), covenants and documentation, then work with the syndicate desk, which runs the live order book from investors and recommends pricing and allocations. Clients: large corporates, banks and insurers, governments and agencies, supranationals. Many issuers come to market repeatedly, so DCM is relationship- and execution-driven, with faster deal cycles than M&A.

Leveraged finance (LevFin) serves sub-investment-grade borrowers (rated BB+ or below, or unrated), most importantly companies being bought by private equity funds in leveraged buyouts (LBOs), where a large part of the price is financed with debt. The bank underwrites or arranges leveraged loans (bank/private-market loans) and high-yield bonds (junk bonds). It earns arrangement/underwriting fees and syndication spreads, and it takes balance-sheet risk if it commits to fund debt before selling it down to investors (CLOs, credit funds, insurers). More credit analysis than investment-grade DCM, and strong ties with the private equity sponsor community, which makes it a classic pipeline to private credit funds.

Other product teams (varies by bank): sponsor coverage (relationships with private equity firms, found at most banks), financing solutions/ structured finance, liability management (helping companies tender for or restructure existing bonds), private placements and derivatives/risk solutions.

P1-2.4 Coverage groups vs product groups, and how they work together

  • Coverage (or "industry/sector") groups own the client relationship. Typical groups: technology/media/telecom (TMT), healthcare, financial institutions (FIG), industrials, consumer/retail, energy/power and infrastructure, real estate, and sponsors. Regional or country coverage (for example "Italy coverage", "Gulf coverage") exists where relationships depend on geography, language and local regulation. The coverage banker knows the client's strategy and generates ideas.
  • Product groups (M&A, ECM, DCM, LevFin, restructuring) own the technical expertise in executing a particular transaction type and the relationships with the investor side.
  • On a deal. A typical flow: a coverage MD builds the relationship and pitches an idea; a product team joins (for an acquisition financed with debt: M&A plus DCM or LevFin; for an IPO: ECM plus the industry team and the equity syndicate). The coverage team handles the client; the product team handles execution and market knowledge. At elite boutiques and many smaller banks, M&A and industry coverage are merged: the same team covers the client and executes the sale, so juniors see more of the deal.
  • Why this matters for a career. At a bulge bracket (BB), analysts are often placed in either an industry group (more varied, builds financial models and pitch decks for many clients) or a product group (more technical). At boutiques there are fewer silos.

P1-2.5 Restructuring advisory

Restructuring bankers advise companies in financial distress, or their lenders and shareholders. Work includes debtor-side mandates (advising the troubled company on liquidity, negotiations with creditors, and formal court processes such as US Chapter 11 or UK restructuring plans), creditor-side mandates (advising banks, bondholder groups or credit funds on recovery and terms), and liability management exercises (LMEs) (negotiated transactions to extend or swap existing debt without a bankruptcy, such as amend-and-extend, "uptier" exchanges and drop-downs). Prospect Rock describes debtor-side mandates as higher-fee and creditor-side as less lucrative (2023). Restructuring is counter-cyclical: demand rises when credit stress rises, and boutiques with large restructuring practices (Houlihan Lokey, PJT Partners, Lazard, Rothschild & Co, Evercore, Perella Weinberg, Moelis, Ducera and others) have been consistently busy. In 2026: PJT's CEO described restructuring demand as elevated and expected it to stay so (earnings call, Q2 2026, snippet-level); Houlihan Lokey called fiscal 2026 "one of the strongest years on record" for restructuring but had a softer fiscal fourth quarter; Debtwire counted 136 European restructuring mandates in H1 2026 involving EUR 50.3 billion of debt (14 August 2026), with PJT leading by mandates (8), then Houlihan Lokey (7) and Rothschild (6). Mandate counts were slightly below the 147 of H1 2025, but the work is concentrated in fewer, bigger situations.

P1-2.7 Corporate and transaction banking (adjacent)

  • Corporate banking (called "commercial banking", "global corporate banking" or "relationship banking" depending on the bank) means lending and relationship management for medium and large companies. Relationship managers (RMs) own the client; they bring in loans, revolving credit lines, and cross-sell products from the investment bank (bond issues, M&A advice, derivatives for hedging).
  • Transaction banking (also "global transaction banking", "treasury and trade solutions", "cash management") is the plumbing: payments, cash pooling, liquidity management, trade finance (letters of credit, guarantees) and custody. Citi calls it Treasury and Trade Solutions (TTS); Deutsche Bank calls it Global Transaction Banking (GTB).
  • Earns: net interest margin on loans and deposits, plus fees for services. Revenue is steadier and the work is less cyclical than M&A.
  • Employers: global banks (HSBC, Barclays, Citi, JPMorgan, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, Santander), national champions (Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit in Italy; ING, ABN AMRO), and regional banks.

P1-2.8 Big 4 and boutique deal advisory (alternative entry)

The Big 4 accounting firms (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC) have "deals" or "deal advisory" teams that advise buyers, sellers and lenders. The main teams:

  • Transaction services (TS) / financial due diligence (FDD): before a deal closes, the buyer (often private equity) hires TS to test the target's numbers: "quality of earnings" (adjusting reported profit to underlying earnings), net debt and "debt-like" items, working capital, forecast risk. Output: a due diligence report and a call with the buyer. Also sell-side "vendor due diligence".
  • Valuation / modelling / purchase price allocation: valuations for accounting rules, tax, disputes and deals.
  • Corporate finance (CF) advisory: smaller M&A advisory work for mid-market clients, run similarly to a mid-market bank but with a Big 4 brand and fee structure. In Italy and much of Europe, Big 4 CF teams run real sell-side processes.
  • Restructuring / turnaround: operational and financial advice to troubled businesses (parallel to restructuring bankers).
  • Independent boutiques: firms such as Alvarez & Marsal and FTI Consulting (restructuring and transaction advisory) appear in Debtwire's European restructuring adviser rankings (Q2 2026).

Fees are typically time-based (hourly), unlike banks' success fees; one source (Mergers & Inquisitions, figures as of 2022) quotes US$200k-800k per engagement. This is an important structural difference: pay is lower but steadier and hours shorter than in banking. A well-trodden path from TS into banking and the buy side exists but is narrower than bank-to-buy-side (section 3.6).

Part 2: Sales & trading, markets and research

This part of the finance branch covers the people who buy and sell financial products all day: the "markets" side of an investment bank (sales, trading, structuring and sell-side research), the physical commodity trading houses (Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore and similar), and the discretionary traders at hedge funds who are often recruited from bank desks. Unlike investment banking advisory (covered in part 1), these jobs are paid mainly for managing risk and serving investors in live markets, not for advising companies on deals. The work is fast, numerate and tied to the market clock, and the pay swings with trading revenues and volatility. Banks' markets divisions had a very strong 2025: the 12-bank Coalition Greenwich index earned US$174.7bn in markets revenue in 2025, up 14.5% (equities US$71.5bn, up 20.7%; fixed income, currencies and commodities, "FICC", US$103.1bn, up 10.5%) (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026). But the same year, headcount did not grow with revenue (see section 2.7).

P2-2.1 What "Global Markets" is, and sell side vs buy side

A bank's markets division (called Global Markets, Markets, Global Banking & Markets or "Sales & Trading", depending on the bank) is the part of an investment bank that buys and sells securities and derivatives for clients and manages the resulting risk. Its clients are the "buy side": asset managers, pension funds, insurers, hedge funds, sovereign wealth funds, central banks, corporate treasurers and wealthy families. The bank is the "sell side" because it sells products and services to those investors. Sell-side employees work for the intermediary; buy-side employees work for the people whose money is invested.

How a markets desk earns money (in plain terms):

  • Bid-ask spread and market making. The desk quotes two prices, one at which it will buy ("bid") and a slightly higher one at which it will sell ("offer"). If it buys from one client and sells to another, it keeps the difference. Per trade the margin is tiny; the business works on volume and good risk management.
  • Commissions. In cash equities (ordinary shares) the bank often charges a small fee per share or a percentage of the trade rather than a spread.
  • Financing. The bank lends cash or securities to hedge funds and earns an interest margin (repo, securities lending, prime brokerage, see 2.6).
  • Structuring margin. For a tailor-made derivative or note, the bank builds a profit margin into the price, plus the later hedging and funding carry.
  • Carry and positioning. The desk holds inventory (bonds, shares, derivatives) to serve clients and earns the yield on it, or loses when prices move.

Market making vs proprietary risk-taking, and post-2008 rules. A market maker holds positions in order to be ready to trade with clients. Proprietary ("prop") trading means taking positions purely to profit from price moves, with the bank's own capital and no client reason. After the 2008 crisis, the US Volcker rule (part of the Dodd-Frank Act) generally prohibits US banking entities from proprietary trading and from investing in or sponsoring hedge funds and private equity funds, while permitting market making, hedging and certain other activities (US Federal Reserve, Volcker Rule page, last updated 30 January 2020; agencies finalised simplifications in October 2019). The practical consequence is that most "prop desks" left banks around 2010-2014: their traders moved to hedge funds or to proprietary trading firms. Alongside it, tougher capital rules (Basel III and its trading-book revisions) made it costlier for banks to hold bond inventory, which is one reason electronic market makers outside banks became large (see 2.7). In the UK and EU the equivalent constraints are capital rules, ring-fencing in UK retail banks, and MiFID II conduct rules rather than a Volcker-style ban (general industry knowledge). Today's bank trader is therefore normally a flow trader: you price for clients and manage the resulting risk, not a bank-funded macro speculator.

Regulatory note on pay (UK). The UK removed the EU-derived bonus cap (variable pay limited to 100% of fixed pay, or 200% with approval) with effect from 31 October 2023 (FCA PS23/15). The cap still applies in the EU (relevant for Milan, Paris, Frankfurt). In practice, EU banks can pay higher base salaries to compensate, so Continental base salaries are often relatively higher and bonuses relatively lower than in New York or London (practitioner consensus).

P2-2.2 Rates

Definition. Rates desks trade government bonds (US Treasuries, German Bunds, Italian BTPs, UK gilts, Japanese JGBs), interest rate swaps (agreements to exchange fixed for floating interest payments), futures and rates options. Sub-areas: government bond trading, swaps (vanilla), "exotics"/rates options, inflation-linked, repo (secured short-term lending, often sits with a funding/financing desk), and emerging-market local rates.

In practice. Clients such as pension funds, insurers, asset managers, banks and central banks use rates desks to hedge interest-rate risk and to express views on central-bank moves. The BIS Triennial Survey (April 2025) found over-the-counter interest rate derivatives trading of about US$7.9 trillion per day, up 59% from three years earlier (BIS, 30 September 2025). Coalition Greenwich reported double-digit revenue growth in G10 rates in 2025 (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026).

Who: the big global banks plus strong European players (Barclays, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, Société Générale, UBS, and in Italy Intesa Sanpaolo and UniCredit, which are major Italian government bond (BTP) market makers; Italian bank postings such as Intesa's "Rates Solutions Analyst" graduate role appeared in Milan in 2026, unverified dates). Rates is the most electronic of the big FICC businesses for vanilla products.

P2-2.3 Credit (investment grade, high yield, distressed, securitised/structured)

Definition. Credit desks trade corporate (and bank and sovereign) bonds and credit derivatives (credit default swaps, CDS, which work like insurance against default) and loans.

  • Investment grade (IG): bonds of companies rated BBB-/Baa3 or higher; high volumes, thin margins, increasingly electronic.
  • High yield (HY): lower-rated issuers; wider spreads, more analysis-heavy, bigger per-trade risk.
  • Distressed: bonds and loans of companies in or near default; trades are few, large and legal/analysis-heavy, and the work overlaps with restructuring and hedge funds.
  • Securitised/structured credit: mortgage-backed and asset-backed securities (pools of loans turned into bonds) and CLOs (pools of corporate loans). Coalition reported double-digit growth in securitised products in 2025, while plain credit was "stable" (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026).

In practice. Credit bonds trade "over the counter" (OTC, bilaterally, not on an exchange) in many thousands of different issues, so liquidity is patchy and traders hold inventory. Clients: asset managers, insurers, hedge funds, pension funds. Credit has seen the largest shift from phone to electronic trading in the past decade, especially in IG.

P2-2.4 FX (foreign exchange: spot, forwards, options, e-FX)

Definition. FX desks trade currencies: spot (immediate exchange), forwards and swaps (exchange at a future date), and options. "e-FX" means automated pricing and execution through electronic platforms.

In practice. The BIS 2025 Triennial Survey found OTC FX turnover of US$9.6 trillion per day in April 2025, up 28% from 2022; the US dollar was on one side of 89% of trades; spot was 31% of turnover (BIS, 30 September 2025; final results published June 2026, so figures could be revised). Clients are corporates hedging foreign revenues, asset managers, hedge funds, central banks, and other banks. A big share is flow: tiny spreads on huge volume, so FX is the most automated of the human-run desks. Coalition reported double-digit growth in G10 FX in 2025.

Where: London is the traditional global hub for FX and rates (Asian hubs and New York follow the sun; the "FX day" runs Sydney-Tokyo-Singapore/Hong Kong-London-New York).

P2-2.5 Commodities (bank desks)

Definition. Bank commodities desks trade energy (oil, gas, power), metals (precious and base) and agricultural products, mainly via derivatives (futures, swaps, options), and sometimes physical. Clients: producers, airlines, utilities, miners, funds. Coalition reported a slight decline in commodities revenue at its 12 banks in 2025 (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026). Many banks have shrunk commodity desks since 2010 because of capital rules and low returns; the large physical positions have gone to the independent trading houses (2.10). Bank desks that remain tend to be hedging and structuring businesses for corporate clients, plus precious metals.

P2-2.6 Equities: cash equities, equity derivatives, delta one, prime brokerage and financing

  • Cash equities: buying and selling shares for institutions. It has two halves. High-touch means a sales trader talks to a client and works a large order carefully; low-touch means the client sends orders through algorithms and electronic access with little human involvement. Revenue is commissions. Coalition reported cash equities revenue up 17.4% to US$12.2bn in 2025.
  • Equity derivatives: options, structured notes, volatility trading, autocallables and similar products on shares and indices. Coalition: US$24.7bn, up 21.4% in 2025. This is among the most quantitative desks, with a structuring function (3.3).
  • Delta one: products whose value moves one-for-one with an underlying asset: ETFs, index and total-return swaps, futures, and baskets. Margins are thin, volumes high, and the work is a mix of trading, financing and technology.
  • Prime brokerage and financing: services to hedge funds: lending cash and shares (securities lending, margin), clearing, custody and reporting. It is a "balance-sheet" business that earns financing spread. Coalition: prime services and futures US$34.5bn, up 21.5% in 2025.

In 2025 equities revenue grew faster than FICC at the 12 big banks; Johnson Associates' May 2026 projections expected equity sales and trading incentives to rise 10-15% for 2026 and fixed income 5-10% (Johnson Associates, 7 May 2026).

P2-2.7 Electronic and algorithmic trading, and what it did to headcount

Electronic trading means automated pricing, execution and risk management. Banks run "e-trading" or "digital markets" businesses (J.P. Morgan's markets programme lists "Digital Markets" next to Sales, Trading and Structuring & Origination, per job-board listings, unverified). Evidence on headcount:

  • Banks' front-office staff overall: about 51,000 at the top investment banks at the end of H1 2025, per Crisil Coalition Greenwich figures cited in a news listing (headline only; the underlying article was not opened).
  • Goldman Sachs International (London) reported FICC sales & trading headcount down 6% (694 to 655) in 2025 even though FICC revenue rose 13% to US$3.6bn; equities sales & trading headcount rose 1.1%; average pay per head at GSI was US$963k (up 21%) against US$399k for Goldman globally (eFinancialCareers citing GSI's annual report, 16 March 2026). This is one London entity and the pay average is skewed by seniors.
  • Dealer-to-client trading's share of total volumes fell from 83.5% (2017) to 75.2% (2025 year to date), according to Crisil Coalition Greenwich (via a news summary; asset classes not stated), consistent with more volume going through all-to-all platforms and non-bank market makers.
  • Electronic equities desks in 2024 expected to add staff in algorithmic sales and coverage (about one-third of respondents) with one participant expecting long-term cuts as AI replaces traders (The TRADE reporting Coalition Greenwich, 16 July 2024).
  • Johnson Associates (May 2026) says firms are reducing analyst-to-manager ratios, outsourcing or offshoring some entry-level roles (e.g., to India) and treating AI fluency as a hiring prerequisite, and describes a "shrinking junior talent pipeline" (Johnson Associates, 7 May 2026).

The net effect (inference from the sources above): fewer voice traders per dollar of revenue, more technologists and quants on the desk, and a premium for people who can both understand markets and code. Human sales and trading roles remain in complex, large or illiquid products.

P2-2.8 Sales vs trading vs structuring

  • Sales covers clients: you call the portfolio manager at a fund, tell them what moved in the market, pitch ideas and get orders to the desk. You are paid on client revenue ("sales credits").
  • Trading prices and holds risk. A trader quotes prices when a client asks, decides how much inventory to take, hedges and is responsible for the desk's profit and loss (P&L).
  • Structuring (also "solutions", "origination" or "strats" in some banks) designs customised products and prices them with quants and traders, e.g., a note that pays a coupon if an index stays above a level.
  • Sales trading (equities, some credit) is a hybrid: it executes client orders and advises on timing and liquidity, closer to the client than a market-making trader, closer to the market than a salesperson.

Typically ~50-60% of desk staff are sales and sales-trading, 25-35% traders and 5-15% structurers and strats (practitioner estimate, unverified; mix varies by desk).

P2-2.9 Sell-side equity research and credit research

Sell-side research analysts cover a sector (e.g., European banks) and publish models, notes and ratings (buy/hold/sell) for institutional investors, and they talk to clients, accompany management meetings and help the sales force. Credit research analysts cover bond issuers, focusing on whether the company can repay (downside) rather than upside. Research is paid for indirectly through trading commissions and, after the MiFID II unbundling rules (EU/UK, January 2018), had to be priced separately; budgets for externally purchased research fell 20-30%, according to the FCA estimate cited by the University of Bath (2024), and research analyst headcount at the 12 largest banks had already fallen 20% between 2012 and mid-2019 (Integrity Research, 2019). In July 2024 the FCA (PS24/9) allowed UK buy-side firms to pay jointly for research and execution again, effective 1 August 2024, and the EU's Listing Act (effective 4 December 2024) allows similar bundling under conditions. Industry commentary (CRISIL, 2024) argues the reversal is probably too late to bring back the old model. Rankings still matter: in the Extel 2025 All-America survey, J.P. Morgan again led the table by team positions (49) and Evercore ISI was ranked No. 1 for analysts on a weighted basis for the fourth year running.

P2-2.10 Physical commodity trading houses and energy majors' trading arms

Definition. Trading houses buy, store, ship, blend and sell physical commodities (crude oil and products, gas/LNG, power, metals, grains and softs) and use futures and swaps to hedge. They earn margin from logistics, location, time and quality arbitrage, financing and optionality, plus trading profit. Big independents: Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore (trading plus mining), Gunvor (rebranded Centalion on 2 October 2026), Mercuria, plus Cargill, ADM, Louis Dreyfus, Bunge and COFCO in agriculture. Energy majors (Shell, bp, TotalEnergies, Equinor, ExxonMobil, Eni) run trading arms; The Economist (2026, via search summary) said the big three trade 40-50m barrels of oil equivalent per day.

Scale (company-reported or press-reported): Vitol earned US$4.2bn in 2025 (down from more than double that) and paid US$5.9bn to employee-owners (about 600) through share buybacks (Bloomberg, in The Star, 27 July 2026). Reuters sources put Vitol's 2025 net profit at about US$4.5bn and revenue at US$343bn (Reuters, via Euronext, 2026; the figures differ, so treat both as approximate). Trafigura reported FY2025 (to 30 September 2025) net profit of US$2.67bn and over 1,400 employee shareholders (Trafigura release, December 2025, via the project's earlier research file) and strong early-2026 results (US$4.1bn for October-March, Kitco/Reuters, 4 June 2026). Gunvor's 2025 net profit fell 85% to US$104m after writedowns; the firm, now Centalion, reports more than 2,000 staff and plans to move its corporate headquarters from Cyprus to Singapore; its trading offices stay in Singapore, Geneva and Houston with hubs in Calgary, London and Stamford (Centalion release, 2 October 2026).

Hubs. Geneva (Vitol, Trafigura, Gunvor, Mercuria all have major Geneva offices), London, Singapore (Asia desks; regional headquarters), Houston (US energy), Dubai (smaller but growing; Shell reportedly moving a Madrid gas trading team to Dubai, reported January 2025), Rotterdam (oil products/shipping), Stamford and Calgary. About the Swiss cluster: commodity trading contributed CHF 19.2bn of value added in 2024 (2.3% of GDP), 58% of it in Geneva (Swiss Federal Statistical Office via blue News, 18 November 2025, via earlier project research).

P2-2.11 Discretionary trading at hedge funds, and how it differs from bank trading

Discretionary traders and portfolio managers (PMs) at macro funds and multi-manager "pod" shops (Millennium, Citadel, Point72, Balyasny, Schonfeld and others) take directional or relative-value positions on rates, FX, credit, equities or commodities with fund capital, based on judgment (as opposed to computer-driven "systematic" strategies). The key differences from a bank trader: (1) no client flow to serve, so no spread income, only price views; (2) pay is a share of your own P&L (typical PM payouts of 10-20% of P&L in the US, 5-20% in London for fund PMs, Selby Jennings 2024); (3) strict risk limits and drawdown stops at multi-manager funds, so a bad few months can end a seat; (4) smaller teams and faster career highs and lows. Detail on hedge fund investing roles is in the buy-side part.

P2-2.12 Briefly: proprietary/electronic market makers and buy-side execution desks

Proprietary trading firms such as Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Optiver, IMC and Susquehanna (SIG) make markets with their own capital and highly automated systems; recruitment is quantitative and covered in the quant part. Buy-side execution (dealing) desks sit inside asset managers (e.g., BlackRock's global trading team executes for all its portfolio managers across asset classes, per BlackRock job postings) and place orders with banks and electronic venues; they are the "buyer" of the sell side's service.

Part 3: The buy side

The "buy side" is the part of finance that manages other people's money and decides what to buy, hold or sell: asset managers, hedge funds, private equity, venture capital, private credit funds, the big institutions that hand money to those managers (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, insurers), and the private banks that look after wealthy families. It is called the buy side because these firms are the clients of investment banks and brokers (the "sell side", covered in parts 1 and 2). Buy-side employers are usually small, pay a lot for a few people and ask for evidence of judgment rather than just hard work. Entry is competitive and often indirect: many of the most-wanted roles (private equity, credit, hedge fund analyst) are filled mainly from people who first spent two to three years at an investment bank, a consultancy or another fund.

Context for 2026: global assets under management reached about US$147 trillion in 2025, up 11%, but BCG estimates that more than 80% of revenue growth came from market gains rather than new client money (BCG, 24th Global Asset Management Report, via Alternatives Watch, 28 April 2026). Hedge fund industry capital hit a record of about US$5.6 trillion in Q2 2026 (HFR, Q2 2026, via Hedgeweek). Private equity (PE) is busy on paper but cash is not coming back to investors: distributions have been under 15% of net asset value for four straight years (Bain, Global Private Equity Report 2026, 23 February 2026). Private credit, the fastest-growing area of the last decade, is under real stress in 2026 (see 2.6).

P3-2.1 Buy side vs sell side, and who the end investors are

The sell side (investment banks, brokers) advises companies, creates and trades securities and earns fees and trading spreads. The buy side takes capital from investors and invests it. The money ultimately comes from:

  • Pension funds (public and corporate): collect contributions from workers and employers and pay retirees decades later. Examples: CalPERS, CPP Investments, Dutch APG and PGGM, Japan's GPIF (about ¥260 trillion in assets, Bloomberg, 11 August 2025).
  • Insurers: invest premiums to meet future claims; they like predictable income, which is one reason insurers have become big buyers of private credit (BCG via Alternatives Watch, 28 April 2026).
  • Sovereign wealth funds (SWFs): state-owned pools funded by commodity income or reserves. Norway's fund (managed by NBIM) is above US$2 trillion; ADIA is estimated at US$1.18 trillion, Mubadala US$358 billion and ADQ US$251 billion (Global SWF 2026 figures, as reported by secondary sources; several funds, including ADIA, GIC and SAFE, do not publish official figures).
  • Endowments and foundations (Yale, Harvard, university and charity funds) and family offices (private investment teams run for one or several wealthy families).
  • Retail investors and wealthy individuals: buy mutual funds, ETFs and, increasingly, private-market products through banks and advisers.

How the buy side earns money differs by business: management fees (a percentage of assets, e.g. 0.05% on an index fund up to roughly 1-2% on active or alternative funds), performance fees (a share of profits, commonly 20% in hedge funds), carried interest or "carry" (the manager's share, typically 20%, of a private fund's profit after investors get their money back plus a minimum return), and interest margin (lending at a spread above the cost of funds, in private credit and banks).

P3-2.2 Asset management (long-only and passive)

Long-only active managers (Capital Group, Fidelity, Wellington, T. Rowe Price, Schroders, Amundi, Eurizon) buy securities they believe will beat a benchmark and cannot short. They earn a management fee on assets, so revenue rises when markets rise and when clients add money, and falls when they leave. Teams specialise by asset class: equities, fixed income, multi-asset.

Passive/index managers (Vanguard, BlackRock iShares, State Street) track an index at a tiny fee. In the US, passive funds and ETFs overtook active funds in total assets around the end of 2023 (about US$13.29 trillion vs US$13.23 trillion, Morningstar data as cited by Walnut Invest); the average index equity mutual fund charged about 0.05% in 2024 against 0.64% for active (same secondary source, to be checked against ICI). In 2025 only 38% of active strategies both survived and beat their passive peers, and over ten years only 21% (Morningstar, 2025 data via Walnut Invest). Why it matters for careers: fee pressure shrinks revenue per employee, so traditional active managers hire fewer analysts, push for cost cuts, and value distribution and product skills more than before. BCG describes distribution and scalable models as the deciding factor for future growth (BCG via Alternatives Watch, 28 April 2026). The industry's global fee yield fell from 0.25% of assets in 2015 to 0.22% in 2023 (BCG, 2024 edition). Italy's managed-savings market is large: a record €2,722 billion at end-June 2026 (Assogestioni via Teleborsa, 18 September 2026).

P3-2.3 Hedge funds, strategy by strategy

A hedge fund takes money from institutions and rich individuals, uses short selling, leverage and derivatives, and charges a management fee plus a performance fee. Typical convention is "2 and 20" historically; large multi-managers now often pass through costs to investors instead (Citadel's bond prospectus reportedly shows about US$17 billion of pass-through expenses, almost 90% employee pay; paid Substack summary, unverified).

  • Long/short equity: buys stocks expected to rise and shorts those expected to fall. Analysts spend time on company models, management meetings and channel checks. HFR's equity hedge index returned +9.6% in the first half of 2026, with technology funds +19.0% (HFR, H1 2026).
  • Global macro: bets on interest rates, currencies, commodities and equity indices based on economic views. HFR's macro index returned +6.1% in H1 2026, systematic CTA (trend-following) strategies +9.7%. Few seats exist; many macro PMs are former bank traders.
  • Event-driven / merger arbitrage: trades situations tied to corporate events (mergers, spin-offs, activism, bankruptcies). Merger arbitrage buys the target at a small discount to the deal price and bets the deal closes. Event-driven returned +7.4% in H1 2026, distressed +12.4% (HFR). Legal reading (merger agreements, antitrust) is a big part of the job.
  • Credit / distressed: invests in corporate bonds and loans, from performing credit to bankrupt companies. Overlaps with private credit and restructuring advisory.
  • Relative value / fixed-income arbitrage: exploits small price gaps between related instruments (for example government bond vs futures) using high leverage. Very quantitative, small number of seats, and prone to sudden losses when markets seize up.
  • Multi-strategy / multi-manager "pod" platforms (Millennium, Citadel, Balyasny, Point72, ExodusPoint, Schonfeld): the firm allocates capital to dozens or hundreds of independent PM teams ("pods"), each with strict risk limits, and charges investors a fee including pass-through costs. Pod PMs are paid a percentage of their own pod's profit, reported at roughly 12-25% (eFinancialCareers, 25 August 2026, citing a former Balyasny analyst's post) or 12-20% of net P&L at Millennium (Quantt estimate, 2026). Stop-loss rules are strict: sources repeat that a drawdown near 5% halves capital and about 7.5% ends the pod, but that comes from LinkedIn posts, not from the firms (unverified). Pods are cut mercilessly: practitioners cite PM median tenures of roughly 2-3 years (unverified, LinkedIn). In March 2026 the model was stress-tested: Balyasny fell 4.3% in the month and was down 3.8% year to date, ExodusPoint fell 4.5% (HedgeCo, April 2026). By Q2 2026 industry capital recovered strongly; investors favour big platforms: firms above US$5 billion took US$38.1 billion of Q2 net inflows against US$6.3 billion for mid-sized and US$0.7 billion for small ones (HFR).
  • Quantitative / systematic funds (Two Sigma, D. E. Shaw, Renaissance, AQR, Man AHL): trade on models. Covered in the quant part of this branch.

P3-2.4 Private equity

PE firms raise "blind pool" funds from institutions, buy whole companies (usually using borrowed money), improve them over about 4-7 years and sell them. Fees: roughly 1-2% management fee on committed capital plus 20% carry over a hurdle, typical industry convention.

  • Buyout: large-cap (mega-funds such as Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, CVC, EQT), mid-market (companies worth a few hundred million to a couple of billion) and lower mid-market (smaller family businesses, many in Italy). Large funds mostly rely on financial engineering and leverage; smaller funds rely more on operational improvement and sourcing.
  • Growth equity: minority investments in fast-growing profitable companies, with less leverage (General Atlantic, TA Associates, Summit).
  • Secondaries: buying existing fund stakes or "continuation vehicles" from other sponsors. Record volume of about US$240 billion in 2025, up 48% (Jefferies Global Secondary Market Review, 2025; other houses report US$220-229 billion).
  • Infrastructure: long-life assets such as airports, grids, data centres; infrastructure fundraising was one of the few bright spots of 2025 (Bain, 2026).
  • Real estate PE (brief): funds buying and developing property; more sensitive to interest rates.

State of the market: global buyout deal value rose 44% to about US$904 billion in 2025 and exit value rose 47% to about US$717 billion, but around 32,000 unsold companies worth about US$3.8 trillion sit in portfolios, average holding periods have stretched to about seven years and fundraising was about US$1.3 trillion, flat on 2024 (Bain, 23 February 2026). Pay is flat because the money is not flowing back: Johnson Associates projected 2025 PE bonuses flat to up 5% at large firms and flat at midsize and small firms (Johnson Associates via AI-CIO, 10 November 2025). In Italy, 2025 PE and VC investment was €11.61 billion, down 22%, fundraising fell 46% to €3.57 billion, and 73% of invested value came from foreign operators (AIFI-PwC via Teleborsa, 23 March 2026).

P3-2.5 Venture capital

VC funds invest in young companies, in stages: seed (first cheques, often €0.5-3 million, on a team and idea), early stage (Series A/B, proving a product and revenue), growth (later rounds before an IPO or sale). Corporate venture capital (CVC) is the venture arm of a large company (Intel Capital, Salesforce Ventures, and in Italy, corporate funds of banks and utilities), investing for strategic as well as financial reasons.

VC returns follow a power law: a very small number of investments produces most of the profit. In Horsley Bridge data shared with Andreessen Horowitz, roughly 6% of investments produced about 60% of returns (secondary summaries); Correlation Ventures' 2004-2013 dataset found 65% of financings returned less than 1x and only 4% returned 10x or more (older data, repeated in blogs; I could not verify the originals). Practically: a VC job is judged on finding rare outliers, you will be wrong most of the time, and the first signals of success take 7-10 years. In 2025 Johnson projected VC bonuses flat (AI-CIO, 10 November 2025). Italy's VC deals rose 30% to 568 in 2025, but at €1.35 billion invested, the market is small (AIFI-PwC, 23 March 2026).

P3-2.6 Private credit

Private credit is lending to companies by funds rather than banks, mainly to mid-sized companies owned by private equity firms. Direct lending (senior loans, the largest part); mezzanine (junior, higher-yield, sometimes with equity kickers); special situations / opportunistic (rescue financing, distressed buys); asset-based finance (loans against receivables, inventory, equipment, consumer loans, aircraft). The manager earns a management fee, often a performance fee, and the loans earn an interest margin.

Why it grew: after 2008, tighter bank capital rules made banks cut risky leveraged lending, and pension funds and insurers, facing low yields, wanted more income (BlackRock's explanation, 2025). Estimates of size vary from about US$1.7 trillion to US$3.5 trillion depending on definitions: the Alternative Credit Council says about US$3.5 trillion at end-2024; Preqin passed US$2 trillion in 2024; BlackRock forecasts US$4.5 trillion by 2030. The second leg of growth since 2022 came from higher interest rates (floating-rate loans paid more) and banks pulling back again.

2026 is the first real stress test: non-traded BDC (business development company, a listed or semi-listed lending vehicle) redemption requests averaged 4.8% of net asset value in Q4 2025 (up from 1.6% in Q3), with one large fund seeing about 14% in early 2026; software loans, about 18% of S&P-rated mid-market CLO assets, were bid at about 87.7 in May 2026, roughly 990 basis points wider than non-software loans (Alvarez & Marsal, 15 July 2026; Alternative Credit Investor, 27 July 2026). Sources disagree on defaults: Mercer cites trailing direct-lending defaults of 1.1% and KBRA forecasts 2.0% for 2026, while another source cites UBS predicting a doubling (Mercer, 16 April 2026; A&M). Hiring: a London headhunter said private credit jobs were down up to 25% in October 2024 (eFinancialCareers, 16 October 2024); I found no 2026 hiring data. A secondary report says Johnson projects 2026 private credit bonuses down 2.5-7.5% (unverified; Johnson's 2025 projection was +5-10%).

P3-2.7 Institutional allocators and consultants

Allocators decide how much goes to which asset class and which manager. In-house teams of pension funds (CPP Investments, Ontario Teachers', CalPERS, APG, GPIF), insurers' investment arms (Allianz, AXA, Generali), SWFs (NBIM, GIC, Temasek, ADIA, Mubadala, PIF), and endowments (Yale, Harvard) both select outside managers and invest directly. Fund-of-funds (e.g., Hamilton Lane, StepStone, Partners Group) pool client money into many private funds. Investment consultants (Mercer, WTW, Cambridge Associates, Aon) advise pension schemes and endowments on managers and strategy, and are a common first job (see 3.7). Gulf SWFs are very active: record commitments of US$53.9 billion across 108 deals in H1 2026 (Global SWF via Khaleej Times). Global state-owned investors, including pensions and central banks, hold US$62.5 trillion (Global SWF, H1 2026).

P3-2.8 Private wealth management and private banking

Private banks and wealth managers (UBS, Julius Baer, Pictet, Lombard Odier, Fideuram-Intesa Sanpaolo Private Banking, Mediobanca Private Banking, UniCredit Private Banking, Morgan Stanley, Merrill, JPMorgan Private Bank) manage portfolios and offer lending, tax-aware and succession advice for wealthy individuals. They earn fees on assets (often 0.5-1.5% a year) plus lending margin, and the relationship manager (RM) brings in the clients. It is the largest buy-side-adjacent employer of client-facing graduates, and the wealth segment is growing: Johnson projected 2025 wealth-management bonuses up 8-10% (AI-CIO, 10 November 2025). In Italy many "consulenti finanziari" in networks (reti) are self-employed agents paid by commission, not employees, which changes both risk and pay.

Part 4: Corporate finance, risk, quant and fintech

This part of the finance branch covers the people who run, protect and mathematically model money, rather than the people who advise on deals or sell products. It has four blocks. First, finance teams inside ordinary companies (budgeting, cash management, in-house M&A). Second, the risk and compliance functions that keep banks, insurers and asset managers inside the law and inside their capital limits. Third, quantitative finance: the mathematicians and programmers who price derivatives, build trading models and run electronic market-making. Fourth, fintech, the technology-led payments, banking and lending firms. These areas are very different in lifestyle (a treasury analyst can have a predictable week, a junior quant trader earns more than almost any other graduate and is under heavy performance pressure), but they share one idea: the work is about measuring and controlling money and risk with data. Compared with investment banking, entry here is easier in corporate finance and compliance, and far harder at the top quant and prop-trading firms.

P4-2.1 Corporate finance inside non-financial companies

Here "finance" means the finance department of a company that makes or sells something else (a carmaker, a retailer, a pharma firm, a software company). The employer is the company itself; the "client" is the business. Four sub-areas:

  • FP&A (financial planning and analysis). The team that sets the budget, produces rolling forecasts, and explains why actual results differ from plan (variance analysis). In practice you rebuild the monthly forecast in Excel or a planning tool such as Anaplan, Adaptive or SAP, then explain a 3% margin miss to a business unit head. Many FP&A people are "business partners": embedded with sales or operations, advising on pricing or headcount. FP&A is protective and decision-supporting; it makes money indirectly by steering resources.
  • Corporate treasury. The team that manages the company's cash, liquidity (can we pay bills next month?), funding (bank loans, bonds, revolving credit lines), foreign-exchange and interest-rate hedging, and relationships with banks and rating agencies. Treasury protects value: a treasurer who hedges a euro revenue stream against a falling euro reduces earnings volatility. The people on the other side are the banks' corporate-banking, FX and rates desks.
  • Corporate development ("corp dev"). The in-house M&A and strategy team: it identifies acquisition targets, values them, runs due diligence with advisers, and integrates deals. It is the buy-side counterpart to the investment bankers in part 1: the company is the client, banks are its advisers.
  • Investor relations (IR), briefly. Communicates with shareholders and analysts of a listed company (earnings calls, guidance, investor roadshows). Small teams, usually staffed from FP&A, banking or sell-side research; not a graduate entry route.

Regional note: in Anglo-American groups "FP&A" is the common label; in Italy, Germany and France the nearest equivalent is controllo di gestione / Controlling / contrôle de gestion, often more tied to accounting close and group reporting. In Italy, finance teams of large Milan-based groups (industrial, luxury, energy, utilities) are the main employers; the project's earlier research treats these as predictable-hours routes (project file careers/accounting-and-corporate.md).

P4-2.2 Risk management at banks, insurers and asset managers

Risk functions measure how much could be lost and make sure it stays within limits and within regulatory capital. Banks organise this in "three lines of defence": the first line (business desks) owns its risks, the second line (independent risk and compliance) challenges and monitors, the third line (internal audit) checks both. Most graduate risk jobs are second line. Sub-areas:

  • Market risk. Measures losses from price moves (rates, FX, equities, credit spreads) on trading books, using Value-at-Risk (VaR), expected shortfall and stress scenarios. Works with the trading desks daily. Employers: investment banks, large asset managers, hedge funds.
  • Credit risk. The chance a borrower or trading counterparty does not repay. Includes credit analysis and underwriting at commercial banks (assessing a company's accounts and cash flows to set a limit or price a loan), portfolio credit risk (models of default probability and loss), and counterparty credit risk for derivatives. Employers: commercial and retail banks, insurers, rating agencies, credit funds.
  • Operational risk. Losses from failed processes, people, systems or external events (fraud, outages, cyber attack, conduct failures). Often the broadest and most generalist risk entry point.
  • Liquidity and balance-sheet risk (ALM, asset-liability management; "treasury risk"). Ensures a bank can fund itself and survive deposit outflows, and manages interest-rate risk on the loan and deposit book.
  • Enterprise risk management (ERM). The group-level view: risk appetite, aggregation across risk types, reporting to the board. Typically senior or mid-career.
  • Model risk / model validation. Independent teams that test the mathematical models used for pricing, capital and credit decisions. A technical route into banking that is partly quantitative.

Regulatory drivers, in plain language. Regulation creates a large share of risk jobs:

  • Basel III is the international framework for bank capital (how much loss-absorbing equity banks must hold). The "Basel III endgame" (US) and "Basel 3.1 / CRR3" (UK, EU) are the final tightening, mostly about how banks calculate risk-weighted assets. In the US a revised endgame proposal was reported released on 19 March 2026 after the 2023 version failed to gain consensus (Bloomberg Professional summary; comments reported due 18 June 2026; I found no final rule). In the UK, the PRA is reported to apply Basel 3.1 from 1 January 2027 (internal-model market-risk part reportedly deferred to 2028; single source, verify with the PRA).
  • FRTB (Fundamental Review of the Trading Book) is the new market-risk capital regime. The EU delayed its application to 1 January 2027 via a delegated act published in 2025 (Regulation Tomorrow / Deloitte, 2025); timelines keep slipping, which is itself a lesson for candidates: implementation teams expand and contract with the regulatory calendar.
  • Stress tests. Regulators run hypothetical recessions on bank balance sheets. In the US the Fed's annual test (the old CCAR plus DFAST, the Dodd-Frank Act Stress Test): the 2026 results (24 June 2026) covered 32 banks, all stayed above minimum capital, and stress capital buffers were frozen until 2027 pending model review (Federal Reserve and secondary press, June 2026). In the EU, the EBA's 2025 test covered 64 banks and showed roughly 370 basis points of CET1 depletion in the adverse scenario (EBA, 1 Aug 2025; one other analysis cites about 304 bp, sources disagree on the measure); the 2027 test is in draft with 63 banks, 55% fewer data points and a first climate module (EBA/Regulation Tomorrow, June 2026).
  • IFRS 9 (and US CECL) are accounting rules requiring banks to book expected credit losses in advance, which created large teams building "expected credit loss" models and provisioning processes.

P4-2.3 Compliance and financial crime

Compliance makes sure the firm obeys financial regulation and does not become a conduit for crime. AML/KYC (anti-money-laundering / know-your-customer) means verifying who a client is, where its money comes from, and monitoring transactions for suspicious patterns, then filing reports with authorities. Sanctions compliance means screening clients and payments against government lists and blocking prohibited business. Regulatory compliance covers conduct rules, market abuse, advertising and licensing. Employers are all regulated firms (banks, asset managers, payment firms, crypto exchanges, insurers, law and Big Four advisory firms). It is one of the largest entry routes into financial services because of sheer headcount, but it is also the area most exposed to automation (see 3.5).

P4-2.4 Quantitative finance

"Quant" means someone who solves financial problems with mathematics, statistics and code. Three very different worlds share the label:

  • Banks (sell side). Strats or desk quants build the pricing and risk libraries used by traders; XVA quants model valuation adjustments to derivatives (credit, funding and capital costs); model validation quants test other people's models. Clients: the bank's own traders, risk and regulators.
  • Quant hedge funds and systematic funds (buy side). Quantitative researchers develop trading signals and portfolio models from data; examples include Renaissance Technologies, Two Sigma, D. E. Shaw, AQR, Man Group/AHL, Citadel (and its multi-manager "pod" peers such as Millennium, Point72, Balyasny) and WorldQuant. They manage outside investors' money and charge fees.
  • Proprietary trading firms and electronic market makers. Firms such as Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Hudson River Trading (HRT), Jump, Optiver, IMC, Susquehanna (SIG), DRW and Flow Traders trade their own capital and provide continuous buy and sell prices on exchanges, earning small margins on enormous volume. Quantitative traders and quantitative researchers design and run the strategies; developers build the low-latency systems. Citadel Securities (market maker) is a separate firm from Citadel (hedge fund).
  • Quant developers write production code for any of the above: pricing libraries at banks, execution and research platforms at funds, ultra-low-latency C++ (or FPGA) at trading firms.

P4-2.5 Fintech

Fintech means technology-first financial businesses. Main segments and how they earn money:

  • Payments (Stripe, Adyen; incumbents Visa, Mastercard; in Italy, Nexi): earn a small percentage plus fixed fee on each transaction (a "take rate"), or interchange shared with card networks. Adyen reported net revenue of EUR 1,302.9m for H1 2026, up 19% year on year, with a 49% EBITDA margin (Adyen H1 2026 results, 13 Aug 2026), a sign that scale payments are profitable.
  • Neobanks / digital banks (Revolut, Monzo, N26, Nubank): earn interchange, subscriptions, foreign exchange and net interest margin (interest charged minus interest paid). Revolut's 2025 annual report (24 Mar 2026): revenue GBP 4.5bn (+46%), profit before tax GBP 1.7bn, 68.3m retail customers.
  • Lending / BNPL (Klarna, Affirm; in Italy Scalapay): earn merchant fees and interest or late fees; credit losses are the main risk.
  • Wealthtech and brokerage: subscriptions, spreads, payment for order flow, assets under management fees.
  • Insurtech: underwriting margin or distribution commissions.
  • B2B infrastructure / banking-as-a-service (BaaS): sells licences, card-issuing and compliance infrastructure to other companies.
  • Crypto and digital assets: exchange trading fees, custody, stablecoin reserve income. Honest risk note: high volatility of revenue, many failures, regulatory shifts. In the EU, the MiCA regulation requires authorisation for crypto-asset service providers; the transitional period ended on 1 July 2026 and unauthorised firms must wind down EU client business (ESMA statement of 23 June 2026, via law-firm commentary); one source counts 244 authorisations by that date. Hong Kong approved its first stablecoin-issuer licences in April 2026 (HSBC and a Standard Chartered-led venture, single-source report).

Funding context: global fintech venture funding was about US$52.7bn in 2025 (CB Insights via press), while KPMG's broader measure of fintech investment recovered from US$95.5bn to US$116bn but deal count fell to an eight-year low of 4,719 (KPMG Pulse of Fintech H2 2025). Capital is concentrating in bigger, later-stage companies, so early-stage startup hiring is thinner than in 2021. Hubs: London (largest in Europe), Milan (Satispay, Scalapay, Nexi's home; a Milan city page claims over 45% of Italian fintechs, promotional), Singapore and Hong Kong (regulated stablecoin and digital-asset regimes), Dubai (DIFC; I found no usable hiring data).

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
