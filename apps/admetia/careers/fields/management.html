Salary data for general management is thin: many employers do not publish graduate pay, recruiter guides sit behind forms, and aggregator sites (Payscale, Indeed, Glassdoor) are weak signals. Where a number rests on an aggregator, a single job advert or a secondary summary, the text says so. "No reliable data found" means exactly that.

1. What this branch is

General management is the work of running parts of an organisation rather than doing one specialist craft: leading a team, a plant, a store, a project, a people function or a business unit, and answering for its results. It is the branch for business graduates who want to work inside companies, public bodies or non-profits (not in a consulting firm, bank or agency) and who like organising people, processes and money more than analysing markets or writing code. Most careers start in a defined function (operations, projects, HR, a rotational "management trainee" scheme) and widen into general management only after 8-15 years; "CEO" is an outcome for very few, whereas "plant manager", "head of HR" or "programme director" are realistic. The branch is large and entry is easier than in consulting or investment banking, but the best-known graduate schemes (FMCG "academies", Fast Stream, EU institutions) are very competitive, and in 2025-26 graduate hiring at large UK employers is softer than a few years ago.

Scope notes: Global view with explicit US, UK, continental Europe (Italy always called out; Milan figures only where found), and Asia/Gulf hubs (Hong Kong, Singapore, Tokyo, Dubai) where evidence exists. Management consulting, marketing and sales, logistics and supply chain, accounting, finance/FP&A, product management and data analytics are other branches and are cross-referenced only. Ratings (stress, people, quant, entry difficulty) use the shared scales; hours and stress figures that are not backed by a statistic (most of them) are labelled as practitioner consensus. Search access was limited this round: Michael Page, Hays, Robert Walters, PMI and CIPD salary tables could not be opened, so UK/Italy pay for HR and project roles leans on the APM survey, BLS, Prospects, recruiter job adverts and aggregators.

2. Map of areas and sectors

2.1 General management and leadership development (management-trainee schemes)

Definition. A "management trainee" or "future leaders" programme is a structured, rotational graduate scheme: you move through two to six assignments (often 6-12 months each) in different functions or sites, with the goal of reaching a first line-manager or specialist-manager role faster than the normal route. "Rotational" simply means you change job every few months.

In practice. The content depends on the employer. At Unilever the UK Future Leaders Programme is described as a 36-month rotational scheme; one technology-stream listing for the 2027 intake shows six rotations of six months (Unilever careers / Bright Network, accessed Oct 2026). Amazon's UK graduate Area/Shift Manager role is a 19-24 month rotation across roughly nine UK sites (JobTeaser listing, 12 July 2026). Aldi's Graduate Area Manager programme is a 12-month programme in which you are given your own store and are expected to be ready for a full Area Manager role by the end of year one (Higherin listing for the 2027 intake). BMW's trainee programme has four assignments over about 16 months (research/careers/industrial-automotive-defence.md). Luxury schemes are small: Kering says "more than 60" young people are in its graduate programmes against 43,731 employees (Kering 2025 filing, per research/careers/luxury-and-fashion.md).

Who employs it. FMCG (consumer goods) groups (Unilever, Nestlé, P&G, L'Oréal, Ferrero), industrial and automotive groups (Siemens, BMW, Rheinmetall), retailers and logistics (Aldi, Lidl, Amazon), energy companies, luxury groups (LVMH's SPRING programmes) and banks (DBS Management Associate Programme, 12 months, 112 hires in 2026, per DBS). The path to general manager or country manager is a 10-20 year ladder through commercial or operational line jobs; schemes do not guarantee it.

2.2 Operations management

Definition. Running the day-to-day work that produces a product or service: plants, warehouses, stores, branches, call centres, field-service teams.

In practice. Shift handovers, output and quality targets, health and safety, staffing rotas, supplier problems and cost per unit. Lean (a method of removing waste from processes) and continuous improvement are the working toolkit; Ferrero, for example, runs a three-year international graduate programme in Lean and Continuous Improvement with a first one-year assignment in Alba, Italy (search summary of Ferrero careers page, Jan 2026; page itself returned 404 when fetched, so details unverified).

Who employs it. Manufacturers, food and pharma plants, logistics and e-commerce (Amazon), retail and grocery, utilities, airlines and airports, hospitals, call-centre and shared-service operators. BLS counts about 252,100 industrial production manager jobs in the US (2025).

2.3 Project and programme management

Definition. A project manager delivers a defined piece of change (a system rollout, a new product line, a factory extension) on time and budget. A PMO (project management office) is the central team that tracks and standardises projects. A programme manager coordinates several linked projects.

In practice. Plans, risk logs, status reports, budget tracking, stakeholder meetings and chasing owners of late tasks. Methods are waterfall (plan first, build later), Agile/Scrum (short iterations) and hybrids.

Who employs it. Almost every large organisation: IT and telecoms, banks, pharma, construction and engineering, energy and utilities, consultancies, government. In the UK the APM 2025 survey found the highest average pay in energy and utilities and in consultancy (£62,500) (APM, 2025).

2.4 Human resources / people management

Definition. Recruiting, developing, paying and supporting employees, and advising managers. Common jobs: HR generalist, HR business partner (HRBP: an HR adviser embedded with a business unit), talent acquisition (recruiting), learning and development (L&D), compensation and benefits (reward), and employee relations.

In practice. A mix of administration, advice and, at times, difficult conversations (redundancies, grievances). In larger firms the work splits into specialist centres (reward, talent) plus HRBPs.

Who employs it. Every organisation above roughly 100 staff. Large employers and consultancies hold the specialist roles; SMEs have one generalist.

2.5 Corporate strategy and business development (in-house)

Definition. Strategy teams inside a company advise executives on where to compete, which markets to enter, and which acquisitions or partnerships to pursue. Business development (BD) and partnerships teams find and negotiate deals with other companies. (Consulting and corporate development in the finance sense are covered in other branches.)

In practice. Market models, planning cycles, board papers and partner negotiations. Teams are small and sit close to the CEO or CFO, so graduate entry is rare; most hires are ex-consultants or ex-bankers with 2-5 years of experience, or internal transfers (consulting-exit guides, unverified as to rate).

Who employs it. Large corporates in tech, pharma, energy, telecoms, banks and industrials.

2.6 Public-sector and non-profit management (brief)

Definition. Management and policy-administration jobs in civil services, EU institutions, international organisations and NGOs.

In practice. Policy drafting, programme delivery, budgets and stakeholders; lower pay than the private sector, more stable hours. Entry is usually by formal competition: the UK Fast Stream, EPSO's AD5 competition (174,727 applicants for 1,490 places in 2026) and Italian concorsi (open competitions with written tests).

Who employs it. National civil services, local authorities, the EU institutions, the UN family, development banks, large NGOs and charities.

2.7 Retail and hospitality management (brief)

Definition. Running stores, hotels, restaurants or rental branches. It is one of the biggest entry routes for graduates: supermarket "area manager" schemes, rental-car "management trainee" schemes, hotel graduate programmes.

In practice. Early starts, weekends, rotas, stock, shrinkage (theft and waste) and complaints; real responsibility for people and money at 22-24.

Who employs it. Grocers (Aldi, Lidl), department stores and fashion chains, rental firms (Enterprise), hotel groups (Hilton, Marriott, De Vere), restaurant groups.

Commentary.

  • Multinationals sell structure: training, rotation, brand. They also impose geographic mobility and relocation requirements. At the entry level pay is close to the market median (High Fliers £35,000; ISE £33,000).
  • Banks use "management associate" schemes (DBS) as a leadership pipeline, but HR and project roles in banks are support functions, paid below front office and shaped by regulation.
  • Tech companies hire operations and project people but often prefer experience; no graduate pay source found, so tech pay claims are unverified.
  • SMEs and family firms are the core of the Italian economy. Of Italian firms with turnover of at least €20 million, 15,568 out of 23,578 (66%) are family-controlled (AUB Observatory, XVII edition, Bocconi/AIDAF/EY, early 2026; one slide says 15,836 firms, so the number differs by document). External managers are increasingly common in succession (earlier editions show about a quarter of successions in €20-50m firms moving to a non-family leader), but families keep strategic control. You get scope fast and sometimes the chance to work with the owner, but promotion to the top is rarely open.
  • Italian pay is governed by national collective contracts (CCNL: contratto collettivo nazionale di lavoro) which set minimum pay by level (livello), plus 13th (often 14th) monthly payments and notice rules. Examples: metalworking-industry level D1 minimum €1,784.94 gross a month from 1 June 2026 and A1 €2,907.01 (DottrinaLavoro); commerce/terziario minimums for "quadri" vary by source (about €2,500 to €2,990 a month). Real pay is above the minimum through "superminimo" (a negotiated top-up). Managers (dirigenti) have a separate contract, with a terziario base pay of €4,660 a month on one platform (unverified).
  • US vs Europe: US managers are typically exempt from overtime and often work longer hours, with at-will employment and higher headline pay. European managers have more statutory protection (notice, working-time rules) but lower pay; the UK is in between. In the US BLS notes many top executives work more than 40 hours, including evenings and weekends.
  • Asia/Gulf: Japanese big companies hire "generalist" (sogoshoku) graduates once a year (recruiting for spring 2027 is under way now) and rotate them across departments for years; Hitachi expects about ¥269,000 a month for bachelor's recruits (2026) and about 90% of surveyed firms raised starting pay for 2026 grads (Mynavi, secondary). In Singapore and Hong Kong, management-trainee pay is below finance (HKTVmall HK$32,000 a month; DBS pay unpublished). Gulf sources were weak, so I give no firm Gulf numbers.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
