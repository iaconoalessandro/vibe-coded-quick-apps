What you actually do

You advise companies in or near distress, or the creditors who hold their debt (section 2.5). Typical analyst work: building a 13-week cash-flow forecast and liquidity model (distressed companies run out of cash, so weekly cash is the central number), a "recovery waterfall" (who gets paid what, in order of priority, under different valuation outcomes), a capital-structure summary (every loan and bond, security, maturity, covenants), a business plan model, and comparisons of options: refinancing, an out-of-court amend-and-extend, an LME such as an uptier exchange, a sale of assets, or a court process (Chapter 11 in the US, UK restructuring plans or schemes of arrangement). You also prepare presentations for creditors and the board, support negotiations, and, in court cases, support expert-testimony materials. The work blends M&A (distressed sales), debt advice and legal/process knowledge. Because restructuring touches documents and law, you work with restructuring lawyers constantly.

A typical day and week

Highly situation-driven. In a live case: early morning call with the company's CFO and advisers about cash; mid-morning update of the liquidity model; afternoon meeting with creditors' advisers; evening work on a term sheet or court filing materials. Many cases run in sprints around bond payment dates, covenant tests, grace periods and court deadlines; weekends are common during a filing. When the cycle is slow, the team does pitches to companies approaching maturity "walls" and monitors credit markets. Travel to client sites and to court locations is more frequent than in M&A (UK/US/European cases). Hybrid working is limited in live cases.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Hours: typical 70-85, peaks of 100+ around a filing or deadline; I found no 2025-26 survey isolating restructuring hours, so this is a practitioner estimate. Stress: 4-5/5 (high stakes, liquidity running out, emotionally charged negotiations, but fewer pitch-book revisions than in coverage). Lifestyle: lumpy; busy when credit is stressed. Restructuring groups are often said to have a more mature, less formatting-driven culture, and they are one of the places where analysts get early responsibility; this is practitioner perception, not measured.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You need strong accounting and credit logic (cash, covenants, priority of claims) and the ability to negotiate and manage tense meetings; it rewards people who like puzzles combining numbers, legal structure and people.

Compensation (approximate)

Entry pay follows the same bank/boutique analyst scales as M&A at the same employer; Prospect Rock (2023) states that restructuring analyst pay is "competitive with other IB roles" and gives no figures. The best proxies are analyst totals at restructuring-heavy firms in London: Houlihan Lokey about £115,000 (base £61-70k plus £30-80k bonus), PJT Partners about £110,000, Rothschild & Co about £104,000, Lazard about £90,000, Perella Weinberg about £119,000 (PrepLounge, 27 August 2026; aggregator). US figures as 3.1 (base US$105-110k for juniors; total around US$150-170k+ depending on year and bucket). Senior-level restructuring MD pay: no group-specific figure found. Italy: no reliable data found; Milan restructuring teams at Rothschild, Lazard, Houlihan Lokey, UniCredit/Intesa special situations and Big 4 pay on the local scales in 3.1 and 3.6. Hong Kong, Singapore, Dubai, Tokyo: no reliable data found.

Career path

Analyst → Associate → VP → Director → MD, with specialisation often starting at associate or VP level (Prospect Rock). Many teams are small, so promotion depends on revenue from a few mandates; lateral moves between restructuring and debt advisory or M&A are common. In Europe, restructuring bankers often sit within "financing solutions and restructuring" groups (Rothschild & Co's Global Financing Solutions & Restructuring group has over 200 bankers in 14 countries and advises on over US$200 billion of capital raises and restructurings each year, per its own programme page).

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: distressed credit and special-situations hedge funds, direct lending/private credit, distressed or special-situations PE, M&A or coverage groups, turnaround consulting, corporate development. Exit evidence is mostly from career guides and forums; the common claim is that credit funds are more typical than classic buyout PE, and that creditor-side experience helps for distressed funds (anecdotal). After 7-10 years: partner at a restructuring boutique, credit fund portfolio manager or head of a special-situations team, chief restructuring officer roles, or in-house at distressed investors.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1: Houlihan Lokey, PJT Partners, Lazard, Rothschild & Co, Evercore, Perella Weinberg, Moelis. Houlihan Lokey topped LSEG's 2025 global distressed and restructuring tables by deal count (83 versus PJT 55, Lazard 50, Rothschild 44, Evercore 33; figures from Houlihan Lokey's own site, snippet-level). In Debtwire's Q2/H1 2026 Europe table, PJT led by number of mandates (8), ahead of Houlihan Lokey (7) and Rothschild (6).
  • Tier 2: Large bulge-bracket restructuring/liability-management groups (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Citi, Barclays, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, Lazard-like European teams), Ducera, Solomon Partners, Centerview (restructuring group), Greenhill (now part of Mizuho), Jefferies, Guggenheim, Gordian, GLC Advisors (recruiting for restructuring in its 2026 analyst class per a job-board posting, snippet-level).
  • Tier 3: Operational restructuring and turnaround advisers (Alvarez & Marsal, FTI Consulting, Big 4 restructuring teams: they were among the Q2 2026 European leaders by mandates), mid-market and regional boutiques (Stephens, Raymond James, Oppenheimer, local Italian and German advisers). Different work: more operational and process-heavy, lower pay, shorter hours.

How to enter

Programmes and calendar as in 3.1: Rothschild's Global Financing Solutions & Restructuring summer analyst programme (London, 10 weeks from June 2027, requires an expected 2:1 and interest in corporate finance and debt markets; interns work on refinancings, acquisitions, LBOs, IPO financings and restructurings; applications opened 19 August 2026, rolling). Most boutiques hire into an "M&A or restructuring" pool and you choose a team after joining. Skills: accounting and credit, reading debt documents, cash-flow forecasting, knowledge of bankruptcy and restructuring processes (a plus), and recent case study knowledge (interviews reportedly ask about recent bankruptcies and recovery waterfalls; anecdotal). Degrees: finance, accounting, economics, law with finance, engineering. Mistakes: assuming restructuring is "losers' banking" (it is core at elite boutiques), not knowing basic bankruptcy mechanics, or not following current LME developments. Alternative routes: Big 4 restructuring or transaction services, credit rating agencies, or law/accountancy then lateral into advisory at associate level (post-grad restructuring talent is in demand: lateral hiring is strong amid LMEs and private credit, per a June 2026 trade article, snippet-level). Entry difficulty: 4/5 overall (5/5 at the best boutiques with few seats; 3/5 at Big 4 restructuring).

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Counter-cyclical demand means a long, unpredictable swing; the work can be emotionally heavy (job losses, creditor fights, litigation), with high-intensity periods; it is a narrower market than M&A, so transferability depends on the cycle. Hours rival M&A. Not a good fit if you dislike conflict, legal complexity, working with troubled clients, or very lumpy workloads; also not ideal if you want a classic private equity buyout exit as the main goal (exits to credit and special situations are the strongest).

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
