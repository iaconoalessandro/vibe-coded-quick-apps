What you actually do

You build mathematical models that tell a business what to do. Concrete examples: decide which warehouse serves which customers, design delivery routes for 500 vans, schedule crews and aircraft, set airline fares and seat limits per flight (revenue management), size safety stock, simulate a new hospital layout, optimise power dispatch. Method: formulate the problem (objective, decision variables, constraints), solve with a solver or heuristic, test against simulation and historical data, deliver a tool or recommendation. Tools: Python, a modelling library or solver (Gurobi, CPLEX, OR-Tools, Pyomo), simulation software, SQL, sometimes Julia/AMPL; Excel remains common at the business end. Deliverables: optimisation engines, what-if tools, business cases quantifying savings.

A typical day and week

Mix of modelling (50%) and stakeholder work (clarifying constraints with planners, validating that the model "feels right" to the people who run operations). Weekly cadence of progress reviews; occasional site visits to warehouses or hangars.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

38-45 typical, 50-55 peak at go-live or planning season (consensus). Stress 2/5 (occasional crunch at go-live or when an operational crisis hits). Mostly desk work, light travel to sites. Hybrid common. A BLS note: most operations research analysts work full time.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5 at research-heavy teams; 4/5 for analyst roles. Mathematical modelling is the core, but the model only matters if planners trust it.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: BLS operations research analysts median US$88,940 (May 2025); P10 US$57,060; P90 US$159,910. Growth 12% 2025-35, about 7,500 openings a year. Entry roughly US$55-75k typical outside tech (state career portals show about US$48k for entry-level; older and weak). Amazon-type optimisation scientist roles in big tech fall in the applied-scientist band (3.6); not verified for OR-specific titles.
  • UK: a careers-profile figure of GBP 25-29k starting and GBP 40-80k+ for experienced operational researchers (undated, probably older); UK Government Operational Research Service (GORS) sandwich-placement advert Nov 2025 GBP 22,699-31,324 pro rata; Fast Stream operational researchers start around GBP 27k (older figure). ITJobsWatch "decision scientist" median GBP 82,500 (6 months to 10 May 2025, only 2 salaries quoted; anecdotal).
  • Italy: no reliable data found. Continental Europe and Asia: no reliable data found.

Career path

OR analyst/associate (0-3) → senior analyst/scientist (3-7) → lead/principal scientist, optimisation architect or planning manager (7-12). Public-sector route: GORS analyst → senior → head of profession.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: supply-chain planning and analytics, data science, consulting (operations), software vendors of planning tools. After 7-10 years: head of supply-chain analytics, product owner of an optimisation product, consulting partner track, operations leadership.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Employer names below are general industry knowledge, not verified in this round.

  • Tier 1: large logistics/e-commerce optimisation groups, major airlines' revenue-management and network-planning teams, planning-software vendors (Gurobi, Kinaxis, Blue Yonder, o9, FICO), and defence/government research bodies. Deepest OR work and best pay; few entry posts.
  • Tier 2: manufacturers, retailers, utilities and grid operators (Enel and Terna in Italy), banks' operations units, consultancies' supply-chain practices.
  • Tier 3: general operations analyst roles where "analyst" means Excel reporting.

How to enter

  • Degrees: industrial/management engineering, mathematics, applied mathematics, operations research, computer science, physics; a master's (OR, industrial engineering, applied maths) is the usual route for modelling roles, and BLS says a bachelor's is the minimum with some employers preferring a master's. Italy is strong in the field through engineering faculties (e.g. gestionale/industrial engineering) - consensus; I did not verify programme details.
  • Programmes and calendars: UK government GORS via Fast Stream (applications open autumn); corporate supply-chain graduate programmes follow the usual autumn-to-spring cycle.
  • Interviews: optimisation modelling case (formulate a vehicle routing or scheduling problem on a whiteboard), probability and statistics, coding (Python), behavioural.
  • Certifications: INFORMS Certified Analytics Professional (CAP) now has three tiers: CAP-Essentials (early career, no eligibility requirements), CAP-Pro, and CAP-Expert (requires an advanced degree with 2 years of analytics experience, or bachelor's with 4, or 8 years' experience). They are recognised in the US more than in Europe; they help rather than decide.
  • Skills and portfolio: linear/integer programming, simulation, solver use, a project such as a delivery-route optimiser or a staff-rostering model on open data.
  • Common mistakes: building elegant models without involving the planners; ignoring data quality; not quantifying business value.
  • Alternative routes: logistics or supply-chain graduates add a quantitative master's or a strong Python/solver portfolio; data analysts can move via planning teams.
  • Entry difficulty: 3/5 (smaller field, specific skills, but fewer applicants than product data science).

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Niche job market (a small number of employers use real optimisation); many roles are labelled "analyst" and become reporting; adoption by planners can be slow; pay is lower than product/ML data science outside big tech. Not a good fit if you dislike formal mathematics or want fast-moving product work.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
