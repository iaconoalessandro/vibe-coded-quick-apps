What you actually do

You cover a list of institutional clients (fund managers, pension funds, insurers, corporates, hedge funds), relay market colour and the desk's ideas, take orders and relay prices, set up trades, and sometimes arrange meetings with analysts and strategists. Deliverables: morning notes/calls, trade ideas, client lists, order tickets, "sales credits" (the revenue attributed to you). Tools: Bloomberg chat, phones, CRM, pricing screens, bank research.

Desk notes:

  • Rates/credit sales: relationship-driven but increasingly supported by electronic platforms; credit sales cover many mid-sized accounts and push bond inventory; rates sales include "solutions" to pension and insurance clients.
  • FX sales: two models: institutional (asset managers, hedge funds) and corporate (hedging treasury flows), the latter more consultative. Electronic execution is the norm for small tickets.
  • Equities sales and sales trading: cash equities salespeople cover long-only funds and hedge funds; sales traders execute orders and are on the phone/chat all day; derivatives sales covers vol and structured products with a heavier quant element.
  • Commodities sales: hedging solutions for corporates (airlines, utilities, miners).

A typical day and week

(Practitioner consensus.) 7:00am start: morning call with the desk, a note to clients; 8:00-4:30 calls and chats, order handling; late afternoon: client meetings or calls; evenings: client dinners/entertainment 1-3 nights per week for senior people; Fridays are usually lighter. Travel: conferences, client roadshows, in-person visits are part of the job (Europe/Asia more than for traders).

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Hours: 55-65 per week typically, 70+ at peak; a 2021 reader comment on eFinancialCareers noted evening client entertainment should be counted in hours (opinion). Stress: 3/5 (4 in volatile markets and on bonus days); the pressure is relationship-driven and revenue-driven; you are expected to be "always on" for key clients. Remote/hybrid: mostly office-based; some regulatory limits on working from home on a desk.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 2/5 (3/5 for derivatives sales). You succeed by being trusted, accurate and fast in explaining products and moves; you need enough market maths to explain pricing.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US (NY), 2025 (Selby Jennings): sales base: analyst US$125k credit/rates/FX, US$100-125k cash equity and corporate FX/rates, associate US$150-175k, VP US$175-275k, director US$250-400k, MD US$350-600k. Total pay: analyst US$175-225k credit and FX, US$175-250k rates and equity derivatives, US$150-225k corporate FX/rates, US$120-170k cash equity; associate US$250-375k (cash equity US$225-350k); VP US$400-700k (rates and equity derivatives up to US$750k; cash equity US$300-450k); director US$575-900k (cash equity US$450-600k); MD US$775k-2m (cash equity US$650k-1.4m).
  • UK: no reliable data found for sales specifically; use the trader ranges above as a rough guide, noting that sales pay is usually somewhat lower than trading pay at the same level in the same desk (inference from the NY table, where sales totals are usually a little below trading totals).
  • Hong Kong/Singapore and Tokyo/Dubai: no reliable S&T-specific data found; see 3.1 for 2024 quant-heavy sell-side bands.
  • Italy: no reliable data found. Proxy: Michael Page Italy 2026 "business development manager" in the banking section is €30-55k RAL for under five years with a 50-60% bonus (Michael Page Italy, 2026).

Career path

Analyst (years 1-3) → Associate → VP (a "coverage" role: you own accounts) → Director → MD (a senior franchise sales person). Equity sales and sales traders can also move to "head of sales" roles. Promotion depends on client revenue and relationships; star salespeople can be very well paid.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: another bank, a sales role at an electronic platform or broker, buy-side relationship role, corporate treasury or fintech sales. After 7-10 years: head of sales, institutional sales at an asset manager or fund (capital-introduction/ investor relations at hedge funds), or a client-facing role at an exchange or data provider.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1: Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Morgan Stanley, Citi, Bank of America: global clients and best products to sell.
  • Tier 2: Barclays, Deutsche Bank, UBS, BNP Paribas, Société Générale, HSBC, Nomura, Jefferies, RBC, Macquarie, Mizuho.
  • Tier 3: regional banks and brokers (Intesa Sanpaolo IMI CIB, UniCredit, Equita in Italy; Santander, ING, Natixis, Commerzbank, Banca Akros; Asian regional firms): narrower client base, local language and relationships matter.

How to enter

Same programmes as 3.1 (summer analyst programmes that rotate across sales, trading and structuring). Any degree works if you show numeracy and communication; languages are a real advantage in Europe and Asia (Tokyo: BNP Paribas's 2027 Global Markets graduate programme in Tokyo lists fluent Japanese and English as must-haves, per its posting, via search results). Skills: public speaking, concise market summaries, product knowledge, empathy for client needs. Alternative routes: junior roles in sales support/trade support, client service at a broker or fund administrator, or a regional bank. Certifications: none needed; employer sponsors licences. Mistakes: being all personality and no market knowledge; failing to track the news; poor listening. Entry difficulty: 4/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Your revenue depends on client activity you do not control; sales is the first function to shrink when execution goes electronic; client entertainment and early starts can be exhausting; you are often the "face" blamed when the desk quotes badly; pay has less upside than trading at the top. Not a good fit if you dislike constant phone/chat contact, if you hate client entertainment, or if you want to be mostly analytical.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
