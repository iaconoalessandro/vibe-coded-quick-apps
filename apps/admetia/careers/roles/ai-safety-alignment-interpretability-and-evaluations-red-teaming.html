What you actually do

Alignment researchers run experiments on whether models behave as intended (October 2026 examples: Anthropic's London role testing safety techniques by training models to subvert them; OpenAI's "Researcher, Alignment CoT Monitorability" and "Researcher, Safety Oversight"; Scale AI's "Research Scientist, AI Controls and Monitoring"). Interpretability researchers analyse model internals (features and circuits). Evaluations researchers design tasks that measure capabilities and risks: METR's roles include "evaluation execution" and "task development engineer" with a roughly two-day paid work trial; Scale AI lists "Frontier Risk Evaluations" and OpenAI "Data Scientist, Preparedness". Red teamers attack products: Anthropic's Red Team Engineer, Safeguards role asks for penetration-testing and jailbreak experience and a public body of work; OpenAI lists a "Red Team Specialist - Cyber"; AISI has alignment, control and misuse red teams. Government evaluators (UK AISI, US CAISI) test models before and after release; AISI open-sources its evaluation tool Inspect.

A typical day and week

Running and analysing experiments; writing evaluation tasks and scoring rubrics; reviewing transcripts of model behaviour; discussing findings with policy or product teams; writing up a safety case or a blog post. In evaluations roles, deadlines come from model release dates: pre-deployment testing windows can be short. Red teamers spend time crafting attacks, building automation, and reporting.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Typical 45-55 hours, peaks 60-70 before model releases (estimate). Stress 3-4/5: high-stakes releases and the sense that errors matter. Government institutes are closer to 40-45 hours with hybrid working (AISI offers hybrid, with offices in London and other UK government sites). METR and Apollo are mostly office-based in Berkeley, London or San Francisco; Apollo offers flexible hours and visa sponsorship.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. Strong written communication is needed because the output is arguments and evidence; interpretability and theory roles are heavily mathematical; red teaming leans on security skills.

Career path

Fellowship or master's (0-1 year) -> junior researcher/engineer, research contributor (1-3 years) -> senior researcher, research lead (3-7 years) -> team lead or head of a safety area (7+). 80,000 Hours describes the same split: contributor versus research lead, with a PhD "practically the default" for leads. Government path: technical staff -> research scientist -> workstream lead (AISI's levels above).

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: mainstream ML research, safeguards or trust-and-safety roles at labs, AI security startups, government institutes. After 7-10 years: head of safety/evaluations at a lab or institute, founder of an evaluation or security startup, senior government advisor, policy leadership.

Tier list of employers (alphabetical within each group)

  • Tier 1: Google DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic (lab safety teams); UK AI Security Institute and US CAISI (state evaluators with pre-release access to models: AISI has access agreements with Anthropic, Google and OpenAI); METR, Apollo Research and Redwood Research (specialist nonprofits).
  • Tier 2: Academic groups (Berkeley/CHAI, Oxford, Cambridge, MIT, NYU), the EU AI Office's AI Safety unit, other national institutes (Japan, Canada, Singapore; general knowledge), big-tech safeguards and trust-and-safety teams, safety research teams at Scale AI, xAI and Cohere.
  • Tier 3: corporate red teams in banks and cybersecurity firms with an AI practice, small startups and consultancies.

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

How to enter

Backgrounds: ML, software engineering, security, physics, maths. Several fellowships say a PhD, prior ML experience or papers are not required (Anthropic Fellows and Astra say no prior AI safety experience is needed; the OpenAI Safety Fellowship asks for no specific credentials but requires reference letters). MATS (631 researchers since late 2021; 75% of alumni work in alignment, transparency or security; alumni at Anthropic, DeepMind, OpenAI, UK AISI, METR; per its own site) is the best-known pipeline; Anthropic reports that over 35% of past Fellows joined it full-time (employer-reported). Cohorts: Anthropic Fellows (4 months; apply by 18 October for January); MATS Winter 2027 applications closed in October 2026; Astra's have run late August to late September; Pivotal and LASR open rounds ahead of each cohort. Horizon covers policy-oriented tech talent in the US (3.5). Portfolio: replicate a safety paper, run evaluations with an open framework such as Inspect, write a public post analysing model behaviour, produce a red-team report, hold CVEs or bug-bounty credit. Interviews: Apollo's process is short application questions, a 30-minute screen, a 2-2.5 hour take-home, three 60-minute interviews and a leadership interview; METR uses a code screen, take-home, interviews and a roughly two-day paid work trial; UK AISI uses a CV, short written statement, skills assessment and 2-4 interviews, sponsors visas for technical roles, and requires civil-service nationality for non-technical roles. Certifications: none valued. Common mistakes: reading only the discourse and never producing empirical work; applying with no artefact; applying to one organization only. Alternative routes: security professionals (red teaming), physicists and mathematicians (interpretability and theory), lawyers and policy people into evaluation policy; fellowships open the door for non-target schools. Entry difficulty: 5/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

The field is small and jobs are few; funding and strategy shift with politics (the UK institute was renamed from "Safety" to "Security" in February 2025). Nonprofit and government roles can be insecure or capped in pay (AISI's top published total is GBP 145,000); lab safety teams depend on company priorities. Reviewing harmful outputs (for enforcement and safeguards roles) can be distressing. Not a fit for people who need a standardised career ladder, who are mainly drawn by pay (non-lab jobs pay far less), or who dislike uncertainty about whether the work will matter.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
