What you actually do

Corporate banking / relationship management: you manage relationships with companies, from medium-sized firms to large multinationals, and sell them loans, credit lines, payment and cash management services, trade finance, currency and interest-rate hedging, and, through the investment bank, bond issues or M&A advice. As an analyst or graduate you prepare credit papers (the internal credit approval memos with financial analysis, covenants, risk grading), monitor client financials, price loans, support client meetings, and coordinate with product specialists, credit, risk and legal teams. Barclays describes its UK corporate bank graduates as working with clients from mid-sized businesses to FTSE 350 firms, rotating through support functions, and aiming for a career as a corporate banker or Relationship Director (Barclays careers, accessed October 2026); HSBC's two-year Corporate and Institutional Banking programme rotates through relationship banking and product teams, including trade finance, cash management and capital markets. Transaction banking: product specialists and sales who design and deliver cash management, payments, liquidity, supply-chain finance and trade finance solutions; at Citi it is called Treasury and Trade Solutions (TTS), at Deutsche Bank Global Transaction Banking. Work is more operational and client-service oriented, with technology and regulation (sanctions, anti-money-laundering) as important parts.

A typical day and week

Meetings with clients' finance teams (CFOs, treasurers) and internal credit teams; preparing credit approvals and annual reviews; responding to client requests (new facilities, covenant waivers, payment issues). Weeks follow client and credit-committee calendars with moderate peaks around large financings or year-end reviews. Travel is common for relationship managers (client visits), minimal for support analysts. Hybrid/remote work is more accepted than in IBD.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Hours: typical 45-55 per week, peaks of 60-65 for large financings or reviews. I found no survey for these roles; this is a practitioner-based estimate consistent with the general view that corporate banking is "generally less intense" than IBD (no hard data found). Stress: 3/5 (credit risk, client expectations, regulation), lower than IBD. Lifestyle: more predictable, hybrid friendly, greater long-term stability. Flexibility: higher than banking at junior levels.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 5/5 (this is a relationships job). Quantitative/technical: 3/5 (credit and financial statement analysis, not advanced maths; transaction banking adds product, payments and regulatory knowledge).

Compensation (approximate)

United States: JPMorgan's Global Corporate Banking summer analyst role in New York listed US$100,000 a year (2025 posting); an experienced analyst in the Consumer, Retail & Business Services group listed a US$105,000-110,000 base; Wells Fargo's Financial Institutions Group corporate banking analyst role listed US$100,000-110,000 base (February 2026 posting); bonus not disclosed. Self-reported bonuses are generally lower than IBD analysts (WSO data on middle-market and bulge-bracket analysts of US$65-75k are for IB, not corporate banking; the closest proxy, anecdotal). United Kingdom: HSBC's Commercial Banking graduate programme listed £35,000 plus a £5,000 welcome bonus (2025 intake, not London-based); Barclays does not publish a figure; Indeed's HSBC analyst average is about £43,700 (generic title; unreliable for this role). No reliable corporate-banking-specific London figure found; practitioner talk is that CIB-aligned corporate banking graduates in London earn below IBD analysts (unverified). Italy: Intesa Sanpaolo I'M IN CIB programme from EUR 40,000 gross a year (8 July 2026, secondary source); UniCredit Milan graduate analyst EUR 40,000 gross base (posting, undated). Both programmes also include non-corporate-banking roles (rates, credit trading, M&A). Bonuses are smaller than in IBD (unverified). Hong Kong/Singapore/Dubai/Tokyo: no reliable data found; a Michael Page Singapore guide shows a corporate banking relationship manager, executive director band of S$250,000-350,000 (2026; snippet-level), which indicates that senior relationship managers can earn comfortably but tells you nothing about entry pay. Seniors: Michael Page (Singapore 2026) as above; no reliable data for other regions.

Career path

Graduate/analyst (2-3 years rotation) → Associate/Assistant relationship manager (credit paper authorship) → Relationship Manager or Vice President (own a portfolio, 3-5 years) → Director/Senior RM (large corporates or sector heads) → Managing Director/Head of corporate banking. Transaction banking: analyst → sales/product specialist → head of region/product. Promotions are slower and more stable than IBD; many people stay in the same bank for long periods. A common internal move is into investment banking coverage or credit risk.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: coverage or capital-markets roles in the same bank, credit risk, treasury or corporate finance at a corporate, private credit and direct lending (analyst/associate), leveraged finance at the same bank, fintech and payments companies (for transaction banking). After 7-10 years: head of a corporate banking team, corporate CFO/treasurer, direct lending portfolio manager, chief risk roles, or senior fintech roles. Moves into mega-fund private equity from corporate banking are unusual (my assessment; unverified).

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1 (global corporate banking franchises): JPMorgan, Citi (its TTS franchise), HSBC, Bank of America, Barclays, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, Santander, Wells Fargo (US). Large-cap clients, global product range, integrated with investment banking.
  • Tier 2: National champions: Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit (Italy), ING, ABN AMRO, Commerzbank, Société Générale, BBVA, Lloyds, NatWest, Mizuho/MUFG/SMBC (Japanese megabanks, global reach), FAB and Emirates NBD (Gulf). Mid and large domestic corporates, strong local relationships.
  • Tier 3: Regional and savings banks, mid-sized domestic banks (Italy's Banco BPM, BPER, banche popolari, Germany's Landesbanken, US regional banks). Smaller corporates, lower pay, less international exposure, but often more local autonomy.

How to enter

Graduate programmes are the main route and are large and open to a wide range of degrees: Barclays UK Corporate Bank Graduate programme (rotations, 2027 intake listed), HSBC Corporate and Institutional Banking Graduate Programme (two years) and Commercial Banking Graduate Programme (UK; three six-month rotations), Citi corporate banking and TTS summer and graduate analyst programmes, Deutsche Bank Corporate Bank graduate programmes, and, in Italy, the CIB graduate schemes of Intesa and UniCredit (master's in economics, finance, maths or statistics, English B2). Degrees: any degree is common in the UK (business, economics, accounting, finance and others); Italian programmes expect an economics/finance/quantitative master's. Calendar: similar to UK graduate recruitment (applications from August-September for the following year; deadlines vary; I found no consolidated calendar for 2026-27). Skills: financial statement analysis, credit basics, communication and negotiation, sector knowledge, product knowledge for transaction banking (trade finance instruments, payments). Certifications: none required; a banking/credit certificate (such as the Chartered Banker or credit qualifications) can help in the UK (unverified); the CFA is of limited use. Mistakes: treating it as a fallback with no preparation, ignoring the importance of knowing clients' sectors, and not asking about rotation structure. Alternative routes: come from audit, credit risk or sector roles, or from a regional bank. Entry difficulty: 2/5 to 3/5 (many openings and large intakes, still competitive at global banks).

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Lower pay ceiling than IBD for equivalent seniority; more regulation, credit committee processes and paperwork; slower promotions; revenue depends on interest rates (margins fall when rates fall) and on credit losses. Roles are often tied to a portfolio or geography. Not a good fit if you want the intensity and deal exposure of M&A, are driven by the largest possible pay quickly, or dislike credit and compliance. Better fit if you want client relationships, stability, hybrid work, and a pathway into credit, treasury or corporate finance.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
