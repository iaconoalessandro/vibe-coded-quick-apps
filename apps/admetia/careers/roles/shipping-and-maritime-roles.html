What you actually do

Ship brokers help shipowners find cargoes and charterers find ships, in dry bulk, tankers, containers or sale-and-purchase (S&P); they quote, negotiate rates, draft fixtures on BIMCO-based contracts, and follow up post-fixture. Chartering/operations in a shipowner or trader: they manage voyages, laytime and demurrage claims, port agents and bunkers (fuel). Liner operations at a container line: customer service, pricing, network planning, equipment repositioning, port and terminal coordination. A trainee broker at Hagland, as posted in 2026, shadows client meetings, drafts charter contracts, tracks vessel positions and prepares market reports (TargetJobs listing).

A typical day and week

Brokers: early start on market reports and vessel positions, phones and chats most of the day, deals in the afternoon, client entertainment in the evening. Liner operations: monitoring vessel schedules, rerouting around Hormuz or the Cape, handling customer exceptions. Both are highly sensitive to geopolitical shocks.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Brokers: 50-60 hours typical (longer during active markets) with an early start and client entertainment; stress 4/5. Liner operations: 40-50 hours with weekend and on-call coverage; stress 3-4/5. Mostly office-based, some travel (Posidonia in Athens, Marintec, ports); remote work rare for junior brokers. Hours data for brokers rests on practitioner descriptions rather than a published survey (unverified).

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 5/5 for brokers and 3-4/5 for liner customer-facing; Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Brokers need fast arithmetic (freight rate and voyage estimates), market knowledge and relationships; liner network planning is more analytical (Excel, SQL).

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: no reliable data found (chartering is concentrated in London, Singapore, Athens, Oslo and the Gulf; liner and port roles in the US are typically paid like other logistics jobs).
  • UK: trainee shipbroker GBP 25,000-28,000 plus performance bonus (Edinburgh posting); around GBP 27,000 plus potential bonus, with funded ICS exams (Hagland Aberdeen, 2026); London trainee pay at Clarksons, Braemar and SSY: no reliable data found (anecdotally higher; unverified). Experienced broker pay depends on bonus and is highly variable; Clarksons' CEO total package of GBP 11.9m is not representative.
  • Italy: no reliable data found (Genoa/Trieste ship-agency and liner jobs are paid within logistics bands, EUR 25-35k; unverified).
  • Singapore/Gulf: no reliable data found.

Career path

Trainee broker or operations trainee (0-1, often with a 12-18 month training) → junior broker (1-3) → broker (3-7) → senior broker / desk head / director (7-15) with equity or bonus participation. Liner: customer service or pricing analyst → sales/pricing manager → regional head.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: another broker desk, shipowner operations, trading house chartering desks (Cargill, Trafigura, Vitol and similar; see the finance report on commodity trading), or port agency. After 7-10 years: head of chartering at a shipowner or trader, establishing a brokerage, shipping finance, or liner commercial management.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1 brokers/shipping groups: Clarksons, Braemar, SSY, Howe Robinson (these appear in trainee-scheme searches; Clarksons runs a one-year Global Trainee Broker Programme, SSY a 12-month London trainee scheme), Maersk, MSC, CMA CGM, Hapag-Lloyd, COSCO, Evergreen, and major trading houses' shipping desks.
  • Tier 2: Regional and specialist brokers (Nordic, Greek, Singapore), mid-size carriers (ONE, HMM, Yang Ming, ZIM), large terminal operators (PSA, DP World, APM Terminals, Hutchison; PSA handled 105m TEU globally in 2025).
  • Tier 3: Small agencies and local port-agent firms, family-owned Italian shipping companies and agencies.

How to enter

Any degree (economics, business, languages, geography, law) with motivation for shipping; maritime degrees (Southampton Solent, Plymouth, Genoa, Trieste, Rotterdam/Antwerp programmes, unverified) help. Programmes: Clarksons Global Trainee Broker Programme (one year, rotations, possible overseas secondment), SSY trainee scheme (London, 12 months; applications opened 1 February for a September intake in 2024), Braemar trainee scheme, Howe Robinson trainee shipbroker, Maersk UK Graduate Programme (customs, sales, customer experience, finance pathways; no sponsorship). The ICS Graduate role with Maritime UK is also listed. Calendar: broker schemes often recruit in winter/spring for a September start (SSY); smaller brokers recruit ad hoc. Certifications: ICS (Institute of Chartered Shipbrokers) Professional Qualifying Examinations lead to MICS membership; Foundation and Advanced Diplomas exist (ICS website); many brokers fund them. They matter for broker careers (employers fund and expect them), not for general logistics. Skills: market curiosity, clear writing, Excel, numeracy, network building, familiarity with shipping news (TradeWinds, Lloyd's List). Common mistakes: applying with no evidence of interest in shipping; ignoring right-to-work (many UK brokers cannot sponsor visas, e.g. Hagland). Alternative routes: junior operator at a ship agent, port or terminal roles, then lateral moves. Entry difficulty: 4/5 for Clarksons-type schemes (few places, high competition); 2-3/5 for liner and port roles.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Earnings swing with the freight market, early hours, a heavy culture of entertainment and long working weeks, a small industry where reputation follows you, and a trend toward consolidation. Liner roles are exposed to cyclical profit swings. Not a good fit for people who dislike sales-oriented, relationship-based work, who want fixed pay, or who need structured work-life balance.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
