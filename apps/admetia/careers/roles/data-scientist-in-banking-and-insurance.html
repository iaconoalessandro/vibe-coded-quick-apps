What you actually do

You build and maintain models that decide money questions: probability of default for loan applicants (credit scoring), the expected loss reserve under accounting standard IFRS 9, transaction fraud scores, customer lifetime value, claims-severity and pricing models for car or home insurance, anti-money-laundering alert ranking. Banks usually separate model development (the data scientist) from independent model validation and from model risk oversight; every model needs documentation, back-testing and explainability for regulators. Tools: Python, SQL, SAS (still common in credit risk), gradient boosting, logistic regression and scorecards, cloud platforms. Deliverables: model code and documentation, monitoring reports, committee presentations.

A typical day and week

Model build and testing (40%), documentation and governance (25%), data checks (20%), stakeholder meetings with risk and business owners (15%). Regulatory deadlines and audit findings cause bursts of documentation work.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

38-45 typical, 50 peak near regulatory deadlines (consensus). Stress 3/5: steady deadlines, accountability for model failures, but far from investment-banking hours. Hybrid is standard in UK and European banks, fully on-site expectations rising in US banks (consensus). Little travel.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. Explaining a model to a risk committee and validators counts as much as its accuracy.

Career path

Graduate/associate modeller (0-2) → data scientist / model developer (2-5) → senior / VP (5-9) → head of credit analytics, chief data scientist or model risk head (10+). Insurance: pricing analyst → pricing data scientist → head of pricing; actuarial qualification opens another ladder.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: fintech or big-tech risk teams, consulting (risk analytics), model validation, credit strategy, or other lenders. After 7-10 years: chief risk officer track, head of data science, fintech leadership.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1: data-driven lenders and large global banks with significant modelling teams (Capital One, JPMorgan, Barclays, HSBC), and leading insurers/pricing houses (Admiral, Allianz, AXA, Zurich, Aviva). Strong tooling, best pay inside finance.
  • Tier 2: high-street and national banks (NatWest, Lloyds, Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit) and Italian insurers (Generali, UnipolSai). Large intakes, rotational programmes, stronger governance burden.
  • Tier 3: regional banks, smaller insurers, and outsourced analytics providers; smaller teams and less modern stack.

How to enter

  • Degrees: STEM (maths, statistics, physics, engineering, computer science) or economics with quantitative focus; NatWest requires a 2:1 in STEM; Admiral lists maths, physics, engineering, chemistry or biology; a master's is common but not required for UK graduate schemes. For Italy a laurea magistrale in statistics, economics, mathematics or data science is typical.
  • Programmes and calendar: UK schemes open August-October for next September, with early deadlines (Capital One closed 6 Sept 2026; Admiral's 2026 intake closed 26 Oct 2025; Aviva's pathway has closed; Allianz Ireland's data analytics programme closes 11 Oct 2026). Italian banks and insurers use a mix of graduate programmes (Intesa Sanpaolo SLAM, a 24-month international programme; Generali's international programme) and internships/stage; UniCredit Group runs a 12-month rotational trainee programme with a 2026 "data scientist for agentic AI and banking" track in Munich.
  • Interviews: online tests (logic, numeracy), coding (SQL/Python) tests, case study on risk or pricing, technical interview on regression and classification metrics (AUC, Gini, KS), behavioural.
  • Certifications: not required; for insurance, actuarial exams (UK Institute and Faculty of Actuaries, Italian Albo) are a different path; FRM/PRM are sometimes seen in risk functions (general knowledge).
  • Skills and portfolio: logistic regression and gradient boosting, scorecard development, calibration, explainability (SHAP), fairness testing, SQL; a credit-scoring project using public data with proper validation.
  • Common mistakes: ignoring interpretability and regulation; treating bank data as clean; overselling deep learning.
  • Alternative routes: analysts in risk or finance with a coding upgrade; actuarial trainees adding machine learning; from the Lloyds scheme, which does not require a particular degree classification.
  • Entry difficulty: 3/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Heavy governance and documentation slow delivery; legacy systems; models limited to explainable techniques; pay at the entry level is much lower than big tech. Not a good fit if you want rapid experimentation or cutting-edge ML, or if you dislike regulation and process.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.
