What you actually do

You build software products whose core is a foundation-model call. You design and tune a RAG pipeline (splitting documents into chunks, embedding them as vectors, storing them in a vector database, retrieving the best passages, and prompting the model to answer from them with citations); build agents that call tools (search, databases, code execution, internal APIs) over many steps; write prompts and structured-output schemas; fine-tune smaller models when it pays off; and, critically, build evaluation: a test set of hundreds of prompts with automated and human grading, regression checks on every change, and cost/latency tracking. A concrete example: you build an evaluation harness of 500 test questions, retune chunk size and reranking so answer accuracy rises from 71% to 84%, and cut p95 latency (the time within which 95% of requests finish) from 4 seconds to 1.5 seconds by caching and streaming.

A typical day and week

Mornings reviewing failed evaluation cases and user feedback; midday building a feature (a new tool for the agent, a guardrail, a retrieval improvement); afternoon syncing with product and design. Weekly: ship an update, run the evaluation suite, review cost dashboards (token spend can dominate costs), talk to customers or support about failures. Because models change every few months, a regular task is re-testing your prompts and pipelines on a new model version.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

40-50 hours typical, 55-65 at startups near launches (practitioner consensus). Stress 3/5 (4/5 at fast startups and customer-facing applied roles at labs). Hybrid or remote is common at startups and software companies; consulting adds client travel. On-call exists if you run a production service.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You talk to users and stakeholders often and need judgment about what "good output" means, and you need solid software engineering; deep mathematics is less central than in 3.1.

Compensation (approximate)

Levels.fyi (9 Oct 2026), US, "AI Engineer" title: median total compensation US$152K (25th US$110K, 75th US$211K, 90th US$280K); no level breakdown was available. The wider "ML/AI" software-engineer focus has a US median of US$250K, so the labs and big tech pull the average up. Employer postings: Anthropic Applied AI Engineer, Startups (London, hybrid): GBP 225,000-255,000 base, 4+ years of experience as a software engineer, forward-deployed engineer or technical founder (Anthropic job posting, accessed Oct 2026). Anthropic Applied AI, Research Engineer (San Francisco/New York): US$300,000-400,000, 6+ years. These show that at a frontier lab "applied AI" roles are senior-and-customer-facing, not entry-level. Entry: US roughly US$110-150K total at non-lab firms (Levels.fyi 25th percentile to median); UK, Italy and rest of Europe: no reliable AI-engineer-specific data found, so use the SWE medians (Germany EUR 84,890, France EUR 58,419, Italy EUR 39,258, UK GBP 89,673 including all levels) as a ceiling rather than an entry guide. Senior (US): US$211-280K+ (75th to 90th percentile) outside the labs.

Career path

Software engineer / junior AI engineer (0-2) -> AI engineer (2-4) -> Senior AI engineer or tech lead (4-7) -> Staff AI engineer, head of AI at a small company, or engineering manager (7-10). Titles are unstable: "AI engineer", "LLM engineer", "applied AI engineer", "forward-deployed engineer" overlap.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: ML engineer or platform roles, product engineering, a startup (your own or early-stage), forward-deployed/solutions engineering (covered in part 2). After 7-10 years: staff/principal, head of AI or CTO at a small company, founder, AI product leadership.

Tier list of employers

Tier 1: frontier labs' applied teams (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Mistral), top AI-native product companies (coding tools, search, productivity), where applied roles combine product and research. Tier 2: well-funded AI-native startups and big tech product teams adding AI (Microsoft Copilot teams, Google, Meta, Notion, Databricks, Hugging Face-type firms). Tier 3: enterprise AI teams (banks, insurers, pharma), consultancies and IT services (Accenture, Capgemini, Reply, Almawave, TCS) and agencies. Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 and 2 differ from Tier 3 mainly in how quickly the product is evolving and how much engineering autonomy you have; Tier 3 gives domain depth and stability but slower tooling.

How to enter

Degrees and backgrounds. The most open entry point in the branch: computer science is the default, but self-taught or adjacent backgrounds (data analytics, cybersecurity, physics, even business with strong coding) work if you can show shipped software. A master's or PhD is not required; a degree is often preferred but many postings say "or equivalent experience" (Anthropic's posting wording). Programs. Few formal graduate programmes target this title; entries are mainly via software engineering graduate schemes, internships and startups. Hiring calendar. Startups hire year-round; graduate cycles as in 3.1. Interviews. Take-home or live build of a small RAG or agent app, a system-design round on an LLM product (caching, evaluation, safety, cost), coding in Python/TypeScript. Certifications. Vendor courses (cloud AI engineer certificates, deeplearning.ai-type courses) are weak signals; a public project beats a certificate. Portfolio. Three or four shipped applications with live demos, each with a written evaluation (test set, metrics, failure analysis), an open-source contribution to an agent/RAG framework, a fine-tune with before/after numbers. Common mistakes: demos that never face real users, no evaluation, thin "wrapper" apps that any model release makes obsolete, ignoring cost and latency. Alternative routes: software engineers move in by building an internal LLM feature; data scientists add engineering; career changers from analytics start with automation projects. A caution: this is also the most crowded entry route in 2026, because it is the easiest to start, so differentiation matters. Entry difficulty: 3/5

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

The skill is partly commoditised and shifts every few months; a pipeline you built can be replaced by a model feature. Titles are inconsistent, pay varies hugely, and many startups die. Customer and stakeholder expectations of "AI magic" are often unrealistic. Not a fit if you want a deep, stable theoretical specialty or hate ambiguity and quality that cannot be measured with one number.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
