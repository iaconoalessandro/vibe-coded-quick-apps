What you actually do

Corporate strategy: build the annual or multi-year plan, model markets and competitors, assess acquisition targets and new-market entries, prepare board and CEO papers, run strategic initiatives. Business development/partnerships: identify potential partners or customers, structure and negotiate deals, manage joint ventures and alliances. Deliverables: market sizing models, strategy decks, deal memos, business cases, partnership term sheets. Tools: Excel/financial models, PowerPoint, market databases (Statista, Bloomberg-type), CRM for BD.

A typical day and week

"Monday refresh a market-sizing model for the CFO, Tuesday interview a division head for the plan, Wednesday draft the board paper, Thursday call with a potential partner's BD team, Friday review with the head of strategy." Planning and board cycles create peaks.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

  • Hours: 45-55 per week; peak 60-70 near annual planning, board meetings or a live acquisition (practitioner consensus; no published statistic found).
  • Stress: 3/5 (lower than consulting but 4/5 around live deals).
  • Lifestyle: office/hybrid, mostly HQ-based; some travel for partner meetings.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You must persuade executives and run models; BD leans more to negotiation (people 5/5) than strategy.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: Payscale (aggregator, 2026): Corporate Strategy Manager base $96k-$177k, median about $123k, bonus $5k-$73k; early-career corporate strategy manager base $79k-$116k, median about $100k. A 2025 H-1B filing for an Adobe Corporate Development/Strategy Partner in San Francisco shows $140,000 base (single data point). Entry-level graduate figures: no reliable data found, since few firms hire graduates directly into strategy.
  • UK, Italy, Asia, Gulf: no reliable data found.

Career path

Typically 2-5 years in consulting, banking or an operating role first, then analyst/associate in strategy (year 3-5) → strategy manager (6-9) → director/head of strategy (10-15) → chief strategy officer or divisional general manager (15+). Direct graduate hires exist in some large firms (rotational "strategy and corporate development" programmes) but are rare; unverified for specific firms.

Exit opportunities

  • After 2-3 years: commercial roles (marketing, sales operations), product, a business-unit role, MBA; or join the consulting firm you used to buy from.
  • After 7-10 years: general-manager or P&L (profit and loss) ownership of a division, CSO, start-up founder, PE/VC.

Tier list of employers

  • Tier 1: strategy teams of large technology, pharma, energy and financial groups, which hire from top consultancies and have board access (examples not verified by firm this round).
  • Tier 2: mid-size listed corporates, regional champions, large family groups with a strategy function.
  • Tier 3: small firms where "strategy" means the owner plus one analyst, or "business development" is mainly sales.
  • Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

How to enter

  • Degrees: business, economics, engineering, sciences plus a strong quantitative record; master's or MBA common. Strategy is rarely a first job.
  • Programmes: some corporates offer rotational schemes with a strategy rotation (Unilever/Nestlé-type); I did not verify a specific "corporate strategy graduate programme".
  • Calendar: usually through the group's standard graduate cycle (autumn) or lateral hiring from consulting.
  • Assessment: case study, modelling test, executive interviews.
  • Certifications: not important. CFA/other finance credentials matter only for corporate development.
  • Skills: modelling, structured thinking, writing for executives, industry knowledge.
  • Common mistakes: expecting to start in strategy at 22; weak commercial grounding.
  • Non-target routes: join the company in a commercial, finance or operations job and transfer in after 2-4 years; take a rotation in a strategy team; BD roles in start-ups.
  • Entry difficulty: 4/5 as a graduate hire; 3/5 for BD roles in smaller firms.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Small teams mean few seats and slow promotion (forum advice: leaving consulting often means a pay cut or a lateral move; anecdotal). Impact is limited if the executive team ignores the plan. Poor fit: people who need hands-on delivery, or want a clear promotion ladder.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
