What you actually do

You cover a sector (for example European banks, Italian utilities or US software), build and maintain financial models for 15-25 companies, write earnings notes, long reports (initiations) and thematic pieces, set a rating and target price, talk to company management, speak to clients and support the sales and trading desks. Credit research analysts cover bond issuers and focus on the ability to repay: leverage, cash flow, covenants, relative value between bonds, and recommendations for investment grade or high yield. They typically support the trading and sales desks and travel with sales to client meetings (sell-side postings, via search results). Associates (juniors) do the modelling and data gathering. Deliverables: models, notes, call notes, marketing presentations. Compliance and conflict-of-interest rules restrict what analysts can say and do, especially around investment banking deals.

A typical day and week

(Practitioner/secondary: ctacquisitions.com, 2026; undated.) Earnings season (about four two-week windows a year): review the results, update the model, write a short reaction note that goes through compliance, join the call, host client calls in the afternoon. Outside earnings: maintain models, publish one or two notes a week, run expert calls and channel checks, build initiation reports over weeks. Travel for client marketing: about 30% of the year at senior level (same source, unverified).

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Hours: typically 55-65, up to 70-80 in earnings season (ctacquisitions.com, 2026, a secondary source citing aggregated data; an eFinancialCareers opinion piece of 2021 claimed research has the longest hours of the S&T-type roles, also opinion). Stress: 3/5. Pressure peaks at earnings and in annual client rankings (Extel); you must be right in public. Hybrid is more possible than on trading floors, but analysts are on call during results.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Modelling and accounting skills are core; writing and the ability to build a clear investment view matter equally.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: a 2026 secondary guide gives first-year analyst total pay of US$165-250k (2025 benchmarks), year 2-3 US$200-330k, senior associate US$275-550k, VP/sector head US$525k-1.2m, director US$0.9-2.2m, MD US$1.5-8m+ (ctacquisitions.com, 2026; cites recruiter sources; not independently verified). Bonuses depend on rankings, sector revenue and client votes. A different, older educational source (WallStreetMojo, undated) claims research bonuses are much smaller than IB (0-25% of base vs 50-100%); the two do not match, and I could not verify either.
  • UK, Italy, Asia: no reliable data found. Michael Page Italy's 2026 study lists "asset management analyst/manager" at €30-55k RAL (<5 years) with a 15-25% bonus; equity research is not itemised (Michael Page Italy, 2026).
  • Johnson Associates (May 2026) lists "Equities" (the traditional asset management category) up 5%, but gives no separate research figure.

Career path

Research associate (0-2 years) → senior associate (2-4) → analyst with own coverage (about 4-7) → senior analyst/sector head; ranking in the Extel survey drives pay and mobility. A common benchmark: analyst status takes about five years plus an MBA/finance degree or CFA (secondary guide). Research is a smaller industry than sales & trading, and the number of analysts has fallen (see 2.9).

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: the buy side (hedge fund analyst, long-only analyst), corporate development/investor relations, equity capital markets, consulting. After 7-10 years: portfolio manager, senior analyst at a long/short fund, head of research, CIO roles, or corporate IR/strategy. The ctacquisitions.com guide lists exits as: hedge funds about 45%, long-only 15%, corporate roles 10%, PE public-markets 8%, consulting 5%, operating companies 5%, staying in research 12% (its own estimates, unverified); a Euromoney poll reported that senior sell-side credit analysts were also leaving for the buy side. A 2024 academic study found high-quality analysts were more likely to leave the sell-side for the buy-side after MiFID II (as summarised in search results).

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1 (top Extel/institutional rankings, global coverage): J.P. Morgan (top the 2025 All-America research team table by number of positions), Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, Citi; Evercore ISI (No. 1 by weighted analyst rank for four consecutive years to 2025), Bernstein.
  • Tier 2: Barclays, UBS, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas Exane, Jefferies, Wells Fargo, RBC, Mizuho, Redburn Atlantic and strong independents.
  • Tier 3: regional brokers and local research houses (in Italy Equita, Intermonte, Banca Akros, Intesa and Mediobanca's research teams); in credit, the research team may be small or merged into the strategy team.

How to enter

Degrees: finance, accounting, economics, engineering/science for sector specialists. Programmes: research summer internships (smaller than S&T), graduate programmes (e.g., Barclays' 2027 Capital Markets graduate listing mentions research and economic analysis among teams, per a job listing). Skills: building a three-statement model, valuation, writing a stock pitch (one or two prepared), accounting. Licences: in the US, Series 86/87 for research analysts are sponsored by the employer (ctacquisitions.com; unverified). CFA: the charter is not required but passing Level 2 is what most hiring managers look for, according to that guide; CFA Level I pass rates were 39-45% across the four windows from November 2025 to August 2026 (CFA Institute, via the project's earlier research). Common mistakes: writing generic pitches; weak accounting; ignoring industry knowledge; submitting too late. Alternative routes: junior analyst at a regional broker; buy-side research assistant; industry experience (engineer, doctor, lawyer) plus modelling skills for a sector team; equity research associate at an asset manager and later switch. Entry difficulty: 3/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

A shrinking, structurally challenged business: MiFID II cut budgets 20-30% and many banks cut analysts; pay is lower than trading and banking at the same level (US junior pay sources agree it is lower than trading but disagree on how much); the job is constrained by compliance and by banking conflicts; you are judged in public; and much of the work is repetitive earnings-season modelling. Bonuses depend on client votes and rankings. Not a good fit if you want dealmaking, trading or fast promotion; if you dislike writing and detailed models; or if you cannot accept being wrong in public.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
