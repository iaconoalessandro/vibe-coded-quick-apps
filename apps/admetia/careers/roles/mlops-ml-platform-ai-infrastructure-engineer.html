What you actually do

Three layers. (1) MLOps/ML platform: build the pipelines that train, test, register and deploy models (Kubernetes, Airflow or similar orchestrators, MLflow/Weights & Biases-style tracking, CI/CD for models, feature stores, monitoring and alerts). (2) AI infrastructure: operate GPU clusters (scheduling, networking with InfiniBand, checkpointing so a multi-week training run survives a failed node, cost and utilisation). (3) Inference and GPU performance: make trained models serve faster and cheaper by writing or tuning GPU kernels in CUDA or Triton, using continuous batching, KV-caching (storing intermediate attention results so each new token costs less), quantisation and speculative decoding. Anthropic's Engineering Manager, Inference Infrastructure posting (US$405,000-625,000, accessed Oct 2026) gives a clear view: the team builds the control plane that decides where each request is served and how much capacity each model needs, drives throughput, latency, utilisation and cost, and runs on-call, incident response and postmortems.

A typical day and week

Check dashboards and overnight alerts; investigate a throughput regression or a failed node; pair on a change to the scheduler or serving stack; code review; weekly capacity-planning or cost review. A GPU performance engineer might spend days profiling a kernel to raise utilisation by a few percent that translates to millions of dollars. Incident weeks (an outage, a cluster failure) consume everything.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

40-50 hours typical; 50-65 in incidents or major launches; on-call rotations are normal and can mean night pages (practitioner consensus, confirmed in nature by the on-call and incident-response requirements in the Anthropic posting). Stress 4/5 at labs and hyperscalers where outages are costly; 3/5 on a quieter enterprise platform team. Mostly hybrid; data-centre or lab travel is occasional.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. You serve internal customers (researchers, product teams), so communication matters, but the work is systems engineering and performance numbers.

Compensation (approximate)

Specific MLOps/inference pay data is thin. Levels.fyi (9 Oct 2026): NVIDIA ML engineer IC1 US$205K, IC3 US$315K, IC4 US$340K; NVIDIA SWE overall: IC1 US$185K, IC2 US$240K, IC3 US$321K, IC4 US$390K, top level up to US$1.08M, overall median US$310K (2,336 submissions). Employer posting: Anthropic inference-infrastructure engineering manager US$405,000-625,000 (manager level, not entry). UK (ITJobsWatch, 6 months to 9 Oct 2026, advertised base salary): MLOps UK median GBP 80,250 (down 5.6% year on year; 10th percentile GBP 52,025, 25th GBP 62,500, 75th GBP 100,000, 90th GBP 115,000; 404 permanent ads citing MLOps, 230 with quoted salaries); London median GBP 83,500; UK outside London GBP 75,000. This means an UK mid-level MLOps salary of about GBP 62-100K. Italy and the rest of Europe: no MLOps-specific reliable data found; use SWE proxies (Italy EUR 39,258 median; Germany EUR 84,890; France EUR 58,419). Entry in US: roughly the NVIDIA/Meta band (US$185-210K for big tech; lower at non-tech firms); UK entry: no reliable data found; the 10th-25th percentile (GBP 52-62K) is a proxy for junior-to-mid.

Career path

Junior platform/DevOps/ML engineer (0-2) -> MLOps or platform engineer (2-4) -> Senior (4-7) -> Staff / principal (7-12), or manager of a platform team. GPU-performance specialists often follow a more research-engineer-like track.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: generalist backend/SRE (site reliability engineering) roles, ML engineer, cloud architect, specialist inference teams at labs. After 7-10 years: staff/principal infrastructure engineer, engineering director, CTO of an AI infrastructure startup, solutions architect at a chip or cloud vendor.

Tier list of employers

Tier 1: frontier labs and NVIDIA (top tier for inference and performance engineering), Google (TPU/Brain infra), Meta, Microsoft/Azure AI, AWS (Annapurna Labs). Tier 2: neoclouds and inference providers (CoreWeave, Together-AI and Fireworks-type firms), Databricks, Hugging Face, large AI-native startups, AMD and Cerebras. Tier 3: enterprise platform teams (banks, insurers, pharma, telecoms), consultancies and systems integrators, mid-sized software firms. Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 offers the scale (thousands of GPUs) where optimisation matters most; Tier 3 offers MLOps practice with mainstream cloud tools and less exotic scale.

How to enter

Degrees and backgrounds. Computer science, computer engineering, electrical engineering or physics with systems skills. For the low-level GPU/performance end, an earlier project file notes that elite postings often prefer a PhD or demonstrated distributed-training and kernel experience, and that master's graduates can sometimes enter with verifiable open-source work (vLLM, FlashAttention-style projects); I verified that this appears in the earlier file but not in a primary posting. The MLOps end is much more open to software engineers, DevOps/SRE engineers and cloud engineers. Programs. NVIDIA internships (12 weeks minimum, year-round) and New College Grad program; Anthropic Fellows (ML Systems track: projects such as building a CPU simulator for accelerator workloads or adding accelerator backends to open-source projects). Hiring calendar. As in 3.1; infrastructure teams hire year-round. Interviews. Coding, systems design (design a model-serving platform that handles traffic spikes), distributed-systems and Linux questions, for GPU roles questions on memory hierarchy, parallelism and profiling. Certifications. Cloud certificates (AWS, Google Cloud ML Engineer at US$200, Kubernetes CKA/CKAD) are useful for MLOps in enterprises and consultancies, much less for GPU roles. Portfolio. A deployed model-serving system with load tests and monitoring, a contribution to vLLM/Triton/PyTorch, a kernel benchmarked against a baseline, a blog post with profiling numbers. Common mistakes: knowing tool names but not the underlying systems (networking, Linux, containers), treating MLOps as just "run notebooks in the cloud", skipping hands-on GPU experience. Alternative routes: DevOps/SRE/cloud engineers are the largest feeder group; backend engineers join ML platform teams; data engineers move toward feature/data platforms. Entry difficulty: 3/5 (4/5 for GPU kernel and inference-performance roles at labs).

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

On-call and incidents are part of the job; success is invisible when systems work. The hardware, frameworks and best practices change quickly. Infrastructure roles at labs depend on compute budgets, which are tied to the funding cycle. Not a fit if you want to work directly on model behaviour, dislike operations and pager duty, or prefer visible product work.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
