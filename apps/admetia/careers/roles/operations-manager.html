What you actually do

Run the daily output of a plant, warehouse, store cluster, call centre or service operation. Concrete tasks: set shift schedules, resolve absences, monitor OEE (overall equipment effectiveness, the share of planned time a machine makes good product), investigate quality defects, manage health-and-safety incidents, control labour and overtime budgets, and lead improvement projects (Lean, 5S, Kaizen). Deliverables: daily/weekly performance reviews, budget variance reports, root-cause analyses, headcount plans. Tools: ERP/MES (manufacturing execution systems), Excel, Power BI, and standard Lean tools.

A typical day and week

"You run the 7am shift handover for 40 operators, chase a late supplier, sign off an overtime request, and present weekly OEE figures to the plant manager on Thursday." Prospects' production manager profile describes 40-45 hours a week, sometimes starting before 7am, with shift work, weekend on-call and extra hours for deadlines or new systems (Prospects, accessed Oct 2026). BLS notes industrial production managers may be on call for emergencies and some work night or weekend shifts (BLS, May 2025 edition).

Hours, stress and lifestyle

  • Hours: 45-50 per week typical; peak 60+ during launches, shutdowns, peak season or incidents (Prospects gives 40-45 as the baseline; peak is practitioner consensus).
  • Stress: 4/5 (incidents, targets and people problems land on you, often outside office hours).
  • Lifestyle: on-site, early starts, on-call. Low flexibility; no remote work on the shop floor.
  • Travel: moderate between sites; international for multi-site manufacturers.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. You lead and coach shop-floor teams and handle conflict; you also read production, cost and quality data and use basic statistics for process improvement.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: BLS median pay for industrial production managers is $126,060 (May 2025), but BLS notes five years of related experience is typical; for general and operations managers, secondary sites quote $102,950 (May 2024, not verified at bls.gov). New-graduate Amazon Area Managers appear at about $65,000-$75,000 (single aggregator listing, 2026).
  • UK: Prospects: trainee production managers about £24,000-£39,000; after about two years often over £40,000 (average about £44,000); experienced or senior £50,000-£63,000, up to about £78,000. Payscale (aggregator, 2026) gives plant managers an average of £68,784 (base £50k-£113k) and operations directors £68,510. Amazon UK early-career £27k-£45k (Payscale).
  • Italy: no reliable role-level data found. A contractual floor for a metalworking-industry level D1 employee is €1,784.94 gross a month from 1 June 2026 (CCNL metalmeccanici renewal, DottrinaLavoro), paid over 13 or more monthly instalments, so about €23,200+ a year before supplements (my arithmetic); graduates are normally placed above the minimum and plant managers are paid under individual agreements (managerial contracts such as dirigenti). Payscale lists "Project Manager, Operations" at an average of €45,009 in Italy (2026; mixed seniority).
  • Dubai: Payscale (aggregator) puts an experienced operations manager with project-management skills at roughly AED 168k a year (about AED 14,000 a month, my conversion; labels hard to read).
  • Asia: no reliable data for operations managers.

Career path

Shift or team lead / operations supervisor (0-2 years) → operations manager / department manager (3-6 years) → senior operations manager or plant manager (7-12 years) → director of operations, VP operations or COO (12-20 years). Lean credentials help but experience dominates: BLS typically asks for five or more years of related experience for production and top-executive roles.

Exit opportunities

  • After 2-3 years: supply chain planning, continuous improvement or quality roles, procurement, project management, an MBA.
  • After 7-10 years: plant manager or operations director in a smaller firm, consulting operations practice, general manager of a business unit, COO of an SME, entrepreneurship in a family business.

Tier list of employers

  • Tier 1: companies known for operations excellence and training managers: global FMCG (P&G, Unilever, Nestlé, Ferrero), automotive and industrial groups (BMW, Siemens), pharma plants. Strong Lean culture, structured career ladders, international transfers.
  • Tier 2: e-commerce and large logistics (Amazon: managers can lead 300-500 staff at the Operations Manager level per Amazon Pathways page), supermarkets, large retailers, airlines, utilities. High responsibility early, high turnover pressure.
  • Tier 3: SMEs, subcontract manufacturers, small chains. Less training, wider scope, pay depends on the owner.
  • Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

How to enter

  • Degrees: business or management, engineering, sciences, logistics. Unrelated degrees can work with study for professional qualifications; apprenticeships (Level 4 Process Leader; Level 6 Manufacturing Management in the UK) and promotion from technician or team leader are also real routes (Prospects).
  • Programmes: Amazon Area Manager (US) / Graduate Ops Area/Shift Manager (UK); Ferrero International Graduate Program in Lean and Continuous Improvement; BMW AcceleratiON; Rheinmetall trainee; supermarket area manager schemes.
  • Calendar: UK schemes recruit from autumn for the next September; Amazon recruits on a rolling basis with several start dates (July-October 2026 in the UK listing).
  • Assessment: online tests, behavioural interview, site visit or assessment day; Amazon uses situational and leadership-principle interviews (general knowledge; unverified here).
  • Certifications: Lean Six Sigma Green Belt is the common one. Aggregators report a salary gap (Payscale 2025: about $96,000 for Green Belt holders vs $76,000 for White Belt; Lightcast: median advertised $103,000), but this is correlation, not proof, and the sources are weak. Employer-paid training is better than paying yourself. Also useful: NEBOSH/IOSH (health and safety) in the UK, depending on sector.
  • Skills: shop-floor credibility, Excel, root-cause analysis, basic labour law, conflict handling.
  • Common mistakes: never having worked a shift; imposing theory on experienced operators.
  • Non-target routes: start as a team leader, shift supervisor, or quality officer.
  • Entry difficulty: 2/5 for supervisor and team-leader routes (many openings); 3/5 for named schemes such as Amazon or FMCG, where ISE shows heavy competition.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Shift work, early starts, weekend call-outs and accountability for safety incidents are real. Pay at entry is low outside retail and logistics schemes. Poor fit: people who want a pure desk job, predictable evenings, or analytical work without people conflict.

Read this before you rely on the explorer

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
