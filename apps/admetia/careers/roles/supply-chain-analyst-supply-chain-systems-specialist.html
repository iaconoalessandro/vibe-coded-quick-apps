What you actually do

Analysts clean and combine data from ERP, WMS and TMS; build dashboards (inventory, service level, transport cost); run scenarios (for example a tariff impact on landed cost); and recommend actions. Systems specialists configure, test and support planning and execution systems (SAP, Kinaxis, o9, Blue Yonder), manage master data, and work with IT and vendors. Tools: SQL, Excel, Power BI/Tableau, Python or R, and the planning systems. Example: you model a new tariff on a Vietnamese supplier and show a Mexican source is cheaper for 40% of volume.

A typical day and week

Pull data, validate it, build or refresh reports, answer ad hoc requests from planners and managers; workshops with users; test releases for systems. Project work follows a sprint or milestone pattern.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

38-45 hours typical; peaks 50 during go-lives and quarter-end. Stress: 2-3/5 (project go-lives are 4). Hybrid or remote is most common in this family; travel to plants or integrators for rollouts. Consultancy-side systems roles have more travel.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You need data skills (SQL, modelling) and the ability to explain results to non-technical operations managers.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: analysts median US$85,000 across experience levels (ASCM 2025 via Supply Chain 24/7); entry level: no reliable data found (BLS logisticians 10th percentile under US$50,890).
  • UK: coordinators, planners and analysts GBP 27,000-29,000 rising to about GBP 37,000 (Prospects); P&G supply chain graduate GBP 37,000 plus bonus (2024 listing); systems roles at integrators are higher (no reliable data found).
  • Italy: logistics analyst EUR 26,000-32,000 at 0-2 years (Masterin.it, weak); no reliable data for SAP/planning systems specialists.
  • Singapore: supply chain analyst average about S$65,000 (Morgan McKinley 2026).

Career path

Analyst (0-2) → senior analyst / systems specialist (2-5) → team lead / solution architect / supply chain data manager (5-9) → head of supply chain analytics or digital supply chain (9-14). Consulting or vendor roles accelerate pay.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: data analyst/data scientist in other functions, planning or procurement, ERP consultant, product management. After 7-10 years: head of supply chain analytics or digital, supply chain consulting partner track, planning-software vendor solution architect, or operations research (see the data science report).

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1: Large FMCG/manufacturers' analytics centres, Amazon, Kinaxis/o9/SAP/Blue Yonder (vendors), Accenture/Deloitte supply chain technology practices.
  • Tier 2: Mid-size manufacturers and retailers' internal teams, regional integrators, 3PL analytics teams.
  • Tier 3: Small firms using Excel with little formal tooling; broad exposure, little training.

How to enter

Industrial engineering, computer science, data science, mathematics, statistics, economics, supply chain; strong SQL and Excel are the core. Programmes: P&G Talent Development Programme (analysis projects in Warsaw), DHL technology specialist graduate programme (prefers IT degrees but welcomes supply chain interest), Unilever, Kuehne+Nagel digital/analytics tracks (unverified), DE&S Operations Supply Chain scheme. Calendar: autumn to early spring for graduate schemes; analyst jobs hire all year. Certifications: ASCM CPIM/CSCP are optional signals; SAP certifications and vendor-specific training (Kinaxis, o9) matter for systems roles; Lean Six Sigma green belt helps for process analytics. Skills: SQL, Excel, Power BI, Python, supply chain fundamentals, one planning system, clear presentation. Common mistake: dashboards with no recommended action. Alternative routes: planner or logistics coordinator who becomes the team's "Excel person" and moves into analytics; bootcamps plus a supply chain portfolio project. Entry difficulty: 3/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Data is messy and ERP data is often wrong; systems projects overrun; AI tools are automating routine reporting, so pure dashboard builders are less safe than analysts who also own decisions. Not a good fit for people who want frontline operations or negotiation, or who dislike repetitive data cleaning.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
