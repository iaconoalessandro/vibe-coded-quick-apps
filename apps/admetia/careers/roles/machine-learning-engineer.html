What you actually do

You own the path from a model idea to a working service. Concretely: write training and data-processing code in Python with PyTorch (sometimes JAX or TensorFlow); build features and datasets from messy logs; train and fine-tune models; set up evaluation (for a ranking model, offline metrics such as NDCG, then online A/B tests); package the model behind an API or batch job; monitor it for drift (input data changing so the model degrades); and iterate. Deliverables are pull requests, trained model artefacts, evaluation reports, dashboards and design documents. At a bank you might rebuild a fraud-scoring model; at a social app, a ranking model; at a lab, a piece of the training or evaluation pipeline.

A typical day and week

Daily stand-up, then 3-5 hours of coding (data pipeline, training script, debugging a failing job), reading experiment dashboards, and code review. Weekly: a design review or reading group, a planning meeting with a product manager or data scientist, an experiment readout. A representative week: Monday you debug why last night's training run diverged (a bad data shard); Tuesday-Wednesday you implement a new feature pipeline; Thursday you present an offline result (say +1.2% in recall at the same precision) and agree an A/B test; Friday you write the deployment config and a monitoring alert. Long training runs mean periods of waiting and then bursts.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Typical 40-50 hours/week; peaks of 55-70 around launches, or at frontier labs and startups during model pushes (practitioner consensus; press reports of 996-style schedules at some AI startups are cited in section 2.2 and are not an industry average). Stress 3/5 at big tech and enterprises, 4/5 at labs and startups where a training run or launch has high stakes. Remote/hybrid: most big tech is hybrid (3 days in office is common); Anthropic's postings I read ask for an office presence of at least 25% of the time, which shows a flexible policy is possible at even the top labs. On-call is usually light for pure modelling roles but real for roles that own a production model.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. You need to explain trade-offs to product and business people, but the job is judged on code and model quality; you need working statistics, linear algebra and strong programming.

Compensation (approximate)

All figures total compensation (base + bonus + stock) unless stated; Levels.fyi, self-reported, 9 October 2026.

  • US (all-level median for ML engineers): US$280K (25th US$200K, 75th US$386K, 90th US$495K).
  • US entry: Meta E3 US$187K; NVIDIA IC1 US$205K (IC2 US$211K); Google L3 US$208K; OpenAI L2 US$249K (SWE page, includes applied AI titles); Scale AI L3 US$227K; Anthropic entry SWE US$367K.
  • US ~5 years / senior: Google L4 US$312K, L5 US$445K, L6 US$646K; Meta E4 US$314K, E5 US$468K, E6 US$751K; NVIDIA IC3 US$315K, IC4 US$340K; Anthropic Senior US$598K, Staff US$1.13M; OpenAI L4 US$786K, L5 US$960K (private equity, see 2.2).
  • UK: Levels.fyi ML engineer median GBP 103,596 (all levels, total comp). ITJobsWatch (advertised base salaries, 6 months to 9 Oct 2026) gives a UK median of GBP 67,500 (down 20.6% year on year, 92 permanent ads) and a London median of GBP 52,500 (down 41.7%, 46 ads); the small ad sample is volatile and the two sources disagree widely, so read "UK ML engineer roughly GBP 65-105K across the market" as a loose band. The London SWE median on Levels.fyi is GBP 104,526. Entry-level UK ML-specific figure: no reliable data found. The earlier project file found a median of GBP 29,000 across 104 graduate software-engineer ads, which is likely a low-end proxy for non-tech employers.
  • Italy: Levels.fyi ML engineer median EUR 32,471 (all levels, small sample; treat with caution); Italy SWE median EUR 39,258 (25th EUR 31.1K, 75th EUR 49.9K, 90th EUR 65.6K, 384 submissions), Milan SWE EUR 50,465. These suggest an Italian ML engineer entry of roughly EUR 28-40K gross; that range is my inference from the Levels.fyi percentiles, unverified.
  • Rest of Europe (ML engineer medians, Levels.fyi, total comp): Germany EUR 88,465; France EUR 75,949; Zurich CHF 152,282. Mistral SWE: entry EUR 104K total (23 submissions, one of the highest EU entry figures), median EUR 90.9K.
  • Asia/Gulf: Singapore ML engineer median S$141,805 (Levels.fyi); Japan SWE median JPY 8.54M (Tokyo JPY 8.05M, all levels); UAE SWE median AED 337,547 (all levels). Hong Kong, China and India: no reliable data found in this research.
  • The transatlantic gap is large: at entry, a US big-tech ML engineer's total pay is typically 2-4 times an Italian one's, and 1.5-2.5 times a Paris, Berlin or London one's (my arithmetic on the medians above).

Career path

Junior/ML engineer I (0-2 years) -> ML engineer II / mid (2-4) -> Senior ML engineer (4-7) -> Staff / Principal ML engineer (7-12) or engineering manager. Titles vary: Google uses L3-L7, Meta E3-E7, NVIDIA IC1-IC7. Many reach senior in 5-7 years; Staff is not guaranteed. Parallel track: moving toward research engineer, applied scientist or ML tech lead.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: AI/LLM engineer roles at startups, ML platform or infrastructure roles, data science, a move from enterprise to big tech, a master's/PhD (less common), or a startup. After 7-10 years: staff/principal engineer, engineering manager or director of ML, chief-of-staff or founder paths, quant-fund ML roles, venture/technical product roles.

Tier list of employers

Tier 1: frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, xAI, Mistral in Europe) and NVIDIA; the top US quant funds' ML groups (Jane Street, Two Sigma, DE Shaw and IMC in Europe) are paid like this tier but work on trading problems. Tier 2: big tech product ML (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, Netflix, Uber, Spotify), well-funded AI-native firms (Databricks, Hugging Face, Cohere, Perplexity, Scale AI), European champions (Mistral, DeepL, Adyen-style fintechs). Tier 3: bank, insurer and pharma ML teams (JPMorgan, Intesa Sanpaolo, Allianz, Novartis and similar), consultancies (Accenture, Capgemini, Reply, QuantumBlack), industrial and automotive. Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 is distinguished by compute access, problem scale and pay; Tier 3 by stability, domain depth and lower pay.

How to enter

Degrees and backgrounds. Computer science, engineering, maths, physics or statistics. The BLS lists a master's as the typical entry degree for computer and information research scientists (median US$140,300 in May 2025, outlook +22% for 2025-35, 38,600 jobs), which is the research end of the field; for ML engineering, a bachelor's with strong projects works at many companies, a master's helps in Europe and at big tech, and a PhD is needed mainly for research-flavoured roles. Anthropic's Applied AI Research Engineer posting asks for a bachelor's "or equivalent experience" plus 6+ years; a Google DeepMind page lists research engineers as software engineers with deep ML knowledge. Programs and residencies. Anthropic Fellows (ML Systems and RL track): 4 months full-time, stipend US$3,850 / GBP 2,310 per week, about US$15K a month of compute, open to people with US, UK or Canada work authorisation, no visa sponsorship, and in earlier cohorts 25-50% of fellows received a full-time offer (the posting itself). NVIDIA runs year-round internships (minimum 12 weeks) and calls them its main new-graduate pipeline, plus a New College Grad program. Google DeepMind has a Student Researcher programme (details not retrievable). I could not retrieve OpenAI's careers page (access refused). Hiring calendar. Big-tech new-grad cycles in the US run August-November for the following year, internships recruit in autumn; European graduate schemes (Milan, Paris, Zurich) typically open September-January; startups hire year-round. Interviews. Typical loop: recruiter call, a coding screen (Python, data structures), ML fundamentals (bias-variance, loss functions, evaluation), an ML system-design round (design a recommender or fraud system), a project deep-dive, and behavioural; Google DeepMind describes a recruiter call, two or three skills interviews and final meetings with leads. Certifications. The Google Cloud Professional Machine Learning Engineer exam (US$200, two hours, 50-60 questions; Google recommends 3+ years of industry experience) is useful as a signal for enterprise and consulting jobs and mostly ignored at labs and top tech. Portfolio. One or two shipped end-to-end projects (data -> model -> deployed service with monitoring), a reproduced paper, open-source contributions to PyTorch, Hugging Face or similar, and Kaggle placements; the project should show you handled messy data and an honest evaluation. Common mistakes: collecting notebooks and course certificates with no deployed system; using only toy datasets; ignoring software engineering basics (tests, version control); applying to only the top labs. Alternative routes: software engineer -> ML engineer by joining an ML team internally (the most common route in 2026), data scientist -> ML engineer by learning production engineering, career changers via a master's in CS/ML or a hands-on bootcamp-plus-portfolio route (harder), non-target schools via open-source work and strong projects. Entry difficulty: 4/5

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

The entry market is tight (SignalFire and the Stanford "canaries" paper above). Work at labs and startups can be intense and often depends on the whims of one training run or a funding round. Much "ML engineering" at ordinary firms is data cleaning and pipeline maintenance, not modelling, and LLM APIs have removed the need to train models in many use cases. Pay at the top is very uneven, and equity at private labs is illiquid. Not a fit if you dislike programming and debugging, want predictable 9-to-5 certainty at top-lab pay, or want mostly client-facing work.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
