What you actually do

You produce and judge the data models learn from. The work ranges from drawing boxes on images and transcribing audio (micro-tasks), to ranking pairs of model answers (RLHF: reinforcement learning from human feedback, where human preferences steer the model), writing ideal responses, grading chain-of-thought reasoning, red-teaming (trying to make a model fail), and, at the expert end, writing hard problems in medicine, law, finance, coding or mathematics that models should be tested on. Salaried versions exist inside labs and data vendors: data operations, quality assurance, data-pipeline and annotation-tooling roles. The Scale AI job board in October 2026 shows data-adjacent salaried roles such as data acquisition lead (frontier environments), data engineers and field engineers, alongside the "Human Frontier Collective" programme for advanced contributors.

A typical day and week

Contractors pick tasks from a platform queue, follow a long guideline document, complete assignments in a browser tool, and are scored on quality; many work on irregular schedules in the evenings. Salaried staff manage vendor quality, write guidelines, audit samples, build tooling and report accuracy metrics. A week may involve a sudden project start or a sudden stop when a client pauses (see below).

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Contractors set their own hours, often 10-40 per week, with pressure from task quality scores and uncertain volume; salaried data-ops staff typically 40-50 hours. Stress 2/5 for task work in routine conditions but income uncertainty and account-deactivation risk are real stressors; 3/5 for managers under project deadlines. Fully remote for contractors; salaried roles at labs are hybrid or office-based.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 2/5 for individual contributors (mostly solo, written guidelines), 4/5 for vendor/quality managers. Quantitative/technical: 2/5 for general annotation; 3-4/5 for coding, maths or domain-expert tasks. The value is in domain judgement and care, not in programming.

Compensation (approximate)

This is the least regulated segment. DataAnnotation's own site (accessed Oct 2026) advertises US$25-50 per hour for generalists, US$40-125+ for professional/STEM roles (finance, law, medicine, maths) and US$40-150+ for coding, ML and data-science tasks, language specialists US$25-40; these are advertised ranges, pay is tied to output quality, there is no commitment, and testimonials on the page are self-reported and unverified. Reports about Mercor (Wikipedia citing The Verge, New York Magazine and Wired) mention a "stressful work environment" and "declining pay" among contractors. Micro-task platforms such as Remotasks have historically paid far less (the Scale AI Wikipedia page notes critics saying pay can be late or very low); I found no reliable per-hour figure for those. Salaried data-operations staff at US data vendors and labs: Levels.fyi lists Scale AI software engineer entry total of US$227K but that is engineering pay, not annotation. For Italy, Europe, Asia and the Gulf: no reliable data found; in lower-income countries micro-task pay is much lower and lawfulness of contractor status varies.

Career path

Annotator / AI trainer (contract) -> senior or expert reviewer / quality lead -> project manager, data operations specialist or guideline writer -> data-quality manager or evaluation lead at a lab or vendor. For people with a technical background, movement into evaluation engineering, ML data engineering or tooling is a realistic step; from pure micro-task work it is rare.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: data operations or quality roles, evaluation specialist, content/domain jobs, a return to one's original profession (many contractors are doctors, lawyers or academics doing this part-time). After 7-10 years: head of data operations at a vendor, data-quality or evaluation lead, product roles in data platforms, or entry to an engineering path if technical skills were developed meanwhile. Honest note: the base of the pyramid is shrinking as platforms move from volume to experts only, so long-term paths from entry-level labelling are narrow.

Tier list of employers

Tier 1: data teams inside frontier labs and the specialist expert-data vendors with the biggest clients (Scale AI, Surge AI, Mercor), which pay best for experts. Tier 2: Turing, Invisible, Handshake-type expert marketplaces, enterprise data-services arms (TELUS Digital, Appen), and corporate data teams. Tier 3: crowd micro-task platforms and outsourced annotation firms in Southeast Asia, Africa and South Asia (Remotasks, Toloka-type marketplaces). Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 pays more per hour for scarce expertise but is concentrated in a few clients; Tier 3 pays least and is most precarious.

Concrete context on precarity (all from the Wikipedia pages on these firms, secondary): Scale AI faced lawsuits in December 2024 and January 2025 over wage theft, worker misclassification and psychological harm from disturbing content, and Meta bought a 49% non-voting stake for about US$14.8 billion in June 2025. Surge AI (DataAnnotation) reportedly worked with about 1 million annotators in 2025, had revenue reported at US$1.2 billion in 2024 with about 110 employees, and faced a May 2025 class action alleging misclassification of annotators; the platform has drawn criticism for unexplained account cancellations. Mercor reportedly managed 30,000 contractors in October 2025 at a US$10 billion valuation, and a reported March 2026 supply-chain data breach led to lawsuits and a temporary work pause by major clients such as Meta. The takeaway for a student: the industry's headline revenues are large; the people doing the work are mostly independent contractors without benefits or guaranteed hours.

How to enter

Degrees and backgrounds. No fixed degree; platforms screen with qualification tests, and expert tracks want a degree or professional credentials in the subject (law, medicine, finance, maths, coding). Languages other than English are in demand. Programs. No graduate schemes; sign up to platforms, pass assessments, and expect a waiting list or irregular projects. Internal data-ops roles at labs and vendors are advertised on their career pages. Hiring calendar. Continuous; project-driven bursts. Interviews. Skills tests, writing and reasoning tests; for salaried roles, structured interviews and a quality-audit exercise. Certifications. Not relevant. Skills and portfolio. Precision, reading long guidelines, domain expertise, strong writing; for the career-building route, learn Python and basic data analysis, spreadsheets/SQL and evaluation concepts, and document any quality-assurance or tooling work. Common mistakes: treating it as stable income; accepting unclear classification or unpaid training time; ignoring tax and legal status as a contractor; assuming the work leads automatically to an AI engineering job (it usually does not). Alternative routes: professionals and graduate students use it as a side income; people with technical skills move into evaluation or data engineering. Entry difficulty: 2/5 for contract task work (platform screening rejects many, but volume is high); 4/5 for salaried data-operations, evaluation or tooling roles at top labs.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Pay can fall without notice, work can vanish when a client pauses, accounts can be deactivated, and contractors often lack benefits, a job title or career ladder. Content can be disturbing (the Scale lawsuits cite psychological harm). It may build little transferable experience unless you actively add technical skills. Not a fit as a primary career if you need predictable income, health coverage or progression; fine as a short-term, flexible bridge or side income for someone who understands the trade-offs.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
