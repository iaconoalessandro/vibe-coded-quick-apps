What you actually do

You source suppliers, run RFQs and tenders, negotiate price and terms, manage contracts, track supplier performance, and fix problems (late delivery, quality failure, price rise request). At category-manager level you own a spend category and its multi-year strategy and savings target. Tools: ERP purchasing modules (SAP Ariba or similar), spend-cube spreadsheets, e-sourcing platforms, supplier risk databases. Deliverables: savings reports, sourcing strategies, signed contracts. Example: you replace a Chinese packaging supplier hit by a new tariff with a Polish one, re-validate quality samples, and negotiate a 6% saving against a 4% price rise elsewhere.

A typical day and week

Morning: review open purchase orders and supplier messages in ERP; midday: price negotiation call with a supplier and an internal meeting with engineering on specifications; afternoon: update a savings tracker and draft a tender document. Weekly: category review with the business owner, supplier performance meeting, month-end savings reporting. Senior roles add board-level supplier risk reviews.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Typical 37-45 hours a week; peaks 50-55 when a supplier fails or at month/quarter end. UK Prospects gives 37-40 hours mostly office-based, with occasional overseas travel (Prospects, CIPS salary guide 2025 data). Stress: 3/5 (rises to 4 in direct procurement when a plant is about to stop). Hybrid working is common in indirect procurement and office-based sourcing; direct buyers are often at or near plants. Supplier visits and trade shows mean occasional travel, more for category managers who audit suppliers in Asia.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Negotiation, influencing internal stakeholders and reading contracts matter as much as cost modelling; the maths is mostly total-cost-of-ownership, price indices and spreadsheet analysis.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: BLS median for buyers and purchasing agents US$77,710 (May 2025), lowest 10% under US$48,380, highest 10% above US$128,870 (BLS). Entry in practice roughly US$50-65k (inferred from the BLS low end and ASCM; no reliable direct entry figure found). ASCM's 2025 report puts the median buyer at US$69,300 and procurement managers at US$95,000-142,700 (ASCM 2025 salary report via Supply Chain 24/7). BLS purchasing managers' median was US$139,510 (May 2024).
  • UK: entry about GBP 27,000; purchasing officers about GBP 28,000 up to 40,000; procurement/purchasing managers about GBP 54,576, rising to about GBP 76,000; very senior over GBP 122,000 (Prospects quoting the 2025 CIPS Procurement Salary Guide).
  • Italy: no reliable data found for general buyers. Indicative: junior procurement specialist EUR 30-38k and technical buyer EUR 32-40k in renewable energy (Talenty.green 2026 guide; sector-specific and likely above average); a training-site table gives EUR 30-38k for "Responsabile acquisti e logistica" at 0-2 years (Masterin.it, weak source). Mercer data show average first-job pay of EUR 30,500 in Italy (ItaliaOggi, 2024).
  • Singapore: buyer/planner about S$50k, S$75k, S$90k at 5-10, 10-15 and 15+ years (Morgan McKinley 2025 permanent data); procurement managers about S$110-115k at 5-10 years (Morgan McKinley, 2025-26). Entry not given.

Career path

Junior buyer/purchasing assistant (0-2 years) → buyer/procurement specialist (2-4) → senior buyer or category manager (4-8) → procurement manager / head of category (8-12) → director / chief procurement officer (12+). Large companies run rotational programmes; smaller ones may require changing employer to progress (Prospects).

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: planning, supplier quality, project procurement, or procurement at a larger or better-known firm. After 7-10 years: head of procurement, supply chain director, consulting (procurement/cost-reduction practices), private-equity operating partner roles at the senior end, or moving into commercial/contract management.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1 (large, structured, best brand and training): Gartner Supply Chain Top 25 style companies, the 2025 list led by Schneider Electric (No. 1) and NVIDIA (No. 2), with "Masters" Amazon, Apple, P&G and Unilever (Gartner 2025, via Supply Chain 24/7); also large pharma, automotive OEMs (VW, BMW, Stellantis) and defence primes. Sizeable category scope, formal programmes, but narrow slices at entry.
  • Tier 2 (large industrial and consumer groups, strong regional brands): Italian groups such as Barilla, Ferrero, Luxottica/EssilorLuxottica, Pirelli, Leonardo, Fincantieri (employer names in Italy are general knowledge, programmes and procurement hiring not verified), mid-cap manufacturers, big retailers' buying teams.
  • Tier 3 (smaller firms, distributors, public bodies): wider variety, faster responsibility but weaker structured training; public sector (NHS Procurement Graduate Scheme is a two-year paid programme, Prospects).

How to enter

Business, supply chain, economics, engineering (for technical buying), or any degree plus procurement exposure. Programmes: Unilever Future Leaders Programme supply chain track (36 months in the UK, STEM, other science-based or supply chain degrees; UK window ended 12 October 2026 per a careers mirror; Dutch track closed 21 March 2026 and asks for a Dutch WO master's); P&G supply chain graduate roles; NHS Procurement Graduate Scheme; Maersk, DHL and Kuehne+Nagel programmes mostly lean logistics-side rather than procurement (Maersk has run procurement graduate programmes in other markets, e.g. South Africa 2021, not currently verified). Recruiting calendar: UK large-company schemes open from autumn and close roughly November-March; Prospects advises looking from late October; Dutch tracks close around March. Certifications: CIPS (Chartered Institute of Procurement & Supply; MCIPS needs accredited qualification, three years' experience and ethics certificate, CPD of 30 hours a year per Prospects) matters in the UK and for Commonwealth/Gulf employers; CPSM (ISM) is the US equivalent (unverified, BLS does not name it); in Italy and Germany certifications matter much less than a relevant master's. Skills: Excel, a spend-analysis tool, SAP or Ariba familiarity, contract basics, negotiation practice, Power BI. Common mistakes: applying only to big-name schemes; underestimating how important commodity or technical knowledge is in direct buying. Alternative routes: start as purchasing assistant and do a CIPS apprenticeship (levels 3, 4 and 6) or take a first job in customer service or planning at a manufacturer. Entry difficulty: 2/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Cost-saving targets can push you into unpleasant negotiations and conflicts with engineers and sales; you are often the person blamed for late materials; strategic sourcing roles at well-known employers are fewer than operational buying roles; AI and automation are removing routine "tail-spend" purchasing work. Not a good fit for people who dislike confrontation or who want a mostly analytical, solo job.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
