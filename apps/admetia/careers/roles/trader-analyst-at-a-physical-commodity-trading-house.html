What you actually do

Trading houses buy cargoes of crude oil, fuel, LNG, metals or grain, arrange shipping, storage, insurance and financing, hedge price risk with futures and swaps, and sell to refiners, utilities and manufacturers. A trader owns a book (e.g., naphtha in the Mediterranean, copper concentrate, Brent time spreads), negotiates deals, manages margin, and balances logistics. Analysts (junior) build position and P&L reports, curve and price analyses, fundamentals models, and support the trader; a Vitol-style "Commercial Analyst" maintains position and pricing reports, daily P&L, curve integrity and mark-to-market (search-result summary of Vitol's early careers page; unverified). Operators/logistics staff schedule ships and cargoes; risk and finance staff manage credit exposure and trade finance. Tools: Excel, Python, Bloomberg/Platts/Argus price assessments, internal trade systems, shipping platforms.

Desk notes: crude/products desks are the most senior and best paid; LNG/gas/power are growing; metals and agriculture have different supply chains and calendars; majors' trading arms (Shell, bp, TotalEnergies, Equinor) mix asset-backed trading with structured graduate rotations.

A typical day and week

Early morning review of overnight prices (Asia open) and shipping news; a morning meeting with the book; calls to brokers and counterparties through the London/Geneva day; nominations, credit-line checks, hedging; evenings or early mornings for time-zone overlap with Singapore or Houston. Week: bunker/loading windows and price benchmarks drive crunch points. Travel varies: junior analysts travel little, senior traders travel to counterparties, ports and conferences.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Hours: 55-70 per week typical, 70+ in volatile markets (practitioner anecdote; I found no reliable survey). Stress: 4/5 (large positions, 24-hour markets and operational risk). A forum thread said trading houses are much faster-paced than the majors, but that is hearsay. Lifestyle: office-based in Geneva, London, Singapore, Houston, Dubai; relocation between hubs is common; Singapore and Dubai attract people for lifestyle and tax (Dubai: zero personal income tax, general knowledge).

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Trading is relational (counterparties, brokers, shipping, partners) and entrepreneurial, with solid numeracy and commercial judgement.

Compensation (approximate)

  • Geneva/Switzerland: a recruiter (Upreer, 2026, method undisclosed) says graduate traders start at CHF 90-130k and established traders earn CHF 150-300k base with bonuses that can double it; ERI SalaryExpert estimates the average Geneva securities and commodities trader at CHF 103,862 base plus CHF 16,878 bonus (updated October 2025). An older recruiter table (2018, outside the two-year window) put analysts at CHF 70-95k. I found no verified graduate survey.
  • London: Upreer gives approximately £70-100k at entry level (same caveat).
  • Singapore: Payscale (2026) shows a SGD 87k median base for commodities traders (25th-75th percentile SGD 63-112k; bonus S$9-48k).
  • Ownership pay is the real money: Reuters reported (2024) that most traders at Vitol and Trafigura are paid a percentage of their book's profit and that partners receive large dividends; Vitol paid US$5.9bn to roughly 600 employee-owners for 2025 on US$4.2bn profit (Bloomberg in The Star, 27 July 2026), Vitol paid US$6.5bn to employee-owners in 2022 (about 450 owners, Bloomberg via Swissinfo), and Trafigura has more than 1,400 employee shareholders. These are averages for a small group of partners and cannot be read as graduate pay. Majors pay less cash and more deferred stock, and struggle to match independents' bonuses (Reuters 2024, as summarised in search results). Hedge funds also hired oil traders with seven-figure signing bonuses (Bloomberg, 2022; outside the two-year window).
  • US, Italy, Dubai: no reliable data found. Eni's paid master's in Milan (Politecnico di Milano/Torino) pays €1,500 gross per month for STEM graduates (Eni, earlier project research).

Career path

Typical at independents: junior analyst/trading assistant (years 1-3) → trader or "senior analyst" (3-6) → trader with own book (5-10) → senior trader/desk head → partner (in some houses, with equity). Graduate programmes: Trafigura offers two-year Commercial (towards junior trading), Development (operations, deals desk, trade finance) and Technical (trading IT, data science, research analysis) tracks in Geneva, Singapore, Houston, Calgary, Montevideo and Shanghai; TotalEnergies offers a two-year programme with four six-month rotations; Gunvor/Centalion has a graduate programme with rotations through trading, risk, operations and analytics (company pages via earlier project research). Equinor has a two-year market analysis and trading graduate programme (search summary). Those programmes lead toward junior trading roles, not guaranteed trader seats; no house publishes how many graduates become traders.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: move to another house, a major's trading arm, a bank's commodities desk, a commodity-focused hedge fund, a producer/refiner commercial role, or risk/finance roles. After 7-10 years: head a book or desk at a trading house, run a commodity fund, move to a producer or utility as commercial director, or start a trading business. Many exits are into trade finance, shipping and risk rather than trading seats.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1 (largest independents, highest upside): Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore, Mercuria, Gunvor/Centalion (the "big five" by trading volume and reputation); in agriculture Cargill, ADM, Louis Dreyfus, Bunge, COFCO.
  • Tier 2: energy majors' trading arms (Shell, bp, TotalEnergies, Equinor, ExxonMobil, Eni) with structured graduate programmes and better predictability; strong regional houses (Hartree, Freepoint, Castleton, Concord, Sucden, Aurubis in metals); bank commodities desks at a few banks (J.P. Morgan, Goldman, Macquarie).
  • Tier 3: smaller independents, utilities' trading units (Uniper, Axpo, EDF Trading, Enel), regional brokers; they offer less pay but sometimes more breadth early.

How to enter

Backgrounds: commodities/energy economics, finance, engineering, logistics and supply chain, maths/data science. Graduate programmes are small and annual: Trafigura's page on 30 September 2026 showed most 2027 intakes closed, with the China programme open to 23 October 2026; Gunvor's programme asks for a 40-minute maths/logic/Python test (company page, per earlier research). The University of Geneva MSc in Commodity Trading is effectively a hiring funnel: in 2024, 170 applied, 100 were shortlisted and 33 were placed in paid company traineeships (UNIGE, 2024, via earlier research). Common mistakes: expecting a trading seat at graduation; ignoring operations/finance entry; applying to one house only; unfamiliarity with how oil/gas/metals markets work. Certifications: none required; shipping/trade finance courses and industry reading (Platts, Argus, Energy Intelligence, FT Commodities) help. Alternative entry: operations, trade finance, risk or analytics roles, then move to the desk; junior roles at brokers; energy-major graduate programmes; experience in a physical industry (shipping, refining, mining) then lateral. Entry difficulty: 4/5 (small intakes; no published acceptance rates; heavy referral/hiring through headhunters for experienced staff, Upreer 2026).

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Results are cyclical (Vitol's 2025 profit halved; Gunvor's fell 85%, then 2026 profits jumped with the Hormuz-related disruption, per Reuters/Kitco), so bonuses swing; the culture can be closed and politically complex; reputational and legal controversies exist (e.g., fines/pleas by Glencore, Trafigura and Vitol, per earlier research); you may be relocated, and hubs are small, expensive cities; opaque pay; and the best money is for partners. Not a good fit if you want transparency, regular hours, or a global brand name; if you dislike negotiation; or if you are uncomfortable working near fossil-fuel industries.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
