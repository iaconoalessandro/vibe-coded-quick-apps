What you actually do

Transaction services (TS) / financial due diligence (FDD): you test a company's numbers for a buyer (often private equity), a seller (vendor due diligence) or a lender. Typical tasks: reconciling management accounts to statutory accounts, adjusting EBITDA to "quality of earnings" (removing one-offs, normalising costs), analysing revenue by customer and product, working capital seasonality and "normal" level, net debt and "debt-like items" (pensions, leases, deferred payments), cash conversion, and the reasonableness of the business plan. You interview management, work in data rooms, produce a written report and workbooks, and present to the client. In the US, work is heavily historical; in Europe teams also review forecasts and do more modelling (Mergers & Inquisitions, article with 2022 figures). Valuation: purchase price allocation after an acquisition, impairment tests, valuations for tax, disputes and financial reporting. Corporate finance advisory: sell-side and buy-side advice for mid-market deals (Big 4 CF teams and independent boutiques), similar to a mid-market bank's M&A work but with accounting-led diligence and fixed-fee/time-based economics.

A typical day and week

FDD: a deal timeline drives the pace: 2-6 weeks of intense diligence, with daily calls with the deal team and the client, then a report deadline, then quieter weeks or the next project. Team structure is a pyramid (analyst/associate, senior associate, manager, director, partner). Weeks are more predictable than banking outside report deadlines; late nights cluster around report delivery and signing (Finbound UK guide, 27 June 2026, no numbers). Valuation teams have steadier schedules with peaks at year-end reporting. Client site visits and some travel, particularly for cross-border deals (KPMG describes a high volume of cross-border deals and looks for second European languages in its Deal Advisory pages, older 2019 PDF). Hybrid working is normal.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Hours: typical 50-60 per week, peaks of 70-80 during report deadlines (Mergers & Inquisitions suggests 50-60 hours with occasional late nights and weekends; no recent survey found). Stress: 3/5 (regular deadlines, steady pressure). Lifestyle: noticeably more predictable than banking, more training and structure, hybrid and part-time options. The "Big 4 busy season" is audit-specific; TS follows deal flow.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5 (accounting is the main skill: strong understanding of financial statements, Excel, data analysis; valuation teams use more modelling).

Compensation (approximate)

United Kingdom (London): Big 4 graduate starting salaries around £30,000-36,000, with KPMG typically about £34,500, EY £30,000-36,000, PwC and Deloitte £30,000-35,000 (City AM, 29 September 2026; firm-wide, not TS-specific); Grant Thornton will pay £40,000 to London graduates from 2027 (same article). I found no published TS-specific graduate pay. Mid-tier firms pay about 10-20% less than the Big 4 (search summary, unverified). Bonuses are modest. United States: Mergers & Inquisitions (figures as of 2022, a dated source) quotes TS associate base US$80,000-100,000 (total US$100,000-130,000), senior associate US$115,000-145,000 base, manager US$150,000-190,000 base, and director US$220,000-300,000 base (total US$290,000-390,000); a Boston advisory-firm posting listed US$100,000-130,000 for a transaction services senior associate (job-board listing, undated). Italy: Big 4 entry-level gross pay of EUR 24,000-28,000 (Trend Online, 12 June 2024; across Big 4 service lines, no TS-specific figure); Indeed shows a Deloitte junior finance and accounting analyst in Milan at EUR 25,000-28,000; internships pay a reimbursement of EUR 400-800 a month. Continental Europe, HK, Singapore, Dubai, Tokyo: no reliable TS-specific data found. A Singapore Michael Page 2026 guide lists an M&A Associate band of S$105,000-175,000 (corporate finance role, snippet-level). Seniors: US director to partner figures above; UK partner pay: no reliable data found.

Career path

Analyst/associate (2-3 years; professional qualification study, for example ACA/ACCA in the UK or CPA in the US, often alongside) → senior associate/senior consultant → manager (4-6 years) → senior manager/director (7-10 years) → partner (12+ years, competitive). Lateral exits are common at manager level.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: investment banking at middle-market or boutique banks (moderately plausible; the Mergers & Inquisitions article says it is possible at boutique and middle-market banks in the first one to three years and harder after four to six), corporate development, FP&A, other advisory roles, transaction advisory at PE-backed businesses. After 7-10 years: private equity in mid-market funds (moderate to strong plausibility per the Finbound guide, harder for mega-funds), corporate development head or CFO, or staying for partner. Evidence base for exits is career guides and coaching sites, not placement data; the claims that "TS is a springboard to IB" and "TS to PE is possible but harder than IB to PE" are consistent across them.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1: Big 4 transaction/deal teams, ordered by practitioners variously: PwC Deals, EY-Parthenon Transaction Advisory (TAS), KPMG Deal Advisory, Deloitte Financial Advisory. Differences between them are small and vary by country; the major point is brand, deal flow with PE, and training. Also top corporate-finance boutiques and valuation specialists: Kroll (valuation), Houlihan Lokey's valuation arm, Duff & Phelps (now Kroll), Alvarez & Marsal (transaction advisory and restructuring), FTI Consulting, Lazard's financial advisory arm.
  • Tier 2: Mid-tier accounting firms with strong deal teams: Grant Thornton, BDO, RSM, Mazars/Forvis Mazars, PKF (BDO and PKF list deal advisory graduate roles), Baker Tilly, plus independent corporate-finance houses such as Clearwater International and Cavendish in the UK (unverified list; Italian mid-market advisers are covered in section 2.6).
  • Tier 3: Regional accountancies, local valuation shops and small corporate-finance boutiques; narrower deal flow, less brand recognition, more generalist work.

How to enter

Big 4 graduate schemes recruit autumn to winter for the following autumn start, with spring/summer internships and industrial placements as pipeline stages (Finbound UK guide, 27 June 2026). KPMG's Deal Advisory UK offers streams that include Transaction Services, Corporate Finance and Restructuring (KPMG careers pages; older programme PDFs). PwC Deals trainees and graduates are recruited in many countries (for example, a Finnish PwC FDD team of about 40 people and a 2-6 month trainee programme). In Italy the entry is via stage (internship) at Big 4 deal advisory practices followed by a fixed-term/permanent contract; EUR 400-800 monthly internship reimbursements are common (Trend Online, 2024). Degrees: accounting, finance, economics and business are best, but engineering, maths or law with strong Excel and accounting skills are accepted; many graduates start in audit and then move into TS after 12-24 months (an internal route that takes 2-3 years, per M&I). Qualification: ICAEW ACA or ACCA (UK), CPA (US), dottore commercialista/revisore legale (Italy, my understanding, unverified); a qualification is not mandatory for TS in all firms but strengthens your progression. Skills: accounting and Excel, data analysis (increasingly Alteryx/Power BI), concise report writing, commercial curiosity, and interviewing management. Mistakes: applying only to banks and ignoring the Big 4 as a route; assuming TS is "easy IB"; not understanding what a diligence report is. Alternative routes: start in audit and rotate; join a mid-tier firm and lateral to a Big 4 deals team; or join a corporate-finance boutique through direct graduate or internship applications. Entry difficulty: 3/5 (large intakes and open to many degrees, but a competitive assessment process and a push for strong academics and internships; 4/5 for EY-Parthenon or top boutique roles).

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Pay is materially lower than in banking at equivalent seniority (a forum poster estimates a 1.5-2x gap; anecdotal); the work is often retrospective ("what happened"), not strategic; you are an adviser to an adviser, as TS is often brought in late in a deal and can be dropped from the timeline; firms are pyramid-shaped and promotion to partner is highly competitive; conflicts of interest (audit independence rules) can restrict which clients you serve. Not a good fit if you want the prestige and pay of top investment banking and are unwilling to take the extra step later, or if you dislike detailed accounting work and reports. Better fit if you want deal exposure with more predictable hours and a stronger accounting grounding.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
