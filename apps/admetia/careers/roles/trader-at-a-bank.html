What you actually do

You price risk for clients and manage the book (the desk's inventory of positions). When a salesperson says a pension fund wants to sell GBP 200m of 10-year gilts, you decide the price, take the risk, then lay it off in the market or hold it. As a junior you build the daily P&L explanation, update pricing tools in Excel/Python, keep an eye on limits, execute small flows and shadow the senior trader; as a senior you set the quotes, size positions and deal with risk managers and regulators. Tools: Bloomberg terminals, bank pricing systems, trading platforms (e.g., MarketAxess, Tradeweb, Bloomberg), voice and chat; coding (Python, SQL, sometimes C++ or kdb+) is increasingly expected.

Desk notes (what changes by desk):

  • Rates: high-volume, quantitative, driven by central banks and data releases; vanilla desks are electronic; options and exotics need strong maths. Typical start 6:30-7:00am London time.
  • Credit (IG/HY/distressed/securitised): inventory-heavy and more analytical. IG is moving to electronic; HY and distressed need credit judgement and legal reading; securitised products need cash-flow modelling. Slower tempo but liquidity can vanish in stress.
  • FX: the most electronic; spot traders manage a flow book with huge volume and tiny margins; options traders work on volatility. The day is long because FX runs around the clock; Asia-Pacific and London hubs start early.
  • Equities: cash equities trading is increasingly algorithmic; derivatives traders ("vol traders") are the most quant; delta one traders work on baskets and ETFs. In practitioner opinion London cash equities has among the better hours (eFinancialCareers opinion piece, 10 June 2021).
  • Commodities: fewer bank seats; hours follow the energy/metal exchanges; more hedging-oriented and weather/geopolitics-driven.

A typical day and week

(Practitioner consensus, not a survey.) 6:30-7:00am: read overnight news, check positions, prepare morning meeting; 7:30: desk meeting, view of the day; 8:00-4:30: quoting, hedging, talking to sales; after close: reconcile P&L, review risk, plan tomorrow. Weeks are structured around data releases (US payrolls, inflation), central-bank meetings, auctions, earnings and month/quarter-end. Evenings are generally free except in volatile markets; client entertainment is more a sales task.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Hours: typically 50-65 per week, peak 70+ in crisis weeks or for juniors; an eFinancialCareers writer put S&T at 50-70 with a median nearer 50 (opinion piece, 10 June 2021) and another (15 May 2026) described the week as arriving a few hours before the open and staying a couple of hours after, roughly stable through the year with peaks around earnings and market shocks. The 2021 writer also claimed hours are longest in Hong Kong, then London, then New York (opinion). Stress: 4/5 (5 in volatile markets: you are the person holding the risk and your P&L is posted daily). Lifestyle: office-based; the desk must sit together for compliance (recording of lines) so remote work is rare; travel is limited (some conferences and client visits); weekend work is rare except in crises.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. Traders talk constantly (to sales, other traders, brokers) but the core skill is probabilistic thinking, mental arithmetic under pressure and risk discipline; coding is a growing differentiator.

Compensation (approximate)

All figures are dated and from recruiters or press, not payroll data.

  • US (New York), 2025: Selby Jennings' NYC guide (2025, based on placement data) shows trading base salary of US$125k for analysts, US$175k associates, US$200-275k VPs, US$300-350k directors, US$400-600k MDs. Total pay: analyst US$175-250k (equity derivatives up to US$275k); associate US$250-425k (credit US$250-425k, rates US$250-450k, FX US$250-400k, equity derivatives US$250-450k); VP US$400-850k; director US$600k-1.2m; MD US$800k to around US$2.5-2.75m (the PDF prints "2.5k" and "2.75MM"; I read the "2.5k" as 2.5MM). A different source, Wall Street Prep (updated December 2023, so older), shows a first-year analyst on US$85k base with US$50-75k bonus (all-in US$135-160k) and associates at US$150k base with US$90-130k bonus. The two disagree by roughly US$40-50k at analyst level; the difference reflects the year, the firms covered and the way the numbers were gathered (recruiter placements vs an educational site).
  • UK (London): I found no recruiter guide that I could open for London S&T pay. A 2025 London/Europe guide seen only as a search-result summary (a page that returned 404 when opened; treat as unverified) gives first-year analyst base of about £60-65k, bonus 25-60% and total of £75-105k, with bulge-bracket analysts at £90-140k total and VPs £280-400k, and says European banks pay 10-15% less. The closest verified London sell-side bands are Selby Jennings' 2024 quant guide (so for quant-heavy desks, not typical traders): Associate/AVP base £75-120k, total £85-150k; VP base £115-180k, total £135-250k; Director £240-450k total; MD base £250-400k, total £600k-1.2m. Disclosure data from Pillar III filings for 2025 show London "material risk takers" (the most senior risk-taking staff, including traders and bankers) averaged US$1.46m (Citi) to US$2.2m (Morgan Stanley) total pay (eFinancialCareers, 8 July 2026); that is a senior-only average.
  • Hong Kong/Singapore: Selby Jennings' 2024 guide (quant-heavy): sell-side Associate base US$85-100k, total US$120-140k; VP base US$150-170k, total US$220-250k; MD total US$550-700k. For banks' IB analysts in 2024, Bloomberg Intelligence/Michael Page data show Hong Kong analysts at about US$92k vs US$63k in Singapore (investment banking, not S&T). I found no reliable S&T-specific figure for Tokyo or Dubai.
  • Italy (Milan): no reliable data found for trader pay. The only formal proxy is Michael Page Italy's 2026 salary study for "investment banking, capital markets, private equity": M&A analyst/associate <5 years €30-55k gross annual salary (RAL), bonus 50-100% of base; asset management analyst €30-55k, bonus 15-25% (Michael Page Italy, 2026). Because the EU bonus cap applies and Milan desks are smaller, expect lower total pay than London for the same title (inference).
  • Bonus outlook: Johnson Associates projected 2026 year-end incentives for equity sales and trading at +10-15% and fixed income sales and trading at +5-10% (7 May 2026).

Career path

US-style ladder: Analyst (years 1-3) → Associate (years 3-6) → Vice President (about year 6-9) → Director/Executive Director → Managing Director, with promotion tied to P&L and risk ownership (practitioner consensus). Many bank trainees rotate through sales, trading and structuring during the internship and are placed on a desk at graduation. Only a minority of juniors become head-of-desk traders; many move into sales or structuring, or out. Bonuses are discretionary and depend on bank, desk and year performance, and a portion at senior level is deferred (typical).

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: another bank desk, a hedge fund analyst or junior-trader seat, an electronic market maker (uncommon from voice desks), buy-side trading, MBA or a master's in finance, a move into structuring, risk or strategy. After 7-10 years: portfolio manager at a hedge fund (eFinancialCareers found that about one-third of 15 star traders identified in 2016 had moved to hedge funds as senior PMs or unit heads by early 2026 (23 February 2026); a sample of 15 is anecdotal), head of desk, asset management, commodity house, fintech/electronic trading firm, or exit to corporate treasury.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Tier 1 (global franchises, the largest markets revenues): Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Morgan Stanley, Citi, Bank of America (the five US "bulge brackets"). Coalition's 12-bank index (2025) also includes Barclays, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, HSBC, Société Générale, UBS and Wells Fargo. Differences: scale, balance sheet, technology investment, the strongest client franchises, higher pay.
  • Tier 2 (strong in specific products or regions): Barclays (rates/credit/FX), Deutsche Bank (rates, FX, flow), UBS, BNP Paribas (equity derivatives, rates), Société Générale (equity derivatives, structured products), Nomura (Japan/Asia), HSBC (Asia, FX), Jefferies, RBC, Macquarie (commodities), Mizuho/MUFG/SMBC.
  • Tier 3 (regional, smaller or boutique): national champions and local players, e.g., in Italy Intesa Sanpaolo IMI CIB, UniCredit, Mediobanca (now within the Monte dei Paschi group) and Banca Akros (Banco BPM group), Equita and Intermonte in equities brokerage; mid-sized banks in Germany, Spain and Asia. Differences: narrower products, fewer graduate seats, lower pay, but often more responsibility early and strong local clients.

How to enter

  • Degrees/backgrounds: maths-heavy degrees (economics, finance, engineering, maths, physics, computer science) are favoured; recruiters in London and New York also take any strong student who can show market interest and numeracy. Nothing replaces strong exam results.
  • Internships and programmes: the pipeline is the summer analyst programme, converted to a full-time offer. Morgan Stanley's Sales & Trading Summer Analyst Programme in London ran 10 weeks starting in June and rotated across institutional equities and fixed income, sales, trading and structuring; the 2027 London posting went live around 7 September 2026 (job-board listings, unverified). J.P. Morgan's 2027 "Markets" summer analyst track lists Sales, Trading, Structuring & Origination and Digital Markets (job boards, unverified); several J.P. Morgan 2027 Corporate & Investment Bank listings show deadlines around 1 November 2026, but the Markets deadline itself was not confirmed. Barclays' 2027 London graduate roles (Banking and Capital Markets) closed on 25 September 2026 according to Gradcracker; a third-party guide says Markets roles may stay open into January 2027 (unverified; Barclays page not confirmed). Goldman took about 2,500 interns in 2026 with a sub-1% acceptance rate for the third year (Fortune, 8 June 2026, via earlier project research). Asia: Morgan Stanley Japan's 2027 summer analyst deadlines were 16 August and 27 September 2026; Nomura's Asia Global Markets internship applications are expected to open around January 2027 (third-party guide, unverified). Italy: Intesa Sanpaolo's "I'M IN" international graduate programme has posted credit-trader and rates-solutions seats in Milan (mid-2026, listings only; details not readable).
  • Recruiting calendar (general pattern): apply in the autumn (September-November) of the year before the summer internship (UK/Europe), or in the August-October window of junior year (US); spring weeks and early-insight programmes for first-years in the UK; off-cycle internships exist but are fewer for markets.
  • Certifications: none required to start. Licences (e.g., US FINRA Series 7/63 or 57, UK FCA certification) are sponsored by the employer (general knowledge, unverified). CFA is of marginal value for trading; it is more useful for research and asset management (CFA Level I pass rates were 39-45% across 2025-26 windows, per the project's earlier research). Skills: Excel, Python, market knowledge (follow rates/FX daily), probability and mental arithmetic, a stock/market pitch, a view on what moved yesterday. Practise brainteasers and market-sense questions.
  • Common mistakes: applying only to one product; not following markets; pitching "I like adrenaline" without a view; underestimating the numeracy test; applying late; ignoring Tier 2 and local banks.
  • Alternative routes: lateral entry from middle office, trade support, operations, risk or control; a one-year finance master's (LSE, Imperial, Bocconi, HEC and similar) to earn an interview at off-cycle or graduate programmes; starting at a smaller bank or brokerage or in a regional hub and moving to a bigger bank; MFE/maths master's for derivatives. For non-target students, networking and demonstrable market projects (a trade journal, a tracked paper portfolio) matter more. Entry difficulty: 4/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

P&L is visible every day and stressful; bonuses fall in bad years (and are discretionary); a quiet year can mean a desk closes (headcount in FICC at GSI fell 6% in 2025 despite higher revenue); electronic trading and AI will keep pressuring junior voice-trading seats; the work is repetitive on slow days; the first years are mostly support work; pay at Tier 3 and in Italy is much lower; and trading careers can end suddenly. Not a good fit if you dislike public mistakes, early hours or being measured in dollars daily, if you are uncomfortable with maths under time pressure, or if you want predictable job security.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
