What you actually do

Four sub-jobs. (a) Public policy: drafting or analysing legislation, consultations, standards input and briefings; at the EU AI Office you support the Act's rules for general-purpose models (units for regulation and compliance, AI safety, and innovation policy). (b) Lab and company policy: OpenAI's "Model Policy Manager" roles, "Senior Product Policy Lead, Regulation" and "Research Program Manager, Governance"; Anthropic's Policy Design Manager (owns the usage policy, builds threat models) and Product Policy Manager; Google DeepMind's "Frontier Public Affairs Lead"; Cohere's public policy roles. (c) Corporate responsible AI / compliance: inventorying AI systems, risk-classifying them under the EU AI Act, writing governance policies, running impact assessments with legal, privacy, security and data teams; Citi's AI Business Risk and Control Director (October 2026) holds approval authority for AI use cases and aligns the framework to the EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF. (d) Think-tank or consulting research: reports, workshops, client advice.

A typical day and week

Reading regulation and draft guidance; drafting a policy paper or compliance checklist; meetings with legal, engineering and business teams; stakeholder calls with regulators or civil society; in the lab setting, reviewing a product launch against policy. Week-ending output: memo, briefing or risk assessment.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Typical 40-50 hours in regulators, think tanks and corporate roles; 50-60 at labs and law/consulting firms; peaks of 60+ around regulation deadlines, consultations and legislative sessions (estimate). Stress 3/5 (4/5 at law firms, labs during incidents, and policy shops during legislative crunch). Hybrid is common outside labs. Travel: moderate (Brussels, DC, Geneva, summits).

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 2/5 (rising to 3/5 for technical policy and evaluation-oriented roles). The job is persuasion, writing and interpretation; technical policy roles require reading model evaluations and understanding ML basics.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US labs and AI companies, base ranges (October 2026): OpenAI Model Policy Manager $207,000-$335,000, Senior Product Policy Lead, Regulation $302,000-$335,000, Research Program Manager, Governance $239,000-$328,000; Anthropic Policy Design Manager and Product Policy Manager $245,000-$285,000; Google DeepMind Frontier Public Affairs Lead $221,000-$307,000 (mirror); Cohere Global Public Policy Manager $150,000-$240,000 and Lead, US Government Affairs $175,000-$330,000; xAI Senior Security Engineer, GRC & AI Governance $152,000-$258,000.
  • US big tech (mirrors of 2026 Google postings): AI Policy Manager II, Washington DC $182,000-$248,000 base (7 years' tech-policy experience); Responsible AI Program Manager, Google Public Sector $165,000-$239,000 (needs Top Secret/SCI clearance).
  • US finance: Citi AI Business Risk and Control Director (Getzville, New York or Tampa; posted 5 October 2026) $200,000-$300,000 base; M&T Bank model-risk analyst validating AI models (mirror) $103,000-$171,600. Junior compliance pay at banks sits below lab bands, but senior risk-and-control jobs overlap with them.
  • US fellowships and government: Horizon Fellowship base salaries for the 2027 cohort are $78,000 (junior), $130,000 (fellow) and $190,000+ (senior), plus a $17,000 a year benefits stipend (Horizon page); placements in executive-branch offices, Congress, national labs and think tanks. NIST AI roles in September 2026 ran $85,447-$197,200 (see 3.3). Think-tank pay: no reliable data found.
  • EU: AD5 basic salary EUR 6,152.64 a month from 1 July 2025, net about EUR 4,859 without allowances (AMLA page; AD6 starts at EUR 6,961). Contract agents at function group IV (the 2026 AI Office legal and technology call) earn a basic EUR 4,449-5,034. Blue Book traineeship grant EUR 1,538.16 a month in Brussels. AD5 competition EPSO/AD/427/26 drew 174,727 applicants for 1,490 places (EPSO, 2026). The AI Office recruits through expression-of-interest calls and lists more than 125 staff.
  • UK: Barclay Simpson's 2026 AI governance report (recruiter; central London base): analyst GBP 60,000-70,000; senior analyst 70,000-100,000; manager 100,000-130,000; lead/head of 130,000-150,000; AI counsel 100,000-150,000; chief AI officer/director 150,000-250,000; UK-wide about GBP 5,000-10,000 lower. Other signals (search summaries): a London Head of AI Governance for a qualified lawyer at recruiter Taylor Root GBP 150,000-180,000; a Ministry of Justice Head of AI Governance advert (2025) GBP 71,381-80,419 (London 75,674-85,257). UK AISI technical pay is in 3.3.
  • Italy: no reliable data found for AI governance or compliance salaries. The Hays Italia Salary Guide 2026 exists but I did not read its tables; Il Sole 24 Ore reports rising demand. Context only: average private-sector pay in Lombardy about EUR 35,137 gross (JP Salary Outlook 2026).
  • Switzerland, Singapore, Gulf, other Asia: no reliable data found.
  • Corporate responsible AI / compliance salaries (global): the IAPP report I read contains no salary figures.

Career path

Policy analyst/assistant, compliance analyst, or trainee/fellow (0-2 years) -> senior analyst/manager (3-6) -> head of AI policy/governance, director (7-12) -> chief AI officer/responsible AI lead or senior official. In law: trainee/associate -> senior associate -> partner (10+). The IAPP report finds organizations usually build governance teams gradually, starting with existing staff and then hiring senior managers, and often hire managers with prior digital governance experience such as privacy.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: privacy, compliance and risk roles; tech-policy jobs at platforms; consultancy; law firm. After 7-10 years: chief privacy/AI officer, head of policy at a lab or regulator, partner at a firm, senior official, NGO leadership.

Tier list of employers (alphabetical within each tier)

  • Tier 1: Frontier-lab and big-tech frontier policy teams (Anthropic, Google DeepMind, OpenAI), the European Commission's AI Office, the UK AI Security Institute and DSIT, US White House/Commerce/CAISI, leading research centres (GovAI, and similar), top Brussels and Washington think tanks.
  • Tier 2: Big-tech public policy and responsible-AI teams (Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft), national regulators and data-protection authorities (Italy's Garante, AgID, ACN; general knowledge), international organisations (OECD, UN, Council of Europe), law-firm AI practices, Big Four and consultancy AI-governance practices, global banks' responsible-AI leadership roles (Citi, for example).
  • Tier 3: Corporate compliance and risk teams in banks, pharma and insurance; startups building governance tooling; national associations and standards committees.

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

How to enter

Degrees: law, public policy, international relations, political science, economics, computer science (technical policy). Lab policy postings ask for a bachelor's or equivalent plus domain experience (Anthropic's do; Google's AI policy role wants 7 years of tech-policy work). 80,000 Hours says policy master's degrees are particularly useful in Washington and law degrees and relevant PhDs help; executive-office roles (OSTP, OMB, NSC) are among the hardest to obtain; it advises against starting in civil service without experience and lists the Presidential Innovation Fellowship, Horizon, TechCongress and AAAS fellowships. Horizon applications run roughly July to early September (2027 cohort deadline 30 August 2026; selection November-December). Other fellowships: Anthropic Fellows economics and policy track (4 months, rolling, next cohort January 2027, no visa sponsorship, US/UK/Canada work authorisation), Constellation Astra's strategy and governance track, and GovAI's summer fellowship (2026 applied track paid GBP 12,000 over three months in London, per an aggregator). Europe: EU Blue Book traineeship (five months; applications ahead of March and October starts), EPSO competitions (heavy competition), AI Office expression-of-interest calls (the 2026 call closed 8 September). Italy: the Garante, AgID, ACN and ministries via public competitions (concorsi), and compliance roles in banks, insurers and consultancies preparing for the AI Act. Certifications: IAPP's AIGP exam costs US$649 for members and $799 for non-members (100 questions, 2.75 hours; IAPP, October 2026); it is common in compliance postings and shows commitment but does not replace a degree or domain experience (my assessment). Portfolio: policy writing samples (a consultation response, an AI Act analysis for a sector), a clear explanation of how a model is evaluated, familiarity with the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 (general knowledge). Interviews: written briefing note, panel interview, regulation case study. Common mistakes: relying on AIGP alone; staying too abstract; ignoring technical literacy; writing like a lawyer for a technical audience. Alternative routes: privacy/IP lawyers, engineers moving into technical policy, consultants, think-tank research assistantships. Entry difficulty: 4/5 for lab, regulator and top fellowship roles; 3/5 for corporate compliance and consulting.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Pay outside labs and consultancies is modest relative to technical AI roles (UK recruiter bands for managers are GBP 90,000-130,000, against six-figure dollar bases at labs). Roles depend on political cycles: the EU delayed high-risk obligations to December 2027 and August 2028, which shifts hiring plans, and the AI Office's 2027 contract-agent hiring depends on budget decisions. Policy teams at companies can be overruled by commercial priorities. Government processes are slow. Not a fit for people who want to build things, dislike ambiguity about whether their work changes outcomes, or dislike bureaucracy.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
