What you actually do

You decide what the team should build next and why. Concretely: interview customers and read support tickets; define a problem and success metric; write a one- to two-page PRD (requirements document) for, say, a new onboarding flow; agree scope with two or three engineers and a designer; prioritise the backlog; run an experiment (A/B test); launch, then review the results dashboard (SQL, Amplitude/Mixpanel/Looker-type tools) and decide to iterate or stop. You maintain a roadmap, run quarterly planning with OKRs, and present to leadership. Growth PMs own conversion and retention funnels and run many experiments; platform/technical PMs own APIs, data platforms or developer tools and spend more time with engineers on architecture; B2B PMs spend more time with sales and customers; consumer PMs spend more time on data and design.

A typical day and week

A day is mostly meetings and writing: a morning stand-up with the engineering team, a design review, a customer call or data pull, afternoon stakeholder alignment (sales, legal, marketing), and an hour of writing specs or reviewing metrics. A week includes sprint planning (the team's one- or two-week work cycle), a roadmap or prioritisation discussion, a customer interview or two, an experiment readout, and a leadership update. About half your time is communication; deep work is squeezed into early mornings or evenings.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

Typical 45-55 hours a week; peak 60-70 around launches, planning deadlines or incidents at startups. Stress 3/5 (steady deadlines, metrics pressure, constant trade-offs; 4/5 at an early-stage startup where the product is the company). Lifestyle is flexible in some firms but big tech has tightened return-to-office (Amazon five days a week; Google, Meta and Apple three or four, per prior Admetia research on tech RTO, 2025-26: unverified at source here). Travel is low. Remote PM postings rose 31% in 2025 (Mind the Product, December 2025).

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5 (4/5 for growth and platform PMs). In practice you must persuade engineers and executives without authority over them, and still be comfortable reading funnel data, writing basic SQL and discussing API or system trade-offs.

Compensation (approximate)

  • US: Levels.fyi new-offer total compensation (base plus bonus plus stock), 2025: PM median US$234k (10th-90th percentile US$136k-350k); Senior PM US$381k; Group PM US$480k; Director US$606k (Mind the Product with Levels.fyi, December 2025; skewed to large tech and the Bay Area). All-level medians on Levels.fyi on 9 October 2026: Bay Area US$312.5k (n=1,297), New York US$211k (n=983). Entry PM (0-3 years) at smaller companies is lower; no reliable data found for non-tech corporate PM salaries in the US.
  • UK: London median £109,949 (n=350, 25th-75th percentile £84k-147k) on Levels.fyi, 9 October 2026; recruiter 3Search data for 2025 puts "Associate PM / PM" at £50k-80k, Senior PM at £70k-110k, Head of Product £90k-125k, and says Director offers have fallen (Mind the Product, December 2025). Revolut UK PM median £135k (n=59, Levels.fyi, 30 September 2026, in prior Admetia research).
  • Italy: Levels.fyi lists an Italian PM median of €51,181 (n=23, so thin), with Milan at €62,727 and a 25th-75th percentile range of €40.5k-70.2k (9 October 2026). Aggregator NettoCalc (Glassdoor-based estimates, 2026) gives Milan median €66,000 gross, junior (0-3 years) €48,000, and Italy-wide median €50,000, junior €36,000; an older Product Heroes/Sec Newgate survey (2023) gave €43,000 average, €35,000 junior. Italian pay is mostly base; equity is rare outside Bending Spoons-style companies.
  • Elsewhere: Germany median €95,144 (n=239), Berlin €99,214; France €70,510 (n=54), Paris €72,220; Singapore SGD 150,330 (n=148) (all Levels.fyi, 9 October 2026). Amsterdam, Stockholm, Dubai, Tokyo, Hong Kong, Riyadh: no reliable data found.
  • Senior: see the US and UK lines above (Group PM US$480k, Director US$606k; London Head of Product £90-125k at typical employers).

Career path

Typical ladder: Associate PM or PM (0-3 years), PM II / Senior PM (4-7 years), Group PM or Principal PM (7-10 years), Director / VP Product (10+), Chief Product Officer. Many people stall at Senior PM; promotion typically takes 2-3 years per level (practitioner estimate).

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: a bigger PM role, founder's-associate/chief of staff roles, growth or strategy roles, or a startup. After 7-10 years: Head of Product or CPO at a smaller company, general manager (GM) roles, founder, venture capital partner or operating partner (see Finance branch), or product consulting/fractional PM (fractional roles are growing, per Mind the Product).

Tier list of employers

(Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.)

  • Tier 1 (highest pay and brand): Google, Meta, Apple, Amazon, Microsoft, plus frontier AI labs and Stripe-level scale-ups. Meta London PM median £301k and Amazon £147k on Levels.fyi (all levels, 9 October 2026). Hardest to enter; best brand for later moves.
  • Tier 2 (strong product cultures): Uber, Airbnb, Spotify, Booking.com, Adyen, Revolut, Atlassian, Salesforce, Bending Spoons, well-funded venture-backed scale-ups. Pay lower than Tier 1 outside the US but more scope.
  • Tier 3 (corporate / non-tech): banks, insurers, retailers, telcos, consultancies' product teams. Lower pay, slower cycles, more titles called "product owner".

How to enter

Degrees: any degree works in principle; computer science, engineering, data, economics and design have the highest hit rate at tech firms, but business and management graduates do get PM roles, mostly after a first role in operations, analytics or a startup. Pathways: APM/RPM programmes (3.2); a lateral move after 1-3 years in an adjacent role; MBA entry (Amazon, Microsoft and others run MBA PM tracks; the experience-requiring MBA route is common in the US); startup first. Calendar: APM/RPM applications run from late summer to winter (Meta in late summer/early fall, Google historically in winter, Uber late summer/September); ordinary PM roles are filled year round. Interview formats (practitioner consensus): product sense (design or improve a product), execution/metrics (diagnose a metric drop, define success metrics), estimation, behavioural, a technical round for platform/technical PMs (system design lite), and a take-home product case or presentation at startups and scale-ups. Certifications: Scrum Product Owner (Scrum.org PSPO or Scrum Alliance CSPO), Pragmatic Institute and Product School certificates are visible in Italian and European corporate job ads, but there is no evidence they matter for big-tech or scale-up hiring; treat them as a low-cost signal for non-tech and consulting roles, not a substitute for shipped work (practitioner consensus; no hard data). Skills and portfolio: basic SQL, ability to read experiment results, customer interviewing, clear writing, and demonstrable shipped work: a side project with users, an internal tool, a case study with real metrics. AI-assisted prototyping is increasingly expected (Builder PM postings). Common mistakes: applying only to the biggest brands; writing generic "I love products" statements; no evidence of shipping; ignoring SQL/analytics; underestimating how competitive junior roles are (400+ applicants within days; Mind the Product). Alternative routes: engineer → PM (strongest at tech firms); consultant → PM (common for B2B and strategy-heavy roles); analyst → PM (data-led companies); internal transfer after joining in operations, support or sales; startup first, where product responsibility arrives early. Entry difficulty: 4/5.

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

You are accountable for outcomes without authority over the people who build them. Junior openings have shrunk; the job is increasingly squeezed between engineering-led AI tooling and flatter organisations. Pay in Europe is far below US levels; Italy is lower again. Not a good fit if you dislike ambiguity and constant negotiation, cannot tolerate being blamed for decisions made by committees, or want deep technical craft or a predictable 9-to-5.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
