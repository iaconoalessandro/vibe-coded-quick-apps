What you actually do

You build models and software that interpret and act on sensor data. Tasks include training detection, segmentation or tracking models (for cars, factory defects, tumours on scans), building data collection and labelling pipelines, compressing models to run on small chips (quantisation, pruning, TensorRT/ONNX conversion), writing real-time code in C++ and Python, calibrating cameras and lidar, running simulation, and, in robotics, integrating perception with planning and control (ROS, learned policies, imitation or reinforcement learning). Deliverables: a model that hits, say, 99.5% recall on defect detection at 30 frames per second on a specific device, plus the test evidence safety and quality teams require.

A typical day and week

Look at failure cases from the last test drive or production line; retrain or adjust the model; profile latency on target hardware; code review (often C++); sync with hardware, mechanical or safety engineers. Weekly: a field or lab test session (a test track, a factory floor, a robot cell), a data-labelling quality review, a safety-case or validation meeting. You spend more time with physical hardware, test benches and labelling vendors than in a pure software role.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

40-50 hours typical; 55-60 near field tests, demos or product launches (practitioner consensus). Stress 3/5, higher (4/5) in safety-critical automotive, medical and defence settings or in robotics startups racing to demos. Mostly in-person or hybrid because hardware is involved; travel to test sites is common; remote work is harder than in pure software.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. You work with hardware, safety and operations teams, but the core is mathematics (geometry, probability), programming and engineering judgement about the physical world.

Compensation (approximate)

No dedicated Levels.fyi or BLS figure for computer vision or robotics was retrievable (the specific pages returned errors), so I use the ML engineer proxy and flag the gap. Levels.fyi, 9 Oct 2026, ML engineer: US median US$280K; Meta E3 US$187K; NVIDIA IC1 US$205K; Google L3 US$208K; these are internet and chip companies, and pay at automotive, industrial and medical-device firms is generally lower (my expectation based on practitioner consensus; no dataset found). UK: ML engineer median GBP 103,596 (Levels.fyi) or GBP 67,500 (ITJobsWatch ads); computer vision specific: no reliable data found. Italy: Levels.fyi ML engineer median EUR 32,471; automotive/industrial entry likely at the lower end of that (unverified). Germany ML engineer median EUR 88,465; Zurich CHF 152,282; France EUR 75,949; Singapore S$141,805. Robotics startups often pay lower base and more equity.

Career path

Junior perception/CV engineer (0-2) -> CV or robotics engineer (2-4) -> Senior (4-7) -> Staff/principal, tech lead, or perception manager (7-12). Master's graduates often enter at the same level as bachelor's with 1-2 years' experience; PhDs enter at a higher level in research-heavy teams.

Exit opportunities

After 2-3 years: general ML engineer, embedded/robotics software, autonomy and simulation, drone/defence tech, medical-imaging AI. After 7-10 years: perception lead, head of autonomy/robotics, CTO at a robotics or vision startup, technical roles in industrial AI.

Tier list of employers

Tier 1: autonomy and physical-AI leaders (Waymo, Tesla Autopilot, NVIDIA robotics/Drive, Wayve in London, Mobileye, Boston Dynamics, well-funded humanoid and robot-foundation-model startups, Google DeepMind robotics) and defence-tech (Anduril, Skydio). Tier 2: large automotive and industrial companies' AI/autonomy groups (BMW, Mercedes, Bosch, Siemens, ABB, Siemens Healthineers, GE HealthCare, Philips), medical-imaging startups, drone makers. Tier 3: system integrators and consultancies, smaller industrial suppliers, inspection and vision-systems vendors (in Italy, for example, groups such as Leonardo, Comau or university/IIT-linked labs are examples I did not verify as hiring now). Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 offers frontier problems and big budgets with intense schedules; Tier 3 offers stability and domain knowledge with slower tools and lower pay.

How to enter

Degrees and backgrounds. Computer science, electrical/electronic, mechatronics, robotics, physics, maths or aerospace. A master's is common in Europe, and many perception roles at serious teams prefer an MSc or PhD with a computer vision thesis; strong bachelor's graduates enter via internships. Programs. NVIDIA internships and New College Grad (the program lists "edge computing" and "autonomous machines" as areas), carmaker graduate programmes, EU research projects, Anthropic Fellows are not relevant here. Hiring calendar: carmakers and industrials recruit on a university cycle (autumn), startups year-round. Interviews. Coding (often C++), ML/vision fundamentals (convolutions, transformers for vision, detection metrics), geometry questions, a project deep-dive, sometimes a take-home on a dataset. Certifications. Mostly irrelevant; NVIDIA Deep Learning Institute courses and Jetson projects are mild positives. Portfolio. A project on a real device (Jetson, Raspberry Pi with accelerator, a small robot or drone), participation in competitions (Kaggle vision, robotics challenges, F1TENTH, RoboCup, formula-student driverless teams), a model deployed with measured latency and accuracy, open-source contributions (OpenCV, ROS, PyTorch). Common mistakes: working only on benchmark datasets and never on messy real data or hardware; ignoring C++ and real-time constraints; underestimating data collection and labelling. Alternative routes: embedded or robotics software engineers adding ML; ML engineers moving to a vision team; mechatronics/electrical engineers learning ML. Entry difficulty: 4/5

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

Smaller job market than general ML; relevant employers cluster in a few cities (Bay Area, Munich/Stuttgart, London, Zurich, Tokyo, Shenzhen), so in Italy the options are fewer; safety certification and testing slow progress; the physical world (weather, hardware faults) makes failures hard to reproduce. Many robotics and self-driving ventures have burned cash for years, so job security at startups is weak. Not a fit if you want pure remote software work, avoid C++ and hardware, or dislike slow, validation-heavy engineering.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
