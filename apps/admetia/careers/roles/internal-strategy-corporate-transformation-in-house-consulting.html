What you actually do

You do strategy and change work for your own employer. Examples: build the three-year strategic plan and the annual budget narrative, evaluate an acquisition target for corporate development, run the transformation office that tracks cost savings across divisions, staff an internal consulting team on a business-unit problem, prepare materials for the CEO and board, and track market and competitor trends. Tools: Excel, PowerPoint, business-intelligence tools, financial models. Because you live with the results, more of your time goes on follow-through than on the initial analysis.

A typical day and week

Morning: sync with finance and business-unit leaders; midday: modelling; afternoon: prepare a board paper; evening: usually off. Peaks occur around planning cycles, board meetings and M&A deals. You attend more meetings and negotiate with internal stakeholders who have their own agendas.

Hours, stress and lifestyle

  • Hours: typical 42-50 per week; peaks 55-60 (StrategyCase, May 2026 and July 2026 cite 40-55 hours for corporate strategy; coach sources, no survey).
  • Stress: 2/5 to 3/5. Occasional crunch around budget, board and M&A cycles.
  • Lifestyle/flexibility: generally the best in the branch; hybrid and remote policies follow the company.
  • Travel: low to moderate.
  • Remote/hybrid: high; follows company policy.

Human vs. quantitative profile

People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Success depends on internal credibility and influence more than on analytical brilliance.

Compensation (approximate)

Entry: graduates usually join through rotational/leadership development programmes or finance/strategy analyst roles; pure strategy jobs usually need 2-5 years of experience. No reliable entry-level data found for the US, UK or Italy by programme; graduate-programme pay at large corporates is generally in line with the national graduate market rather than consulting (inference; Mercer cites about €30.5k for Italian first-job pay in 2024, as quoted by Sky TG24, Jan 2025).

~5 years: a coaching site gives corporate strategy total compensation for a senior manager of $250-350k in tech, $200-280k in pharma, $200-260k in consumer and $180-240k in industrials (StrategyCase, May 2026; a coach's table with internal inconsistencies, treat as indicative). UK and Italy: no reliable data found.

Senior: Directors $260-600k, VPs $450k-1.2m and SVP/CSO $700k-2.5m+ depending on sector in the same table (US, 2026; indicative); equity makes a big difference at tech companies.

Career path

Strategy analyst/associate → manager → senior manager → director → VP strategy/CSO or business-unit general manager. Median tenure at a first corporate strategy employer was reported at 2-3 years (StrategyCase, May 2026, coach source). Typical entry is after 2-5 years of consulting or an MBA; exits to P&L roles are the aim.

Exit opportunities

  • After 2-3 years: business-unit roles, product or marketing management, corporate development, or move to a consulting firm.
  • After 7-10 years: CSO, COO, business-unit general manager, chief of staff to the CEO, startup executive, private-equity operating roles.

Tier list of employers

Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.

  • Internal consulting arms with consulting-style staffing: large technology firms (strategy and operations teams), some large banks, industrials and pharma groups. These are the most sought after and often recruit ex-MBB.
  • Corporate strategy/corp dev at blue-chips: big tech, pharma, consumer goods, energy, financial services.
  • Rotational leadership programmes: the main route for graduates (for example at consumer-goods, industrial and financial groups).
  • Differences: whether "strategy" means CEO-adjacent decisions or running a PMO varies a lot; some teams are title-only (StrategyCase, 2026).

How to enter

  • Degrees/backgrounds: business, economics, engineering, finance and any degree with a strong analytical record. Direct graduate entry is mostly through rotational programmes, finance or planning analyst roles, or business-analytics roles.
  • Programmes: graduate/leadership development schemes (many open in autumn; vary by employer); internships; MBA internships and post-MBA recruiting for strategy roles.
  • Interview formats: structured behavioural interviews, case/strategy exercises, presentations to a panel; 3-5 rounds over 4-8 weeks is described for corporate strategy (StrategyCase, 2026).
  • Certifications: usually irrelevant; Lean Six Sigma or PMP help in operations transformation roles.
  • Skills to build: financial modelling, understanding of the industry, executive communication, ability to build coalitions.
  • Common mistakes: assuming a degree alone gets a strategy job; skipping industry knowledge; underrating politics.
  • Alternative routes: start in finance, FP&A, operations or product and move into strategy; join as a consultant first (the common route); internal transfer within the company.
  • Entry difficulty: 3/5 for graduate rotational programmes at well-known companies; 4/5 for strategy-team roles at top companies (few seats, experience required).

Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for

  • Smaller teams and fewer promotions; strategy teams are cut in downturns; politics and slow decision-making; lower pay than consulting at the junior and mid levels; limited exposure to many industries.
  • Not a fit if you want fast learning across many industries, want to be compensated for long hours, or dislike working inside one organisation for years.

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
