Why early beats the deadline

In a rolling process the stated deadline is the last possible day, not the recommended one. Employers say assessments start before the deadline and that roles close when they are full; none publishes offer rates by application week, so how much earlier helps is unknown. The research’s verdict on this, word for word:

H2. "Rolling applications mean a submission in the first two weeks has materially higher odds." Verdict: partly supported (direction yes, size unknown).

  • For: employers say assessments start before deadlines (BofA), that roles close "once positions are filled" (EY), that the process is "first-come, first-served" (Unilever NL), and that a role closes when "the required number of applications" is reached (Unilever UK) [employer-stated]. ISE reports employers "closing roles early or reducing application periods" in response to volume (ISE, Apr 2026) [data].
  • Against: no employer or survey publishes offer rates by application week, so "materially" cannot be quantified. Some employers reopen roles later in the season (EY) [employer-stated].
  • Holds best for: high-volume schemes with fixed intake (Big 4, FMCG, bank summer programmes). Weakest for: boutiques and off-cycles that hire when a need appears.

Some figures in circulation put a number on the advantage (for example that applying three days before a deadline cuts interview odds by 70–80%). The research file carries them without a source, and they contradict its own verdict above, so this page does not use them.

Decision rules

  1. If you will start a one-year UK MSc in September and target London IB or consulting, prefer finishing your CV, a 25–40 firm target list and online-test practice by 31 July before arrival (because London windows open in July and fill on a rolling basis; LSE Careers, BofA, Citi).
  2. If a firm says it recruits on a rolling basis or first-come, first-served, prefer submitting within two to three weeks of opening (because assessments start before deadlines and roles close when full; direction employer-stated, size unquantified).
  3. If you have not yet practised a firm's test format, prefer doing practice tests (vendor or firm practice zones) before your first live test of the season (because a failed test can bar you for 6–12 months: EY, P&G).
  4. If you missed the London bulge-bracket summer window, prefer graduate analyst applications in September–November plus boutique and off-cycle applications from January (because boutiques recruit year-round; LSE Careers).
  5. If you are a continental MiM student targeting London IB, prefer applying in July–October of M1 for the M1 summer, or planning a London off-cycle in a gap year (because London converts summer interns).
  6. If you target a French corporate, bank or consultancy and can work in French, prefer a programme that ends with a six-month stage de fin d'études, and choose the stage host as the employer you want (because 42.3% of hires come through that route; CGE 2026).
  7. If you target Germany/DACH, prefer internships taken while enrolled (mandatory or within the three-month voluntary limit) over post-graduation internships (because MiLoG §22 makes post-graduation long internships more expensive for employers).
  8. If you target Big 4 or an FMCG graduate scheme in the UK, prefer applying in the first month the scheme opens (because EY and Unilever close roles once full).

Myths

  1. "Deadlines are what matter." In rolling processes the deadline is the latest possible date, not the recommended one; BofA says assessments start before it, and Citi has no deadline at all [employer-stated]. Treat the opening date as the real deadline.
  2. "I'll do the spring week during my master's." Spring and insight programmes target first- and second-year undergraduates (Deloitte, Deutsche Bank, Rothschild pages) [employer-stated]. Master's students go straight to summer, off-cycle or graduate roles.
  3. "The online test is a formality." P&G's assessments decide progression automatically for most roles, with a 12-month wait to retry; EY imposes a six-month bar after rejection (P&G, EY pages) [employer-stated]. A test failed in September can cost you the whole season at that firm.
  4. "A one-year UK MSc gives me a year to recruit." It gives you one autumn. The next cycle's window (for roles starting a year after graduation) opens during the dissertation (LSE Careers timelines) [practitioner consensus].
  5. "In France I'll apply to graduate schemes in my final year." Most hiring runs through the final stage; students who treat the stage as a formality give up the main channel (CGE 2026) [data].

A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

  • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
  • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
  • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.
