Application toolkit

Four one-column CV templates that applicant tracking systems can read, in Word and LaTeX, and motivation-letter skeletons sized to Bocconi’s, Imperial’s, LSE’s and ESCP’s own limits, with a checker for your draft.

CV templates

Each template is one column with standard headings, the contact details in the body, real bullets and no tables, text boxes, icons or photos: the safe default for applicant tracking systems. Replace every [bracketed] placeholder. The Word file opens in Word, Google Docs, Pages and LibreOffice; the LaTeX file compiles with pdfLaTeX, and the Overleaf button opens a copy in your own Overleaf account.

Template 1

Investment banking and markets: one-page CV

For Investment banking, sales and trading, equity research, asset management, private equity (graduate and intern roles), and finance roles at London-run schemes.

  • Education first, with the grade in the local system and the scale beside it: finance screens read grades, and a missing grade is read as a bad one.
  • Experience bullets name the deal type, the size and the analysis you did, because the first screen looks for those words.
  • One page, no photo, no date of birth: the UK and US finance norm, and the norm for London-run schemes recruiting in Milan or Frankfurt.

Template 2

Strategy consulting: one-page CV

For Strategy and management consulting (MBB, tier 2, Big 4 consulting and deals), corporate strategy and rotational programmes.

  • Every bullet is an action, its context and a result with a number, so the CV doubles as the list of stories for the fit interview.
  • Leadership gets its own section: McKinsey, BCG and Bain all interview on it, and the CV is where the interviewer picks the story.
  • One page, no photo, standard headings, contact details in the body.

Template 3

Software engineering and data: one-page CV

For Software engineering, data science and analytics, machine learning, quantitative developer roles and technical product roles.

  • Technical skills sit high and are grouped by type, because recruiters and parsers search for named languages and tools.
  • Projects come before experience if they are stronger: each names the stack, links the code and gives a measurable result.
  • One column with no skill bars or icons; parsers cannot read graphics.

Template 4

Master's application CV (academic)

For Applications to MSc, MiM and MiF programmes, and scholarship applications. Admissions offices read credits, grades and tests first.

  • Credits by subject area, in ECTS or CFU, sit under each degree: continental schools gate on credits in named sectors, and a test score does not waive them.
  • The thesis, test scores and language certificates have their own lines, with dates.
  • Referees sit at the end, typed by school: some want an academic referee, some a professional one, some one of each on the CV.

Writing the bullets

Start with a verb, say what you worked on, and end with a result, with a number where there is one. The first screen is quick and pattern-based, so the employer, the deal or project and the number should be readable at a glance.

Weak and strong versions of the same bullet
WeakStrong
Responsible for financial analysis on deals.Built a three-statement model and DCF for a €120m packaging target; the valuation range went into the client’s board deck.
Helped organise events for the finance society.Organised 6 recruiting events with 4 banks; attendance up from 40 to 150 a session.
Worked on a machine-learning project.Trained a gradient-boosted model on 2m transactions in Python; cut false fraud alerts by 18% against the rules-based baseline.
Team player with strong communication skills.Led a 5-person team through a 48-hour case competition; 2nd of 60 teams.

The examples are invented to show the pattern. Never invent a number on your own CV: interviewers ask about every line.

Motivation letters by school

Limits differ by an order of magnitude, so one letter cannot be reused: write a bank of facts and stories once, then write each letter to that school’s prompt and limit. The schools publish limits and prompts; how to divide the space is our suggestion.

  • “Reusing one letter across schools breaks at least one limit and, per Imperial, risks naming the wrong programme.” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md
  • “write a fact bank (stories, numbers, projects) once, then rewrite each letter to that school's limit and prompts” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md
  • “The schools publish limits and prompts, not percentages.” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md

Bocconi · MSc programmes

Limit: about 4,000 characters (1–2 pages) M

What the school says
  • “Motivation letter to programme directors, "around 1 to 2 pages (indicatively no more than 4000 characters)" (search summary of Bocconi admissions page)” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md
  • “Bocconi states: "The selection process is based on the admission test performance, your GPA, your 'Dossier' and your Motivation"” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md

A way to spend the limit (Admetia’s suggestion, not the school’s rule):

  1. Why this programmeabout 1,000 characters

    Name the programme, two or three courses or tracks you want and why. Show you know how it differs from the alternatives.

  2. What prepared youabout 1,000 characters

    Your degree, the courses and grades that matter for this programme, your thesis. If you are short of credits in a required area, say how you are filling the gap.

  3. Evidence from experienceabout 1,000 characters

    One internship, project or activity told as a short story with a result.

  4. Your goal and the linkabout 700 characters

    The role or sector you aim for after the programme, and which parts of it get you there.

  5. Closeabout 300 characters

    One or two sentences. No list of adjectives.

Imperial Business School · MSc programmes

Limit: 3,500 + 2,500 characters (two questions) H

What the school says
  • “Q1 "Why do you want to study at Imperial Business School and how will you contribute to our community?" 3,500 characters (~500 words)” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md
  • “Q2 describe a time you embodied an Imperial value (Respect, Collaboration, Integrity, Innovation, Excellence) and its impact, 2,500 characters (~350 words)” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md
  • “Admissions blog: "we want to hear about you, not us"; do not paste statements from other applications and "use the correct programme title"” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md

A way to spend the limit (Admetia’s suggestion, not the school’s rule):

Question 1 (3,500 characters)

  1. Why Imperial Business Schoolabout 1,400 characters

    The programme by its correct name, the modules, projects or people that matter to you, and why they fit what you want to do.

  2. What you will contributeabout 1,500 characters

    Two pieces of evidence: what you have done that other students will gain from (a skill, an experience, a perspective).

  3. Where it leadsabout 600 characters

    One sentence on your goal and how the year gets you there.

Question 2 (2,500 characters)

  1. The value and the situationabout 450 characters

    Name the value (Respect, Collaboration, Integrity, Innovation or Excellence) and set the scene in two sentences.

  2. What you didabout 1,250 characters

    Your actions, in the first person, in the order you took them.

  3. The impactabout 550 characters

    What changed, with a number where there is one.

  4. What you took from itabout 250 characters

    One sentence.

LSE · Statement of academic purpose

Limit: 1,000–1,500 words H

What the school says
  • “"Statement of academic purpose", typically 1,000-1,500 words, programme-specific” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md
  • “"avoid general statements about LSE's performance in global rankings, and generic statements about London being a global/cosmopolitan centre of excellence"” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md

A way to spend the limit (Admetia’s suggestion, not the school’s rule):

  1. The academic question that interests youabout 250 words

    A topic or problem from your studies you want to go further with, stated precisely.

  2. How your studies prepared youabout 400 words

    Courses, grades, thesis and methods, linked to what the programme teaches.

  3. Why this programmeabout 400 words

    Specific modules, the department's research, the structure of the programme. Not the school's ranking, not London.

  4. Relevant experienceabout 200 words

    Work, research or projects that show you can do the work.

  5. Your plansabout 150 words

    Further study or the career you intend, and how the programme fits.

Check your draft

    AI and your own words

    Employers and schools allow AI for research, structure and proofreading, and ban it for writing answers in assessments and interviews. Detectors misread non-native English, so the practical risk is a follow-up question about a sentence you cannot explain.

    • “keep a dated draft in your own words and be able to explain every sentence” research/getting-in/applications-and-interviews.md

    A reality check on pay, hours and tiers

    Every figure here is dated and approximate, copied from the research with its source and year. Pay is in local currency and never converted. Tier lists reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Three limits apply to almost every page:

    • Asia and Gulf pay across almost all branches rests on aggregators, small self-reported samples (Levels.fyi has 12-40 submissions in some cities) or recruiter blogs. Tokyo is the thinnest.
    • Hours and stress are researchers' estimates from practitioner accounts, prep sites and a few surveys (mainly M&A). No report found a systematic hours survey by role family.
    • Gated recruiter guides (Hays, Robert Walters, Michael Page tables outside Italy, Management Consulted, CIPD, PMI) could not be read. Many pay figures therefore come via secondary summaries, as the reports note.

    The full list of gaps, the rating scales and the researchers’ assumptions are on the sources page.
