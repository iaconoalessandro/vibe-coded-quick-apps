# Working in Spain, Portugal and the Nordics after a business master's

> **Not legal, tax or immigration advice.** This file summarises official sources as of the dates given. Rules change and individual cases differ: confirm with the competent authority or a qualified adviser before acting.

Today is 2 October 2026. Exchange rates are ECB reference rates of 1 Oct 2026: €1 = SEK 11.3310 = DKK 7.4758 = NOK 10.8750 (ECB, 2026, https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/eurofxref/eurofxref-daily.xml) [data]. Method for net pay and rent follows money/salaries-and-roi.md section 5 (net minus a central one-bed rent). Where this file says "author calculation", the method and parameters are in section 3.

## Bottom line

1. **Three different bets, not one.** Sweden and Norway have strong recent-graduate employment (91.1% and 91.8%) and good net pay after rent; Finland and Denmark are middling (86.5% and 86.4%), and Denmark's first year after graduation is slow. Spain and Portugal are accessible and English-friendly in niches, but local entry pay is low against big-city rents (Eurostat, 2025; DI, 2026) [data].
2. **Net minus rent reorders the cities.** At the same €45,000 gross, the money left after a central one-bed is highest in Helsinki (≈€1,770 a month), then Stockholm (≈€1,470), Madrid (≈€1,385), Porto (≈€1,370), Oslo (≈€1,135), Lisbon (≈€1,100) and Copenhagen (≈€725). Denmark keeps 65% of €60,000, the lowest in the table (author calculation; tax tables and Numbeo rents, Oct 2026) [data inputs; anecdotal rent].
3. **Reported local graduate pay favours Stockholm and Oslo.** SSE's 2025 class reports a mean of SEK 663,810 (≈€58,600) incl. bonus, leaving ≈€2,240 a month after tax and rent; NHH's 2024 class a median of NOK 580,000 gross (≈€53,300), leaving ≈€1,600 to €2,000. The all-fields Spanish master's graduate earns €26,613 in year one, leaving ≈€410 in Madrid (SSE 2026; NHH 2025; BBVA-Ivie 2024; author calculation) [data]. In money/salaries-and-roi.md the comparators are Zurich ≈€3,500 and London ≈€915.
4. **In the Nordics, access is a stay-rate question.** 53% of international master's graduates were employed in Denmark two years out, but 46% at CBS; Italians were 9% of the stayers, second to Germans. Only 36% of international master's graduates were in Sweden three years out (SCB), and 38% at SSE. In Finland 53% of foreign graduates were employed three years out against 87-88% of Finns, 63% of them in expert jobs against 77% (DI 2026; SCB 2026; SSE 2026; OPH 2025) [data].
5. **Expat tax relief mostly misses graduates, with one exception.** Spain's Beckham regime is worse than the normal regime at €30,000-45,000 and level at €60,000. Denmark's 27% scheme needs DKK 65,400 a month, Finland's 25% regime €5,800, Sweden's 25% exemption excludes anyone resident in the last five years. **Portugal's IRS Jovem (under 35, up to 10 years) is aimed at graduates** and is worth about €7,000 a year at €45,000 in year one on my approximation (author calculation; official pages in section 4) [data].
6. **Non-EU readers: Denmark narrowed its door on 1 October 2026.** New study permits come with a 1-year job-seeking permit instead of 3. Sweden gives up to 1 year, Finland up to 2, and Spain allows a direct switch to a work permit (SIRI, 1 Oct 2026; Migrationsverket; Migri; BOE) [data].
7. **Language binds outside a few niches.** Only 5.8% of Spanish postings waive Spanish. Named graduate programmes are the Nordic exception (Novo Nordisk: Danish not required; SEB and Nordea: English, with Swedish needed for specific roles) (Indeed 2024; employer pages 2026) [data; employer-stated].
8. **For an Italian graduate:** Sweden and Norway good if you reach working language skills or enter via a corporate programme; Finland and Denmark conditional; Madrid and Barcelona good for international-facing roles but weak on pay outside consulting, banking or tech; Lisbon and Porto poor on pay without IRS Jovem, conditional for tech and shared services (author judgement, sections 1-6).

## 1. Graduate market and stay rates

### 1.1 Headline health

Eurostat's three measures need to be read together, as places/countries-and-cities.md argues. All figures are 2025 (Eurostat, pulled from the dissemination API on 2 Oct 2026, data updated 10 Sep 2026; datasets edat_lfse_24, lfsa_urgaed, une_rt_a) [data].

| Country | Recent graduates in work (20-34, 1-3 yrs out) | Tertiary-educated unemployment, 25-29 | Youth unemployment, 15-24 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spain | 86.1% (77.2% in 2019) | 10.0% | 24.9% |
| Portugal | 88.0% | 8.1% | 19.5% |
| Sweden | 91.1% | 8.3% | 24.3% |
| Denmark | 86.4% | 9.1% | 13.8% |
| Norway | 91.8% | 3.4% | 14.0% |
| Finland | 86.5% | 7.4% | 21.8% |
| Italy (reference) | 78.7% | 8.2% | 20.6% |
| Netherlands (reference) | 92.1% | 4.1% | 8.8% |

Norway and Sweden are the strong employment markets for recent graduates; Spain has closed most of the gap with the EU average (86.9%) since 2019. Sweden's youth rate (24.3%) is high even though its graduates do well, which is the pattern flagged in places/countries-and-cities.md. Norway is the outlier on tertiary unemployment (3.4%).

**Denmark needs a second measure.** Eurostat shows Danish recent graduates at 86.4%, but Danish register data show the first year is slow. A graduate with a long higher education finishing in 2024 was unemployed on average 23.3% of the first twelve months (22.1% for the 2023 cohort), and social sciences 18.5% (DI, April 2026, based on Statistics Denmark DREAM data) [data]. This is the share of months on unemployment-related status, not a headline unemployment rate. Treat it as "Danish graduates often have a gap, then find work".

Non-obvious points:
- **The Danish figure is rising fast, which is good news for EU graduates.** It went from about 40% to 53% since 2019, and Italians are a visible group among stayers (9%). The caveat is that the DI figure covers all master's graduates and is dominated by technical and health fields (66% and 59%); business-type graduates are below average (CBS 46%) (DI, 2026) [data].
- **Finland's EU/EEA citizens leave more often than other foreigners, but those who stay are more likely to land expert jobs.** OPH says so directly (OPH, 2025) [data]. So for an Italian the Finnish pattern is "fewer stay, those who do are well placed".
- **The Norwegian SSB study covers non-EU/EEA students only.** There is no equivalent EU/EEA stay-rate source in what I found, so the Norwegian figure does not describe an Italian (SSB, 2025) [data].
- **Spain and Portugal:** no stay-rate source comparable to the Nordic ones was found. Fundación BBVA-Ivie notes that foreign students were 23% of Spanish master's graduates in 2021-22 and that Spanish social-security affiliation understates employment for those who return home (Fundación BBVA-Ivie, 2024) [data].

### 1.3 What entry roles exist

Evidence here is thin outside SSE's report. SSE's 2025 class went 40% into consultancy, 29% into finance, 9% into technology and 5% into retail; first employers include McKinsey, BCG, Bain, Deloitte, EY, PwC, KPMG, Strategy&, Roland Berger, Handelsbanken, SEB, Swedbank, Klarna, H&M, Ericsson, Goldman Sachs and J.P. Morgan (SSE, 2026) [data]. Aalto's business graduates (2024 cohort) work in consultancy (21%), finance (13%) and the public sector (9%), 57% in large companies (Aalto, 2026) [data, school-reported]. For Spain and Portugal I found corporate graduate programmes (Santander, EDP, Galp; section 6) but no primary breakdown by sector, and no entry-pay source for shared-service centres (see places/gulf-and-central-eastern-europe.md for the CEE comparison).

## 2. Language: how binding it is

| Country | Language | Evidence on where English-only works | Evidence on where the local language is needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spain | Spanish | 5.8% of postings waive Spanish, and higher-paid occupations are less flexible; the exceptions are international hubs (an English-speaking developer in Barcelona is Indeed's example) (Indeed, 2024) [data]; IB, trading and international consulting are the usual niches [practitioner consensus, places/countries-and-cities.md] | Audit, tax, retail banking, FMCG sales and marketing [practitioner consensus] |
| Portugal | Portuguese | EDP's graduate programme states Portuguese is not required to apply (EDP, 2026) [employer-stated]. InterNations ranks Portugal last for expat career prospects (InterNations, 2026) [data, survey] | No source found |
| Sweden | Swedish | SEB's International Trainee Programme asks for fluent English | Specific SEB roles require English and Swedish (SEB, 2026) [employer-stated] |
| Denmark | Danish | Novo Nordisk: Danish "definitely not a requirement" for the graduate programme (Novo Nordisk, 2026) [employer-stated]. jobsinenglish.dk's top English-only employers in 2024 were DTU, Grundfos, MAN Energy Solutions, Terma and Topsoe; it misses Novo, Maersk and LEGO (The Local DK, Mar 2025) [practitioner consensus] | Some Novo International Operations tracks; mid-sized Danish firms at junior and middle levels, per press reporting [practitioner consensus] |
| Norway | Norwegian | No source found | No source found |
| Finland | Finnish, Swedish | No share-of-postings source found | Foreign graduates reach expert jobs less often than Finns (63% vs 77%) (OPH, 2025) [data] |

- **No primary source gives the share of Nordic postings that waive the local language,** and Indeed's 2024 table covers Spain but not the Nordics or Portugal (see Claims to verify).
- **"Everyone speaks English" is not "the workplace is in English."** The Local reports that Danish matters most at the lower and middle levels of mid-sized firms, where recruiters worry about fit even if the ad does not ask for Danish. That is press reporting of recruiter attitudes, not measurement.
- **Employer rules are sharper than country rules.** Cite Novo (Danish not required), EDP (Portuguese not required) and SEB or Nordea (English, with role-specific local language), and do not generalise them to a country.

Reading notes:
- **The Spanish figure is chart-read.** The BBVA-Ivie PDF's text layer lists the bar labels in two blocks (year 1, year 4). I mapped them to the 2017-18 cohort and cross-checked the bachelor's series against the prose (€29,559 / €22,134 = +33.5%, as the report states). The contribution base approximates gross pay, caps at about €61,200 and excludes graduates working abroad. It covers all fields, not business only [data].
- **FT figures (section 7) are not starting salaries** (money/salaries-and-roi.md, H3). Within Portugal, Nova SBE's $131,640 and Católica Porto's $55,831 differ because alumni destinations differ (FT international-work-mobility rank 10 against 85), so where you go matters more than which Portuguese school you pick (FT, 2026) [data].
- **NHH's median (≈€53,300) and SSE's mean (≈€58,600)** are above the LBS UK mean (£44,675, ≈€52,000) and the Paris Île-de-France mean (≈€42,400), and far above Milan (money/salaries-and-roi.md section 1).
- **Finland:** Aalto's business school reports 90% employed or entrepreneurs one year out, 92% of the employed in Finland, 75% with a job before graduating, 13% international (2024 cohort, 34% response). Its pay figures sit in a graphic I could not extract (Claims to verify).

### 3.2 Method for net pay

Author calculations for a single person, no children, no church tax, full year, no voluntary pension, 2026 parameters. Rent is the Numbeo central one-bedroom (pages updated 23 Sep to 1 Oct 2026) [anecdotal], as elsewhere in the library.

- **Sweden:** Skatteverket's official 2026 monthly tax table 32, column 1 (SKVFS 2025:20), for the row containing monthly pay; it assumes an average municipal rate, so Stockholm net is slightly understated. No employee social contributions [data].
- **Denmark:** AM tax 8%, ATP DKK 1,188, bottom tax 12.01%, middle and top tax 7.5% above DKK 696,956 and 845,543, municipal 25.049%, personal allowance DKK 54,100, employment deduction capped at DKK 63,300, job allowance DKK 3,100 (PwC Denmark, 25 Jun 2026) [practitioner consensus]. Assumed: employment deduction 12.75% of personal income up to the cap (inferred from the cap); employee pension ignored, so net is a few per cent high.
- **Norway:** 22% general income tax after a minimum deduction (assumed 46% of income, capped at NOK 95,700) and personal deduction NOK 114,540; employee social security 7.6%; bracket tax 1.7% to 17.8% above NOK 226,100 (PwC Norway, 3 Sep 2026) [practitioner consensus].
- **Finland:** state scale 12.64% to 37.5%, municipal 5.30% (Helsinki's 2026 budget proposal), employee pension 7.30%, unemployment 0.89%, sickness 1.98% (PwC Finland, 24 Jun 2026) [practitioner consensus]; basic deduction up to €4,265 (vero.fi) [data]; earned-income credit up to €3,430 with an assumed phase-out (1.84% above €33,000), so Finnish net is good to about ±€400.
- **Spain:** social security 6.5% up to €5,101.20 a month, €2,000 expenses, €5,550 personal minimum, 2026 state withholding scale (19% to 47%), as in money/salaries-and-roi.md, which this reproduces (€41,400 at €60,000). Catalonia's higher regional scale is not modelled, so Barcelona is likely worse.
- **Portugal:** 2026 IRS scale (12.5% to 48%), 11% social security, specific deduction set at 8.54 × the 2026 IAS of €537.13 (≈€4,587; the 2025 value of €4,462.15 equals 8.54 × that year's IAS) (PwC Portugal, 24 Jul 2026) [practitioner consensus]. Family-expense credits (≈€250) excluded.

## 4. Expat and return tax regimes: with and without relief

| Country | Regime and terms | Fit for a graduate (author calculation) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spain | "Beckham" (art. 93 LIRPF): 24% flat up to €600,000 (47% above) for the year of arrival plus five; no Spanish residence in the previous five tax years; move must follow an employment contract | **Worse than the normal regime at €30,000 (net €20,850 vs €23,124) and €45,000 (€31,275 vs €32,600); level at €60,000 (€41,700 vs €41,436)**; +€6,000 at €90,000, +€12,000 at €120,000 | Agencia Tributaria; PwC Spain, 30 Jun 2026 [data; practitioner consensus] |
| Portugal | IFICI (art. 58-A EBF): 20% flat for 10 years on qualifying income in eligible roles; no residence in the previous five years; NHR closed to new entrants from 2024 | Eligibility of a fresh graduate in a business role is doubtful (places/origin-countries-eu.md); if it applied, net at €60,000 ≈€41,400 vs ≈€38,000 | PwC Portugal, 24 Jul 2026; Portal das Finanças [practitioner consensus; data] |
| Portugal | **IRS Jovem** (art. 12-B CIRS): age up to 35 at 31 Dec, not a dependent; **exemption 100% in the first year of income, 75% in years 2-4, 50% in 5-7, 25% in 8-10, capped at 55 × IAS = €29,542**; claimed each year in the return | Approximately +€7,200 at €45,000 in year one (≈€30,300 to ≈€37,500) and +€4,800 at €30,000 | Portal das Finanças, ruling PIV 30125, 30 Apr 2026 [data] |
| Denmark | Researcher and key-employee scheme: 27% + 8% AM = 32.84% for up to 7 years; **at least DKK 65,400 a month on average in 2026 (DKK 78,000 in 2025)**; no Danish tax liability in the previous 10 years | At the threshold (≈€105,000) net rises from ≈62.3% to ≈67.0%; irrelevant at graduate pay | skat.dk; PwC Denmark [data] |
| Sweden | Expert tax relief: 25% of pay exempt for the first three years; employer or employee applies within 3 months; "experts", or pay above 1.5 × the price base amount (SEK 88,800 a month in 2026); not for anyone resident in Sweden in the previous five years | **Excludes anyone who studied in Sweden.** At SEK 88,800 a month the table-32 tax rate is 34%; with relief ≈21%, a gain of ≈€11,900 a year | Business Sweden, 2026; Skatteverket table 32 [data] |
| Finland | Expert regime: flat **25% from 1 Jan 2026 (32% before)**; cash pay at least €5,800 a month; no Finnish residence in the previous five calendar years; up to 84 months | No gain at €69,600; at €100,000 net rises from ≈61.5% to ≈66.8% | Vero.fi; PwC Finland [data] |
| Norway | No expat relief for resident employees verified; a flat 25% PAYE applies to temporary non-residents earning under NOK 725,050 | Not applicable | PwC Norway, 3 Sep 2026 [practitioner consensus] |

Traps for graduates who study first:
- **Beckham needs five years of non-residence.** A one-year master's in Madrid or Barcelona (over 183 days) will normally make you tax-resident, which would break the test. This is my reading of art. 93, not a ruling; ask an adviser first (AEAT note) [data; inference].
- **Sweden, Finland and Denmark exclude recent residents** (five calendar years, five calendar years, ten years). A two-year SSE or CBS master's removes the relief [data].
- **At graduate pay the relief that matters is IRS Jovem.** I could not resolve whether earlier internship or foreign income uses up its "years of income" (Claims to verify).

## 5. Permits and registration

**EU citizens** need no permit. Your Europe says registration with local authorities may be required after three months (accessed 2 Oct 2026) [data]; Finland lists a registration of the right of residence (Migri); Portugal issues a CRUE at the municipality within 30 days of the three-month mark for €15 (Lei 37/2006 art. 14; Portaria 13/2024; see `visas_immigration/portugal/portugal_visas_immigration_guide.md`) [data]. Norway's rules were not verified.

## Hypotheses tested

**H1: "Nordic pay is higher, but tax and rent erase the advantage."** Partly supported. At the same gross, Helsinki and Stockholm keep the most after rent (€1,771 and €1,472 at €45,000) and Copenhagen the least (€725). At reported pay the advantage survives: SSE's mean leaves ≈€2,240 a month and NHH's median ≈€1,600-2,000, against ≈€410 for the Spanish all-fields case. It fails for Denmark and for Oslo on Numbeo rents (author calculation; SSE; NHH; BBVA-Ivie) [data].

**H2: "Expat regimes are worth little at entry level; Portugal's IRS Jovem is the exception."** Supported. Beckham lowers net at €30,000-45,000, Finland's regime gives nothing at €69,600, Denmark's needs DKK 65,400 a month, Sweden's excludes people who studied there. IRS Jovem adds ≈€7,200 at €45,000 in year one. It fails if the year-count or tax-residence rules exclude you (unresolved) (Portal das Finanças; PwC; vero.fi; skat.dk; AEAT) [data; author calculation].

**H3: "Where you study decides whether you can work in the Nordics."** Partly supported. For non-EU readers a local degree unlocks the 1-3 year job-seeking permit. For EU readers the constraint is language and networks: SSE's report shows 45% of employed alumni work at a corporate partner, and only 36-53% of international graduates remain in Sweden, Denmark or Finland (SCB; DI; OPH; SSE) [data].

**H4: "Italians are an established group among Danish stayers."** Supported (descriptive): 9% of employed international graduates two years out, behind Germans (11%) and level with Spaniards (DI, 2026) [data]. Business graduates do worse than engineers (66% employed) or health graduates (59%).

**H5: "A Portuguese or Spanish MiM pays for itself only if you leave."** Partly supported. FT mobility ranks (Nova 10, Católica Porto 85) and the BBVA-Ivie note on foreign graduates fit it, and Spanish all-fields pay leaves ≈€410-750 after Madrid rent. But a Madrid consulting or tech job at €45,000 leaves ≈€1,385, so the sector matters (author calculation; FT 2026) [data].

## Common mistakes and myths

1. **"Everyone in the Nordics speaks English, so I can work in English."** English-only programmes exist (Novo Nordisk, EDP), but SEB says specific roles need Swedish, and Danish press reports recruiters worrying about fit at mid-sized firms (SEB 2026; The Local DK 2025) [employer-stated; practitioner consensus].
2. **"Beckham makes Madrid a tax haven for graduates."** It lowers net below about €60,000 (author calculation) [data].
3. **"Expat schemes help students who stay after studying."** Sweden, Finland and Spain require five years without residence, Denmark ten years without Danish tax liability (Business Sweden; vero.fi; AEAT; skat.dk) [data].
4. **"Denmark's 3-year job-seeking permit is a given."** It changed on 1 October 2026 for new study permits (SIRI) [data].
5. **"FT salary is Madrid or Lisbon pay."** It is a three-year, PPP, sector-weighted mean of respondents; Nova's $131,640 and Católica Porto's $55,831 are both Portuguese (FT 2026) [data].
6. **"Sweden's 24% youth unemployment means graduates struggle."** Its recent graduates are employed at 91.1% (Eurostat 2025) [data].

## Decision rules

1. If you are an EU citizen who can reach working Swedish within two years and want a corporate, consulting or finance path, prefer Stockholm (≈€2,240 a month left at SSE's mean pay; 38% of international SSE graduates stay).
2. If you target Denmark, prefer English-language corporate programmes (Novo Nordisk: Danish not required; September 2027 applications open 6 December 2026) over generic applications to mid-sized Danish firms.
3. If you are non-EU and study in Denmark from now on, assume a 1-year job-seeking window and plan for a job at or above the DKK 552,000 Pay Limit.
4. If you are non-EU and study in Spain, plan the direct switch to a work permit (art. 190, RD 1155/2024) and secure a written offer before the study permit ends.
5. If you want Beckham in Spain, do not study there first without advice, and do not count on it below about €60,000 gross.
6. If you are under 35 and choose Portugal, check your IRS Jovem year-count before accepting an offer (≈€7,000 a year at €45,000 in year one).
7. If you target Finland, check the €5,800 monthly threshold and five-calendar-year rule; at entry pay the expert regime does not apply.
8. If you compare Nordic or Iberian offers, rank by net minus rent: at €45,000, Helsinki > Stockholm > Madrid ≈ Porto > Oslo ≈ Lisbon > Copenhagen (author calculation).
9. If you choose a Spanish or Portuguese school for a local career, ask for pay split by country of work; FT figures do not give it.
10. If you compare Madrid with Milan, expect about €500 more left per month in Madrid at €60,000 gross, but neither is a strong base for saving without an international-facing role.
