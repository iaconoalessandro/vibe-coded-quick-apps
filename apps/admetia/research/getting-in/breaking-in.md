# Breaking in: non-targets, internationals and career switchers

## Bottom line

1. **A referral is the biggest lever a candidate controls, and it works mostly at the screening stage.** In one large US firm, referred applicants were 6% of applicants but 21% of interviewees and 29% of hires (Brown, Setren & Topa, 2016) [data]. In a large retail bank, referred applications were about five times as likely to lead to an interview (Fernandez & Galperin, 2012, as cited by Brown et al.) [data]. But the referral effect was measurably weaker for jobs with higher education requirements, and structured bank and consulting graduate schemes still put referred candidates through the same tests. A referral gets the CV read. It does not pass the numerical test or the case interview.
2. **Moderately weak ties are the most useful contacts.** In a five-year LinkedIn experiment on about 20 million users, ties with around 10 mutual connections produced the most job moves; the very weakest and the strongest ties produced fewer (MIT/LinkedIn, Science, 2022) [data]. The practical target is alumni, former colleagues' colleagues and friends of friends, not strangers and not only close friends.
3. **Cold outreach is a volume game with low conversion, and it works best for small firms.** Practitioner rules of thumb: a 10–25% response rate to good cold emails, about half of responders agreeing to a call, and a non-target student needing roughly 1,000–2,000 emails over months versus a few hundred for a target student (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2019, updated) [anecdotal: single practitioner source]. Direct cold emails asking for internships work mainly at boutiques (a few dozen to 50+ bankers) and much less at large banks (150–200 bankers per offer) (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2021) [anecdotal].
4. **A top master's re-opens the door but does not by itself open it.** The most detailed practitioner source (Mergers & Inquisitions, across several articles) says a pre-experience MSc at LSE, LBS, Imperial, HEC, Bocconi, St. Gallen and similar schools makes a non-target graduate eligible for European IB and consulting recruiting, but only if it is paired with a relevant internship before or during the programme (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2017 and 2022, updated 2026) [anecdotal: one publisher, consistent across articles]. No bank or MBB firm states on the record that it screens on the most recent degree only; see "Claims to verify".
5. **Name-based discrimination is measurable, and it varies a lot by origin.** In Britain, applicants of Western European or US origin got callbacks at a rate close to white British applicants (20.2% vs 24.1%), while MENA, Black African and South American/Caribbean-origin applicants got about 12–13%, even though all were British-educated (CSI Nuffield, 2019; fieldwork 2016–17) [data]. In Germany, a Turkish-sounding name cut callbacks for student internships by about 14%, and the gap disappeared when a favourable reference letter was attached (Kaas & Manger, IZA, 2010) [data]. France ranks as the highest-discrimination labour market of nine countries in a 97-study meta-analysis (Quillian et al., 2019) [data].
6. **For an Italian or other white EU candidate, the bigger barriers are language, timing and visas, not names.** Local offices in Milan and Frankfurt expect fluent Italian or German for full-time roles, and applications to London from continental Europe are often routed to local offices first (M&I reader case, 2011) [anecdotal]. Post-Brexit visa costs for EU citizens in the UK are covered in places/visas-and-work-rights.md.
7. **Back-door routes are real but narrow, and they depend on moving within 1–3 years.** Practitioner accounts say Big 4 transaction services, valuation, corporate banking and boutiques can feed IB, mostly into middle-market and boutique banks first, and that the window closes after about 2–3 years of experience in another field (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2020) [anecdotal: one publisher]. Back and middle office to front office is described as hard for IB and more feasible for trading.
8. **CV norms are country-specific, and getting them wrong is an avoidable error.** UK guidance says omit photos and date of birth (Prospects) [practitioner consensus], while the EU's own Europass template recommends a professional photo (Europass, 2026) [employer-stated, EU body], and German applications have traditionally carried a photo (Weichselbaumer, IZA, 2016, notes job seekers "typically attach their picture") [data, context statement]. For UK and US finance and consulting, use one page, no photo, grades visible.
9. **The market has shrunk, which probably raises the value of back doors (untested).** Graduate recruitment at the UK's 100 leading employers fell 24.5% from 2022 to 2025, with a further 0.5% cut targeted for 2026 (High Fliers, 2026) [data]. Fewer structured places mean more candidates for each one, and relatively more value in off-cycle, lateral and boutique routes.

## 1. Referrals: what the data says, and what it does not

**The peer-reviewed evidence.**

| Study | Setting | Main finding | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brown, Setren & Topa (2016), JOLE | One large US corporation, all staff levels | Referred: 6% of applicants, 21% of interviewees, 27% of offers, 29% of hires. Initial wage premium that fades by year 3. Longer tenure. Effects stronger at lower skill levels. | [data] |
| Fernandez & Galperin (2012), cited in Brown et al. | Large retail bank, repeat applicants | Referral applications about 5x as likely to produce an interview | [data] |
| Burks, Cowgill, Hoffman & Housman (2015), QJE | Nine large firms in call centres, trucking and high-tech | Referred applicants more likely to be hired and to accept; similar skills; 10–30% less likely to quit (about 30% in high-tech); better on rare "high-impact" metrics such as patents | [data] |

Sources: Brown et al. IZA DP 8175 (https://repec.iza.org/dp8175.pdf); Burks et al. IZA DP 7382 (https://docs.iza.org/dp7382.pdf).

**What this means for a business-school candidate (non-obvious).** The Brown et al. data show that the referral advantage is concentrated at the interview stage: the share of referred candidates jumps from 6% of applicants to 21% of interviewees, then only rises to 29% of hires. In other words, a referral mostly buys a read of the CV and an interview slot. After that, candidates compete on performance. The same paper finds "sizable, significant" referral effects for positions with lower education requirements, and weaker ones higher up (Brown et al., 2016) [data]. For graduate IB and consulting schemes, with online tests, video interviews and assessment centres, the referral is best understood as a way past the CV filter, not a way past the tests.

**Employer and platform statistics.** LinkedIn's own HR analytics team says referrals are its "most efficient way to hire" and that referred hires stay longer and perform better; after launching its referral tool in 2015, LinkedIn hired 11.4% more referred candidates and filled referred roles in 54 days instead of 67 (LinkedIn Talent Blog, 2016) [employer-stated, about LinkedIn as an employer]. When LinkedIn launched its "Ask for a referral" button, trade press reported that applicants were four times more likely to get a response with a referral (HR Dive citing TechCrunch, 2018) [anecdotal: secondary, unverified original]. The widely repeated "referred candidates are 4x more likely to be hired" line appears to trace back to LinkedIn marketing material; treat it as a marketing claim, not a study.

**Do banks and MBB run referral programmes for students?** Most large employers run internal employee-referral schemes, but I found no bank or MBB careers page that describes a special referral track for campus applicants. Goldman Sachs' student pages describe programmes and a single online application route (Goldman Sachs Careers, 2026) [employer-stated]. Practitioners describe the working mechanism as informal: an analyst or consultant "passes your CV along" or flags your application to HR, which raises the chance of a first-round interview (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2019) [anecdotal]. See "Claims to verify" for the MBB referral question.

## 2. Cold networking: what works and how often

**Mechanics that practitioners agree on (tagged by source strength).**
- Target Analyst to VP (or consultant to manager) first; they answer more often than senior staff. Use them to get referred to senior people (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2019) [anecdotal].
- The sequence is: informational call → "mini-ask" (pass on the CV, introduce a colleague) → "real ask" (put me into the process) (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2019) [anecdotal].
- Response rates of 10–25% with a good template; about 50% of responders take a call; non-targets need 1,000–2,000 emails, targets a few hundred (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2019) [anecdotal: single source].
- Cold emails that ask directly for an internship work best at boutiques; drop a firm after about three unanswered contacts; expect a few dozen to 50+ bankers per boutique offer and 150–200 at large banks (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2021) [anecdotal].
- Cold emails work better if you already have a known brand on the CV (school or employer). A well-known regional university can be enough even if it is not top-50 nationally (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2021) [anecdotal].

**The weak-ties evidence supports the "alumni first" rule.** The LinkedIn experiment (about 20 million users, 2015–19, 600,000 job moves) found an inverted U: moderately weak ties, around 10 mutual connections, were best. Weak ties helped most in digital, fast-changing industries such as tech (MIT News on Rajkumar et al., Science, 2022) [data]. So: alumni of your school or master's, people from the same home town or previous employer, and second-degree LinkedIn contacts.

**Europe differs.** An Italian non-target candidate who won IB internships in London, Milan and Frankfurt reported that he "did not do much networking" and won on technical preparation and one prior contact (M&I reader case, 2011) [anecdotal]. The structured European graduate process (online tests, assessment centres) leaves less room for networking to override the screen than US recruiting does. Networking matters most for boutiques, off-cycle roles and local banks that do not run structured programmes.

**Career-service guidance (Imperial).** Imperial's Careers Service tells international students that well-known graduate schemes "can attract very high volumes of applications" and that "networking to find 'hidden' opportunities is strongly encouraged" (Imperial College Careers Service, accessed 2026) [practitioner consensus: career service]. Its guidance on speculative applications says many start-ups and SMEs rely on direct approaches, that "many large companies do not accept speculative applications", and that applicants should write to a named person (a department head or someone at the right level) rather than a generic inbox, keep it brief, and track every approach (Imperial, accessed 2026) [practitioner consensus]. This matches the practitioner view above: speculative approaches convert at small firms and mostly produce information and contacts at large ones. I could not read equivalent LSE or HEC pages (LSE's careers pages are rendered by script; HEC's returned 403), so they are not cited.

## 3. CV norms by country

| Market | Length | Photo | Date of birth / age | Grades | Tag / source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK (all sectors) | "No longer than two A4 pages" for general graduates | Omit (unless acting or modelling) | "Irrelevant", omit | List qualification type and grades | Prospects [practitioner consensus] |
| UK/US finance and consulting | One page for students ("1 page unless you are applying to Managing Director-level positions") | Never in the US; acceptable only where local practice expects it | No | Always show GPA or UK class (e.g. "2.1 cumulative average"); omitting it signals a bad grade | M&I resume template [anecdotal: single source, but the one-page norm is universal in bank templates] |
| EU generic (Europass) | Not specified | "Add a professional photograph" | Template allows it | Yes | Europass (EU), 2026 [employer-stated] |
| Germany | Tabular Lebenslauf | Traditionally attached | Traditionally included | Grades and reference letters (Zeugnisse) carry weight | Weichselbaumer, IZA 2016 (context) [data]; Kaas & Manger 2010 on reference letters [data]. | Italy / France | One to two pages | Common in practice | Common in practice | Final grade (e.g. 110/110) or average often expected | Not verified from a primary source; see Claims to verify |

**Non-obvious consequence.** In a German field experiment where photos were part of the application, a Turkish-named woman shown wearing a headscarf got callbacks from 4.2% of firms, against 13.5% for the same Turkish name without a headscarf and 18.8% for a German name (Weichselbaumer, IZA DP 10217, 2016) [data]. A photo is therefore not a neutral formality: it adds information that employers can and do use.

**Grades.** A candidate who recruited in London, Milan and Frankfurt reported that Milan and Frankfurt offices asked specifically for GPA, while London HR "just scanned it quickly" (M&I reader case, 2011) [anecdotal].

**Length.** Imperial's UK guidance says a good CV should "fill one or two whole pages" (Imperial, accessed 2026) [practitioner consensus]. The UK general-graduate norm is therefore two pages, but the finance and consulting norm is one. That split trips up many European applicants who send a two-page or Europass CV to a London bank.

**How long recruiters look.** The often-quoted figures (6 seconds in the 2012 Ladders eye-tracking study, 7.4 seconds in the 2018 update) could not be verified: the PDF returned 403 to every fetch. The study was run by a job-board company on a small sample of recruiters, so treat it as a marketing statistic in any case. A banking practitioner source puts the figure at "30 seconds" for IB CVs (Mergers & Inquisitions, resume template) [anecdotal]. The robust conclusion does not depend on the exact number: the first screen is quick and pattern-based, so school, employer names, grades and dates must be findable at a glance. Imperial also says a short "personal profile" should be included when applying to larger firms "that may use AI in their recruitment processes" (Imperial, accessed 2026) [practitioner consensus].

## 4. LinkedIn

**How recruiters search.** LinkedIn's Future of Recruiting 2025 report (survey of 1,271 recruiting professionals in 23 countries, September 2024, plus platform data) says companies whose recruiters use the skills filter most are 12% more likely to make a "quality hire", and 37% of organisations were integrating or experimenting with generative AI in recruiting (LinkedIn, 2025) [employer-stated: LinkedIn about its own platform]. The practical implication: recruiter search is increasingly keyword- and skills-driven, so the skills section and job titles need the words a recruiter types ("financial modelling", "DCF", "market sizing", "SQL", "brand management").

**Profile factors with some evidence.**
- Photo: LinkedIn says members with a profile photo "receive up to 2X more profile views" (LinkedIn Help, accessed 2026) [employer-stated]. Imperial notes that a photo is "generally expected" on LinkedIn, "unlike a UK CV" (Imperial, accessed 2026) [practitioner consensus]. The older "21x more views" figure seen on many blogs is not what LinkedIn's help page now says.
- Completeness: LinkedIn says a complete profile increases "discoverability and profile search appearances" but gives no number (LinkedIn Help) [employer-stated].
- Headline: 120 characters; make it factual, not a slogan (Imperial) [practitioner consensus].
- Skills and recommendations: up to 50 skills; endorsements and recommendations feed LinkedIn's search ranking (Imperial) [practitioner consensus].
- Referral button: LinkedIn added "Ask for a referral" beside job posts in 2018, which lets a candidate see which openings are at companies where they have connections (HR Dive, 2018) [data: product description].

**Non-obvious point.** For a non-target candidate, LinkedIn's main value is not being found by recruiters. At graduate level, banks and MBB rarely source students from LinkedIn. Its value is finding the moderately weak ties (alumni, same home town, same previous employer) that the MIT/LinkedIn experiment shows are most useful, and checking that your profile says the same as your CV when a contact looks you up.

## 5. Does a top master's "re-target" a non-target undergrad?

**Practitioner lists of European targets include master's programmes.** Mergers & Inquisitions lists London/Europe Master's in Finance targets as LSE, LBS, Imperial, Oxford, Cambridge, Bocconi, HEC, SSE, WHU, RSM, IE, CBS, ESCP, St. Gallen, Esade and "a few others" (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2022, updated 2026) [anecdotal: single source, but consistent with school employment reports covered in getting-in/employer-pipelines.md].

**The consensus view is "necessary but not sufficient".**
- The MSF is "the best last-minute path" but "not a panacea"; it helps most when you already have some finance coursework or internships, and "would make sense … only if you could complete finance internships before or during the program" (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2017, updated) [anecdotal].
- Its main uses are "re-branding" plus better access to internships ("contact firms and say you're an incoming or current MSF student at University X") (same source) [anecdotal].
- For a Big 4 auditor with one year of experience and a non-target European undergrad, a top MSF "might be more helpful … if you did not attend a target university" and is "normally best" used "to improve your academic background and/or restart the recruiting process" (Mergers & Inquisitions, audit-to-IB article comments, 2015, updated 2026) [anecdotal].
- Location matters: if the goal is London IB, a London programme makes networking easier (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2022) [anecdotal].
- Age is not the constraint in Europe; years of work experience are. Two or three years can still lead to Analyst roles; ten or more cannot (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2017) [anecdotal].

**Where the master's re-target fails.** A non-target MSF is "still an uphill battle"; an MBA from a non-target is worse (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2022) [anecdotal]. Engineers with no finance experience have "almost 0%" chance of moving directly into IB even with a master's, unless they add a finance role or internship (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2017) [anecdotal].

**Employer statements.** I found no bank or MBB careers page that says, on the record, whether screening uses the most recent degree or the undergraduate degree. Goldman Sachs' student pages invite applications through a single online portal and speak of seeking "people with diverse skill sets, interests, and experiences" (Goldman Sachs Careers, accessed 2026) [employer-stated], which says nothing about school filters. The honest position is that the "re-target" effect is inferred from practitioner accounts and school employment reports (see getting-in/employer-pipelines.md), not from employer statements. The practitioner advice that CVs list most recent education first, and that recruiters then see both degrees, suggests the undergraduate school is still read, just discounted.

## 6. International students: language, visas and names

**Hiring discrimination by name: the field experiments.**

| Country | Study (fieldwork) | Design | Result | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK | CSI Nuffield, Heath & Di Stasio (Nov 2016–Dec 2017) | ~3,200 real jobs, British-born or raised applicants, identical CVs | Majority 24.1% callbacks; Western Europe/US origin 20.2%; Eastern Europe 15.6%; India/East Asia 19.4%; Pakistan/Bangladesh 13.6%; MENA 12.7%; Sub-Saharan Africa 12.3%. Minorities overall needed 60% more applications | [data] |
| Germany | Kaas & Manger, IZA DP 4741 (2010) | 528 student-internship ads, paired German/Turkish names | German name raised callback probability by about 14% (24% at small firms); gap vanished with favourable reference letters | [data] |
| Germany | Weichselbaumer, IZA DP 10217 (2016) | 1,474 applications with photos | German name 18.8%; Turkish name 13.5%; Turkish name with headscarf 4.2% | [data] |
| 9 countries | Quillian et al., Sociological Science (2019) | Meta-analysis of 97 field experiments, 200,000+ applications | France highest discrimination, then Sweden; in high-discrimination countries white natives get nearly twice the callbacks of non-whites | [data] |

Two non-obvious points from the CSI data:
- **Western Europeans were close to parity in Britain overall, but not men.** Women of Western European/US origin were treated about the same as the majority (24.8% vs 26.5%), while men of Western European/US origin were not (15.7% vs 21.8%, ratio 1.4) (CSI Nuffield, 2019) [data]. That is relevant for Italian, Spanish and French men applying in the UK, though the study used UK-educated applicants and all occupations, not finance graduate schemes.
- **High-skill roles showed smaller gaps for some groups only.** Gaps were smaller for jobs requiring a degree for white minorities and "model minorities", but applicants from Africa, MENA and South America/Caribbean faced strong discrimination even in high-skill roles (CSI Nuffield, 2019) [data].

The Kaas & Manger result is the most actionable for a business student: it was run on student internships, and the penalty disappeared when a positive reference letter was attached. This is consistent with statistical discrimination (employers filling an information gap) rather than pure taste. Adding credible third-party signals (reference letters, known employer names, a local degree) should shrink the penalty (Kaas & Manger, 2010) [data; the inference for finance roles is mine].

**Language.** Local offices want the local language for full-time roles: fluent Italian in Milan, fluent German for full-time IB in Frankfurt, though German was not required for a Frankfurt internship (M&I reader case, 2011) [anecdotal]. In London, extra European languages are "viewed very highly" (same source) [anecdotal]. For cross-border deal teams, a home-country language and network can be an asset (Mergers & Inquisitions, international students, 2016) [anecdotal].

**Visas.** The first goal for a non-EU international "has to be winning an internship at a firm that can sponsor your visa and then converting it" (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2016) [anecdotal]. Boutiques are less likely to sponsor visas, which makes the boutique back door weaker for non-EU candidates (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2022) [anecdotal]. Rules by country: see places/visas-and-work-rights.md.

**France and the Netherlands.** France's large-firm testing study (DARES with ISM Corum, 2021) and later national testing waves are referenced on ISM Corum's site, but the DARES pages returned a CAPTCHA, so their figures are not used here (see Claims to verify). The meta-analysis evidence is enough for the decision: France ranked as the highest-discrimination country of the nine studied (Quillian et al., 2019) [data]. The Netherlands was among the lower-discrimination group in the same meta-analysis [data]; I did not read a Dutch primary study. None of these studies isolates graduate finance or consulting schemes, which mostly use online tests before a human reads the name. That may reduce the name effect at the first screen but not at later human stages; this is my inference, not a finding.

Why laterals exist at all: "the turnover rate in investment banking is extremely high: many full-time Analysts quit before their first year ends", and as undergraduate recruiting accelerates, lateral hiring "has become increasingly important" (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2020) [anecdotal]. Lateral slots open unpredictably, so the candidate has to be continuously networking.

## 8. Pivots between fields and countries

The common thread across practitioner accounts is a **stepping-stone rule**: a pivot works when each move is one step from the last, and within 2–3 years. Direct jumps across two gaps (field and level, or field and country) rarely work.

| Pivot | Typical working route | Evidence and tag |
|---|---|---|
| Engineering → IB | Engineering → Big 4 / valuation or a top MSF → IB | Direct move "almost 0%" (M&I, 2017) [anecdotal] |
| Engineering → consulting | Direct application | Easier than IB because consulting "hire[s] entire classes each year" (M&I, 2017) [anecdotal] |
| Audit → IB / PE | Audit → TS or valuation → IB; audit → PE more common in Europe than in the US | "Audit is too removed to make a direct jump" (M&I lateral hiring, 2020); Europe note (M&I, 2011) [anecdotal] |
| Audit → corporate finance / FP&A | Direct, often internal at a client or via the firm's advisory arm | Plausible at 1–3 years (M&I Big 4 TS, 2020) [anecdotal] |
| Bank operations / middle office → front office | Trading more feasible than IB; ops also a route to strategy consulting | Reader case: middle-office trade support to trading; ops experience helps for consulting (M&I, 2010) [anecdotal] |
| Wealth / asset management → IB | Hard, because no deal experience | (M&I lateral hiring, 2020) [anecdotal] |
| Marketing → consulting | Via MBA, or via a master's in management for early-career candidates | No primary or specialist source read; see Claims to verify |
| Country pivot (continental Europe → London) | Local degree or London MSc; apply to London and expect routing to the local office | Reader case: London applications from continental Europe screened by local offices first (M&I, 2011) [anecdotal] |
| Country pivot (Europe → US) | US master's with STEM designation (36 months OPT), then sponsorship | M&I international students (2016) [anecdotal]; rules in places/visas-and-work-rights.md |

**Age versus experience.** Practitioners say European banks care about years of full-time experience, not age, because five-year degrees are normal; 2–3 years can still lead to Analyst roles, 10+ cannot (M&I, 2017) [anecdotal]. Past about 3–4 years in another field, the realistic route to IB becomes an MBA (M&I lateral hiring, 2020) [anecdotal].

## 9. Competitions and societies

- The CFA Institute Research Challenge is an annual global equity research contest for teams of 3–5 undergraduate or graduate students, with local, sub-regional, regional and global rounds from autumn to May, and each team gets an industry mentor, "preferably CFA charterholders" (CFA Institute, 2026) [employer-stated]. The mentor contact is the most tangible networking benefit.
- Practitioners say competitions help most for undergraduates and recent graduates, can be listed as "Work & Leadership Experience", and sometimes lead to spring weeks or internships, especially for non-targets who otherwise get little bank attention (Mergers & Inquisitions, 2012, updated 2026) [anecdotal].
- L'Oréal describes Brandstorm as its "global innovation challenge" and "a practical way to get noticed and open doors at L'Oréal" (L'Oréal Careers, accessed 2026) [employer-stated]. The careers page does not promise internships or jobs to finalists, and the Brandstorm site blocked automated access, so prize and hiring terms are in Claims to verify.
- No public source I could read gives a conversion rate from competition finalist to hire, for Brandstorm, the CFA Research Challenge or consulting case competitions. The evidence supports competitions as a CV line, a source of mentor and recruiter contacts, and interview stories, not as a pipeline.
- Student finance and consulting societies: no employer data found. Their demonstrable value is access to alumni (the moderately weak ties above), training for technical interviews, and early information on spring weeks and insight days (see getting-in/recruiting-calendar.md).

## Hypotheses tested

**H1. A top master's largely "re-targets" a non-target undergrad for European IB and consulting.** Verdict: **partly supported.**
- Holds when: the master's is on the practitioner target lists (LSE, LBS, Imperial, Oxford, Cambridge, HEC, Bocconi, St. Gallen, SSE, RSM, Esade, IE and similar), it is in or near the hiring city (London for London IB), and the candidate adds a relevant internship before or during the programme (M&I, 2017 and 2022) [anecdotal, consistent across several articles and answers].
- Fails when: the candidate has no finance or consulting experience at all, has more than about 3 years of unrelated work, or the programme is itself a non-target ("still an uphill battle") (M&I, 2022) [anecdotal].
- Gap: no employer states on the record that it screens on the latest degree. The claim rests on practitioner consensus and school employment reports.

**H2. Employee referrals are the largest single lever a non-target candidate controls.** Verdict: **supported for getting interviews, partly supported for getting offers.**
- Evidence: referred applicants were 6% of applicants but 21% of interviewees and 29% of hires in one large firm (Brown et al., 2016) [data]; referral applications about 5x as likely to reach interview at a retail bank (Fernandez & Galperin, cited in Brown et al.) [data].
- Fails when: the process is test-gated (online numerical and situational tests, then assessment centres), which is the norm for large European graduate schemes. The referral effect also shrinks for jobs with higher education requirements (Brown et al., 2016) [data].

**H3. Name-based discrimination measurably reduces callbacks for some applicants in the UK, Germany and France.** Verdict: **supported, but strongly origin-dependent.**
- UK: MENA, Sub-Saharan African and South American/Caribbean-origin applicants got about 12–13% callbacks against 24% for the majority; Western European/US-origin applicants were close to parity overall, but not men (CSI Nuffield, 2019) [data].
- Germany: about 14% fewer callbacks for Turkish-named applicants for student internships, vanishing with a favourable reference letter (Kaas & Manger, 2010) [data]; strong penalty for a headscarf photo (Weichselbaumer, 2016) [data].
- France: highest discrimination of nine countries in the meta-analysis (Quillian et al., 2019) [data].
- Limits: data are mostly 2010–2017 and possibly stale; no study isolates graduate finance or consulting schemes.

**H4. Lateral moves from boutiques are a more reliable route into bulge-bracket IB than direct graduate applications for non-targets.** Verdict: **partly supported.**
- Holds when: the move is one tier at a time (boutique → middle market → bulge bracket or elite boutique), in a high-turnover hub such as London or New York, within 1–3 years (M&I lateral hiring, 2020) [anecdotal].
- Fails when: the boutique is unknown and small ("not … directly to GS, MS, or JPM"), the candidate needs visa sponsorship (boutiques rarely sponsor), or the candidate is not already in IB ("probably not going to win" a bulge-bracket or elite-boutique offer) (M&I, 2020 and 2022) [anecdotal]. It is more reliable than a direct application only for candidates who would be screened out of structured schemes anyway.

**H5. Photos and age on a CV hurt applications in the UK and US but are expected in Germany and Italy.** Verdict: **partly supported.**
- UK and US: omit photo and date of birth (Prospects; M&I "never include your photo … in the US") [practitioner consensus].
- Continental Europe: the EU's Europass template recommends a professional photo [employer-stated, EU body]; German job seekers "typically attach their picture" (Weichselbaumer, 2016) [data, context]. I could not verify Italian or French norms from a primary source.
- Non-obvious: "expected" does not mean "harmless". Where photos are normal, they transmit ethnic and religious signals that measurably change callbacks (Weichselbaumer, 2016) [data]. And international firms' London-run schemes follow UK norms even for applicants in Milan or Frankfurt.

**H6 (mine). For white EU citizens, language and local-office routing are bigger barriers than name discrimination.** Verdict: **partly supported.**
- Western European-origin applicants got 20.2% callbacks against 24.1% for the majority in Britain, a much smaller gap than for visible minorities (CSI Nuffield, 2019) [data]. Local offices required fluent Italian (Milan) and German for full-time roles in Frankfurt, and London applications were routed to local offices (M&I reader case, 2011) [anecdotal].
- Fails for men: male Western European/US-origin applicants in the UK faced a 1.4 callback ratio (CSI Nuffield, 2019) [data].

**H7 (mine). Cold outreach converts mainly at small firms; at large firms it mostly buys information.** Verdict: **supported by practitioner sources.**
- Boutique offers from a few dozen to 50+ contacts versus 150–200 at large banks (M&I, 2021) [anecdotal]; Imperial notes that "many large companies do not accept speculative applications" (Imperial, 2026) [practitioner consensus].

**H8 (mine). Reference letters and known employer names can partly neutralise the foreign-name penalty.** Verdict: **partly supported (one German study).**
- In Kaas & Manger (2010), discrimination disappeared when applications included reference letters with favourable personality information [data]. The generalisation to other countries and to finance roles is my inference and untested.

**H9 (mine). A shrinking graduate market raises the relative value of back doors.** Verdict: **insufficient evidence for the causal claim; the market contraction itself is data.**
- UK top-100 graduate recruitment fell 24.5% from 2022 to 2025 (High Fliers, 2026) [data]. I found no data on whether off-cycle or lateral hiring grew in the same period.

## Common mistakes and myths

1. **"A referral gets you the job."** It mostly gets you an interview; offers still depend on performance (Brown et al., 2016) [data]. In test-gated schemes, prepare for the numerical and situational tests first.
2. **"Networking means messaging senior people."** Junior-to-mid bankers (Analyst to VP) respond more; use them to reach seniors (M&I, 2019) [anecdotal]. Moderately weak ties beat strangers (MIT/LinkedIn, 2022) [data].
3. **"Sending a Europass or two-page CV to a London bank is fine."** UK and US finance and consulting expect one page, no photo, grades visible; omitting a GPA is read as a very bad one (M&I resume template) [anecdotal]; UK guidance says drop photos and date of birth (Prospects) [practitioner consensus].
4. **"The master's alone will fix a non-target undergrad."** Without a relevant internship before or during the programme, the MSF "isn't a panacea" (M&I, 2017) [anecdotal].
5. **"A back-office job at a big bank is a foot in the door to IB."** Moving from the back or middle office to IB is hard; it is more feasible for trading, and easier before accepting a full-time back-office offer (M&I, 2010 and 2011) [anecdotal].
6. **"Audit is a route into IB."** Only via a stepping stone such as transaction services or valuation; audit is "too removed" for a direct jump (M&I, 2020) [anecdotal].
7. **"Studying for the CFA will make up for a weak CV."** An international student who broke into New York IB called spending the first MSF year on the CFA and FRM a "silly" strategy compared with getting internships (M&I, 2016) [anecdotal].
8. **"Discrimination is the same for everyone with a foreign name."** It varies hugely by origin and gender (CSI Nuffield, 2019) [data].
9. **"The 6-second CV scan is a scientific fact."** It comes from a job-board company's small eye-tracking study that I could not verify. The conclusion (make key facts scannable) holds; the number is marketing.
10. **"LinkedIn recruiters will find me."** For graduate schemes, banks and MBB rarely source students there; LinkedIn's value is finding alumni and keeping the profile consistent with the CV (inference from sources above).

## Decision rules

1. **If** the target employer runs a structured, test-gated graduate scheme (bulge-bracket bank, MBB, large FMCG), **and** you are from a non-target, **prefer** getting one referral per firm plus heavy test preparation over mass cold emailing (because referrals raise interview rates but the tests decide offers; Brown et al., 2016).
2. **If** you are from a non-target and have no finance internship, **prefer** cold outreach to 50+ bankers at boutiques over large banks (because boutique offers need a few dozen to 50+ contacts versus 150–200 at large banks; M&I, 2021).
3. **If** you are choosing whom to contact, **prefer** people with about 10 mutual connections (alumni, same previous employer, same home town) over strangers or close friends (because moderately weak ties produced the most job moves; MIT/LinkedIn, 2022).
4. **If** you have a non-target undergrad **and** want European IB or MBB, **prefer** a target MSc in or near the hiring city **only if** you can complete a relevant internship before or during it (because practitioners say the degree without experience "isn't a panacea"; M&I, 2017).
5. **If** you have more than about 3 years of unrelated full-time experience **and** want IB, **prefer** a top MBA over a pre-experience master's (because Analyst-level laterals and MSF routes close after about 3–4 years; M&I, 2020).
6. **If** you are in Big 4 audit and want IB, **prefer** an internal move to transaction services or valuation within 1–2 years, then a move to a boutique or middle-market bank (because audit is "too removed" for a direct jump, and TS works best at 1–3 years; M&I, 2020).
7. **If** you land a boutique IB role **and** want a bulge bracket, **prefer** moving one tier at a time (boutique → middle market → bulge bracket) in London or New York (because turnover and lateral openings are highest in hubs; M&I, 2020).
8. **If** you are non-EU and need visa sponsorship, **prefer** internships at large firms that sponsor over boutique back doors (because boutiques rarely sponsor; M&I, 2016 and 2022). See places/visas-and-work-rights.md.
9. **If** you apply to UK or US offices (including London-run schemes for continental offices), **use** a one-page CV, no photo, no date of birth, grades shown in the local system. **If** you apply to a local German employer, **include** reference letters and follow local format (because favourable references removed the name penalty in Kaas & Manger, 2010).
10. **If** your name signals a visible-minority origin **and** you apply in France or the UK, **plan** for roughly 1.6–2x the applications of a majority candidate for the same number of callbacks, and front-load third-party signals (known employers, references, a local degree) (CSI Nuffield, 2019; Quillian et al., 2019).
11. **If** you target Milan or Frankfurt full-time IB, **require** fluent Italian or German respectively; **if** you target London, **prefer** listing extra European languages prominently (M&I reader case, 2011).
12. **If** you are Italian, French or German and graduating, **consider** a 6-month post-graduation internship at a local bank or boutique as the main route to a local full-time role (because local banks without structured schemes convert interns directly; M&I reader case, 2011).
13. **If** you are choosing a competition, **prefer** ones with industry mentors or recruiter judges (CFA Research Challenge, company-run cases such as Brandstorm) over trading-return contests, and **list** it as experience (because the benefit is contacts and stories, not a hiring pipeline; M&I, 2012; CFA Institute, 2026).
