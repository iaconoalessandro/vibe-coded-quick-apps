# Application and interview craft

## Bottom line

1. **Structured interviews are the best-supported selection method, and unstructured chats the weakest.** Sackett et al. (2022) put structured interviews first at .42 and unstructured at .19 (down from .51 and .38 in Schmidt & Hunter 1998); cognitive tests fell from .51 to .31. Treat every interview as scored against criteria [data].
2. **No evidence supports paid case or banking-interview coaching, and the hour counts are vendor lore.** Practice on cognitive tests gives about .26 SD, flattening after roughly three attempts; I found no study of case coaching, and employers publish no quantities. Use the firms' free, official cases and tests first [data; practitioner].
3. **Employers allow AI for preparation and polishing but ban it in live assessments and interviews.** Deloitte, PwC, McKinsey and Goldman (EMEA programme) say so, with withdrawal or disqualification as the sanction; EY, BCG and McKinsey explicitly allow CV/application help. Detection is not the control: OpenAI withdrew its classifier and detectors misread non-native English [employer-stated; data].
4. **Only St. Gallen's SIM publishes numeric admission weights, and the video interview is the heaviest (35%) against 20% for GMAT/GRE and 20% for grades; activities are 25%.** Elsewhere schools say "holistic" and list components [employer-stated].
5. **Schools' official advice is consistent: be programme-specific, skip ranking and city boilerplate, respect the limits.** Limits range from about 4,000 characters (Bocconi) and one page (ESCP) to 500 + 350 words (Imperial) and 1,000-1,500 words (LSE), so a single letter cannot be reused [employer-stated].
6. **Consulting processes are not case-only.** BCG, Bain and McKinsey publish behavioural/fit or PEI stages alongside the case, and BCG's page defines the case as reasoning quality, not a single right answer [employer-stated].
7. **No employer publishes psychometric pass marks or a technical-question syllabus.** The "75th-85th percentile" and "400 questions" claims in circulation have no employer source in what I found.
8. **Read the contract before the salary.** France caps cadre probation at 4 months (8 with renewal and consent) and CDD at 18 months in general; Italy allows 12 months fixed term without a reason; Germany limits no-reason fixed terms to 2 years and bars them after prior employment, with up to 6 months' probation at 2 weeks' notice; Spain allows up to 6 months' probation for degree-holding technicians [official pages].
9. **Graduate pay negotiation evidence is thin and mostly American.** Treat base pay in cohort schemes as fixed unless the employer says otherwise; ask for start date, relocation and clawback terms in writing [anecdotal / secondary].

Why this matters:
- **Only St. Gallen publishes numeric weights** among the schools I read, and its video interview carries more weight (35%) than the test (20%) or the grades (20%). This is the clearest official evidence that a "soft" component can outweigh GMAT for some programmes. It is one programme (SIM); other HSG masters weight the test differently (search summary: Banking and Finance 30% for the test, not verified on the page).
- Elsewhere the schools say "holistic" and name components without weights. Bocconi's four-part statement is the most explicit: test, GPA, dossier, motivation.
- The recurring official advice across LSE, Imperial and ESCP is the same: be specific to the programme, do not recite rankings or school facts, do not recycle another school's text, state the career link. This is employer-stated and consistent, so it is safe to present as the rule.

### 1.2 Practical points the schools state

- **Length varies by an order of magnitude.** Bocconi about 4,000 characters; ESCP one page; Imperial 500 + 350 words; LSE 1,000-1,500 words. Reusing one letter across schools breaks at least one limit and, per Imperial, risks naming the wrong programme.
- **References are cheap to get wrong in two ways.** HEC wants an academic referee; LBS prefers a professional one; St. Gallen wants one of each on the CV; Esade accepts either. Plan referee type per school, not once.
- **Video assessments sit at different points.** LBS uses video after the interview for waitlisted candidates; IE uses it as a core step; St. Gallen uses it as a graded 35% component. Practising on camera helps only for schools where it is scored.
- **Interview style is conversation, not case.** HEC (30 min), ESSEC (45 min), ESCP and LBS describe motivational/background interviews. The business-school case-style or technical Q&A that students fear is not described in these official pages (LBS's blog-level mentions of a mini-case are for the MBA and unverified for MiM).
- **Who evaluates:** LBS says alumni or staff, scored against the same criteria. ESSEC uses a jury of at least two.

### 1.3 Common errors, as stated by the schools themselves
Rankings recital and "London is cosmopolitan" boilerplate (LSE); copying another school's statement or using the wrong programme name (Imperial); paper-thin programme knowledge (ESSEC blog: research the programme, tracks, courses); missing dates on the CV (ESCP). These come from official or school-run blogs [employer-stated]; no school publishes error statistics.

Reading across employers:
1. **Every large employer publishes the stage list but none publishes pass marks.** SHL's candidate site, for example, states its 23 free practice tests "do not attempt to provide you with an exact experience" of the real assessment and says nothing on cut-off scores (https://www.shl.com/shldirect/en/practice-tests/, read in full). The "75th-85th percentile cut-off" in the Gemini lead has no traceable employer source in what I found; treat it as folklore (see Corrections).
2. **Behavioural content is everywhere**, even in case-heavy processes (BCG's own alumna quote: "BCG interviews also include a behavioral portion"; Bain: operating principles). Case-only preparation is a known blind spot, and it is directly contradicted by the employers' own page structure.
3. **Video screens are now standard** at banks (Goldman, J.P. Morgan) and are a scored 35% component at St. Gallen's SIM (section 1). Employers describe them as structured, but none published scoring criteria in the pages I read.
4. **Tech roles add coding screens** (Goldman engineering via HackerRank; Bain coding challenge). See careers/tech-data-and-ai.md for the content.
5. **Regulatory backdrop (EU):** emotion-recognition in the workplace is already prohibited under the EU AI Act, and AI used to screen or evaluate candidates is "high-risk" (Annex III). The original 2 Aug 2026 start for high-risk duties was reported as postponed to 2 Dec 2027 under the May 2026 Digital Omnibus agreement, subject to formal adoption (Gibson Dunn summary, May 2026, https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/; not fetched) [practitioner consensus, secondary]. Practical meaning for an EU applicant: recorded-video AI scoring will face transparency and human-oversight duties, but today the protection is mostly GDPR and employer policy. HireVue states it removed facial analysis from its assessments in 2021 (SHRM/EPIC coverage) [practitioner consensus].

### 2.1 The first screen in practice: applicant tracking systems and knockout questions

Most large employers receive applications through an applicant tracking system (ATS) such as Workday, Greenhouse or Taleo. Sources here are recruiter-facing and job-seeker guides rather than the vendors' manuals, so tag everything below [practitioner consensus, secondary] and check each employer's own application page.

1. **The CV is parsed into data that recruiters search and filter.** Poor parsing mostly hurts discoverability rather than triggering a rejection. Guides agree that Workday is the least tolerant of unusual layouts and Greenhouse the most tolerant, so a single-column CV with standard headings (Experience, Education, Skills), contact details in the body rather than a header or footer, and no tables or text boxes is the safe default. A designed two-column template can still parse on some systems; the cost of the plain format is close to zero.
2. **Automatic rejection comes from knockout questions, not from a hidden CV score.** The recurring questions are work authorisation, visa sponsorship, location or relocation, minimum years of experience and licences. They are set by the recruiter and applied to the answer you type. Greenhouse's documentation is reported to say that users alerted to new applications "will not be notified of auto-rejected applicants", so a wrong tick can end the application silently.
3. **Answer sponsorship questions truthfully, but know what you are answering.** For an EU or EEA citizen applying to a continental employer the question is usually irrelevant. For a UK role the right answer depends on your status; the Graduate route (see places/visas-and-work-rights.md) allows work without sponsorship for its duration, so "I will need sponsorship" is a different statement from "I need a visa today". Read the exact wording of the question and ask the recruiter if it is ambiguous. The figure sometimes quoted for how fast the rejection arrives ("within 30 seconds") has no source and is not used.
4. **Give dates as month and year** for degrees and roles, because screens compare graduation date and experience length.

Employers name stages ("online assessment", "digital interview", "game") that are delivered by a small set of vendors. ### 2.3 What "no evidence for paid coaching" does not mean

The evidence summary in section 4 says there is no study showing that paid case or banking coaching raises offer rates, and that vendor benchmarks such as "40-60 mock cases" have no research source. That is a statement about **paid coaching and invented thresholds**, not permission to skip preparation. Employers publish that they assess technical ability and structured reasoning (Goldman Sachs, BCG, Bain pages in section 2), and behavioural or case-only preparation is a known blind spot. For banking and finance interviews the technical core that candidates are commonly expected to explain is [practitioner consensus; no employer page listing it was read]:

- how the three financial statements link (a change in net income or depreciation flows to the cash flow statement and the balance sheet);
- the bridge from enterprise value to equity value (net debt, minorities, leases) and which multiples pair with which value measure;
- the logic of a discounted cash flow (free cash flow, WACC, terminal value) and what drives it;
- basic accounting and valuation questions on your own CV and internships.

For behavioural and fit questions, the common structure is STAR (situation, task, action, result): one concrete example, your own action, and a measurable result, prepared for each competency the employer names [practitioner consensus]. For consulting, the published expectation is a structured approach, quick quantitative handling and clear communication in a case (BCG and Bain pages in section 2). Case formats differ: in an interviewer-led case (associated with McKinsey) the interviewer moves you through set questions, while in a candidate-led case (common at BCG and Bain) you drive the structure and choose what to analyse next; offices vary, so ask the recruiter [practitioner consensus]; practising with free official materials (section 6) and a few live practice sessions covers it without any paid programme. The honest summary: prepare the content, do not buy certainty.

## 4. Evidence on selection quality

### 4.1 Structured vs unstructured interviews (H-a)

The two reference meta-analyses disagree on the size of the numbers but agree on the ranking.

| Predictor of job performance | Schmidt & Hunter 1998 (operational validity) | Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens 2022 (revised) |
|---|---|---|
| Structured interview | .51 | .42 |
| Unstructured interview | .38 | .19 |
| General mental ability (cognitive tests) | .51 | .31 |
| Work sample | .54 | .33 |
| Assessment centre | .37 | .29 |

Source: Table 3 and the "changes" discussion in Sackett et al., Journal of Applied Psychology 107(11), 2022, read in full from the author-posted PDF (https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/meta-analysis/2021-sackett.pdf; abstract also at the APA record) [data: peer-reviewed meta-analysis]. The 1998 column is the values Sackett et al. quote from Schmidt & Hunter (Psychological Bulletin 124(2), 1998). The authors state that the older figures overcorrected for range restriction, so "mean validity estimates reduced by .10-.20 points", and that "structured interviews emerged as the top-ranked selection procedure" (abstract, 2022) [data].

What the numbers mean in practice:
- A validity of .42 is a correlation with supervisor-rated job performance. It is useful, not a crystal ball: it explains under 20% of the variance in performance ratings.
- The 1998 headline that cognitive tests are "the best predictor" (.51) no longer holds in the 2022 re-estimate (.31), so structured interviews overtook them. Sackett et al. caution that estimates for several predictors rest on thin or old studies [data].
- Unstructured interviews fell by half (.38 to .19). The conversation-style "fit" interview is the weakest of the commonly used methods, and the one candidates most often assume matters most.
- "Structured" means the same job-related questions, asked of every candidate, scored against anchored rating scales. A firm calling its process "structured" does not guarantee this; the validity figure applies only to genuinely standardised interviews. This is an inference from how the meta-analyses define structure (McDaniel et al. 1994 and Huffcutt et al. 2014 are the underlying sources).

Implication for candidates: behavioural/competency interviews and case interviews at large firms are usually scored against rubrics, so answers that map to the stated criteria (below) are rewarded; charm without evidence scores poorly in a structured format.

### 4.2 Practice, retesting and coaching (H-b)

| Study | What it covers | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Hausknecht, Halpert, Di Paolo & Moriarty Gerrard, J. Applied Psychology 2007 | 50 studies, 107 samples, 134,436 participants, cognitive ability tests | Adjusted overall practice effect .26 SD; larger when practice came with test coaching and when the identical form was reused. Authors note 25-50% of applicants in organisational and educational settings are retested (Cornell eCommons copy, abstract read in full: https://ecommons.cornell.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/4fbba765-0d9c-4940-8492-66fecaca5227/content) [data] |
| Scharfen, Peters & Holling, Intelligence 2018 | 174 samples, 122 studies, 153,185 participants | Significant retest effects; "no further score gains after the third test administration"; size depends on test content, form equivalence, retest interval and age (abstract read in full, from https://www.Gwern.net/doc/iq/2018-scharfen.pdf) [data] |

Reading: retaking or practising the same type of test raises scores by roughly a quarter of a standard deviation, with diminishing returns after about three attempts. Coaching adds more than bare repetition, but the abstracts I could read do not give a clean, independent "coaching on top of practice" effect size, and none of this is about case interviews. For the school tests that matter to this site (GMAT/GRE) see getting-in/admissions.md.

I found **no peer-reviewed study** of paid case-interview coaching or of banking-technical coaching and offer rates. The "40-60 mock cases" benchmark in the Gemini lead comes from a coaching vendor, not research (see Corrections). Maurer, Solamon & Troxtel (J. Applied Psychology 1998; extended 2001) studied coaching and situational interviews; the search result only identified the papers, and I could not read their results, so I do not report an effect size [anecdotal-level for this file: not verified].

One caution that cuts the other way: if practice raises scores on a cognitive screen without raising the underlying ability, the test is a weaker signal for firms (Hausknecht et al. flag the open question of the impact of retesting on validity). The practical consequence for candidates is to treat timed psychometric tests as trainable skills, and the cheapest way to train is the publisher's own free practice test (section 6).

### 4.3 AI-assisted applications and detection (H-c)

- Employer side: ISE 2025 figures already in the library report 79% of employers redesigning or reviewing selection and 48% worried about AI misrepresentation (see the ISE entry in the brief; not re-fetched here).
- Detection accuracy: OpenAI withdrew its own AI Classifier in July 2023 for "low rate of accuracy"; at launch it correctly flagged 26% of AI text and wrongly flagged 9% of human text (OpenAI statement as quoted in Quartz/Inquirer coverage, July 2023: https://qz.com/chatgpt-openai-inaccuracy-classifier-detection-tool-1850677501; OpenAI's own page returned 403 to my fetch) [practitioner consensus, secondary].
- Bias: Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu & Zou, Patterns 2023, tested seven detectors; the abstract (read in full, https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819) says they "consistently misclassify non-native English writing samples as AI-generated" and caution against use in evaluative settings. The 61.3% average false-positive rate on 91 TOEFL essays comes from press coverage of the paper (Times Higher Education via search result), not from the abstract I read [data, figure secondary]. For an Italian or other non-native applicant this is the relevant risk: honest work can look "AI-like" to a detector.
- Employers' practical response is not detection but process: ban AI in live assessments and ask for the candidate's own voice (section 5), and move to live or proctored formats.

## 7. Reading an offer: contracts, probation, notice, bonus

Official legal rules for the three contract systems most of this site's readers will meet, re-read on primary pages on 1 Oct 2026. These are national statutory defaults; collective agreements (CCNL in Italy, convention collective in France, Tarifvertrag in Germany, convenio in Spain) can change them within legal limits. This is information, not legal advice.

| Issue | France | Italy | Germany | Spain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed-term contract: general cap** | CDD maximum 18 months in general, with exceptions by purpose (replacement, 9 months waiting for a permanent hire, 24 months for some cases). Allowed only for listed temporary reasons (replacement, temporary increase in activity, seasonal work, etc.) (service-public.gouv.fr F36, verified 21 Aug 2026) | 12 months without justification; up to 24 months only with a reason: collective agreement, "technical, organisational or productive needs" agreed by the parties (valid until 31 Dec 2026 in the text I read) or replacement; above the limit the contract converts to permanent (Art. 19 D.Lgs. 81/2015, normattiva.it) | Without a material reason up to 2 years, with at most 3 extensions; not allowed if the employee previously had any fixed-term or permanent employment with the same employer (§ 14 Abs. 2 TzBfG, gesetze-im-internet.de) | Production-circumstance contracts limited to 6 months (extendable to 12 by sector agreement) and 90 days a year for occasional situations (Art. 15 Estatuto de los Trabajadores, BOE; summary of the fetched page) |
| **End-of-contract payment** | At least 10% of total gross pay on CDD end, unless the employee is hired permanently or refuses an equivalent CDI; collective agreements may reduce to 6% with training compensation (service-public F803, search summary) | Not applicable as a statutory bonus; employer pays an extra INPS contribution (1.4%, plus 0.5 points per renewal; Gemini lead, unverified here) | Not applicable | Not read |
| **Probation** | CDI: 2 months (workers/employees), 3 months (supervisors/technicians), 4 months (cadres); renewable once only if the sector agreement allows, the contract says so and the employee agrees in writing, giving 4/6/8 months. Notice before ending probation: 24 hours (<8 days), 48 hours (8 days to 1 month), 2 weeks (1-3 months), 1 month (after 3 months). CDD: 1 day per week of contract, up to 2 weeks (to 6 months) or 1 month (longer) (service-public.gouv.fr F1643, updated 12 Aug 2026) | Set by written agreement and collective agreements; statutory ceiling under R.D.L. 1825/1924 reported as 6 months for managerial categories and 3 months for others (secondary summary of Art. 2096 Codice Civile and CCNL texts; verify against your CCNL) | Agreed probation up to 6 months with 2 weeks' notice; otherwise basic 4 weeks to the 15th or end of a month (§ 622 Abs. 1 and 3 BGB, read in full) | Up to 6 months for "técnicos titulados", 2 months for others, 3 months for non-graduates in firms under 25 staff; 1 month in temporary contracts of up to 6 months (Art. 14 Estatuto, BOE) |

What this means for a graduate offer:
- **France:** a 4-month cadre probation, renewable once to 8 only with sector-agreement basis and your written consent, is the legal ceiling. An offer that starts with 8 months or a renewal you did not sign is not valid on the published rules. Ask whether the role is a CDI or a CDD and why; a CDD must fit a listed reason.
- **Italy:** a 12-month fixed term with no stated reason is lawful; past 12 months the employer needs a legal reason, and past 24 months the contract becomes permanent. Check the CCNL, which sets probation and notice, and note that internships (tirocinio extracurriculare) are a separate regime, not an employment contract; see places/italy-playbook.md and money/salaries-and-roi.md for pay levels, not repeated here.
- **Germany:** a "sachgrundlos" fixed term cannot follow earlier employment with the same company, so a prior fixed-term job or earlier contract with the group can invalidate it (check whether an internship counted as employment with your lawyer; I did not verify how courts treat internships). Probation is up to 6 months at 2 weeks' notice, the weakest period for you.
- **Spain:** graduates ("técnicos titulados") can face up to 6 months of probation; fixed-term production contracts are short.
- **Non-compete clauses:** the Gemini lead states that Italian, German and French post-employment non-competes need paid consideration (Art. 2125 Codice Civile; § 74 HGB; Cour de cassation 2002). I did not re-read these statutes; the rules are widely stated but figures such as the German "at least half of last pay" and the Italian "15-35%" are for lawyers to confirm. Read any non-compete line before signing, and ask whether it is paid.

### What is negotiable for graduates
- **Evidence is thin and mostly American.** A search surfaced NACE-based figures that about 38% of recent US graduates negotiated their offers, and an employer survey (reported by Diverse Education, not fetched) where three-quarters of employers said they usually have 5-10% room, and 84% said a candidate would not lose an offer by negotiating [practitioner consensus, secondary, US, unverified]. Do not apply these to European structured graduate schemes.
- **Cohort schemes (large banks, consultancies) usually publish a single entry salary**, and practitioner sources say base pay is not negotiable while start date, relocation help or signing bonus sometimes are. I found no employer statement of this, and the sources in the Gemini lead are vendor blogs; label as [anecdotal / practitioner].
- **Where negotiation is more plausible:** start date, location, rotation, remote days, training and exam support, relocation, and any sign-on bonus. Ask in writing and compare against the national legal minimums above, not against forum anecdotes.
- **Clawbacks:** if an offer includes a sign-on or relocation bonus repayable on early exit, ask for the repayment period and whether the repayment is gross or net. The Gemini lead's UK HMRC mechanics (EIM00805) were not re-read; check with HMRC guidance.

## Hypotheses tested

**H-a. Structured interviews predict job performance better than unstructured ones.** Verdict: **supported**. Holds when "structured" means the same job-related questions, scored on anchored scales. Fails as a guide to any specific firm's process, because the label is not audited. Evidence: Schmidt & Hunter 1998 (.51 vs .38) and Sackett et al. 2022 (.42 vs .19; structured interviews ranked first) [data]. The gap widened in the newer estimate.

**H-b. Paid coaching materially improves case-interview or test outcomes.** Verdict: **insufficient evidence** (not supported for case interviews). Holds, modestly, for cognitive-test practice: about .26 SD from practice, more with coaching, flattening after about three attempts (Hausknecht 2007; Scharfen 2018) [data]. Fails as a claim about case or banking interviews: I found no study, and McKinsey says coaching for Solve is "not required or necessary" (search summary of its pages) [employer-stated, secondary]. The "40-60 mock cases" benchmark is vendor advice [anecdotal].

**H-c. Employers can reliably detect AI-written applications.** Verdict: **not supported** as a general claim. OpenAI withdrew its own classifier (26% detection, 9% false positives at launch); detectors misclassified non-native English essays in a peer-reviewed test [data, partly secondary]. Employers rely on bans in live stages and on follow-up questions, not detection. Holds only in the narrow sense that live questioning exposes content the candidate cannot discuss.

**H-d. (own) The more "holistic" the school's language, the less a candidate can rely on test scores.** Verdict: **insufficient evidence**, partly supported. St. Gallen's SIM publishes 20% test, 20% grades, 25% activities and 35% video interview, so a strong video can outweigh a strong GMAT. But only one school publishes weights in what I read; the others state components without weights [employer-stated].

**H-e. (own) Case-heavy preparation is enough for a consulting offer.** Verdict: **not supported**. BCG's page and a BCG alumna warn that interviews include a behavioural portion; Bain runs a fit interview on past experience and its operating principles; McKinsey runs a PEI [employer-stated]. Fails only for pure screening stages that are cognitive or game-based.

**H-f. (own) Employers ban generative AI in the recruitment process.** Verdict: **partly supported**. Holds for live interviews and assessments (Deloitte, PwC, Goldman EMEA programme, McKinsey) [employer-stated]. Fails for preparation and CV or application polishing, which Deloitte, EY, BCG and McKinsey explicitly allow, and for roles where the employer asks you to use AI (McKinsey's US pilot; PwC case exceptions).

**H-g. (own) One strong motivation letter can be reused across schools.** Verdict: **not supported**. Limits range from about 4,000 characters (Bocconi) to 1,000-1,500 words (LSE); Imperial warns against pasting other statements and wrong programme names; LSE says to avoid generic London/ranking statements and to write a separate statement per programme [employer-stated]. Holds for the core stories and facts, not the text.

**H-h. (own) Fixed-term and probation terms in a European graduate offer are the same everywhere.** Verdict: **not supported**. France caps cadre probation at 4+4 months with conditions; Germany at 6 months with two weeks' notice; Spain up to 6 months for degree-holding technicians; Italy depends on the collective agreement [official pages, section 7].

## Common mistakes and myths

1. **Myth: a long, passionate letter beats a tailored short one.** Schools say the opposite: be specific, avoid rankings and city boilerplate, name the right programme (LSE, Imperial, ESCP) [employer-stated].
2. **Myth: the interview is a casual chat.** Structured interviews predict best; unstructured ones are the weakest. Treat each interview as scored against criteria [data].
3. **Myth: you need 50 mock cases.** Employer pages say nothing about quantities; the number comes from coaching vendors [anecdotal]. Dose matters less than feedback on structure and communication, the criteria BCG and Bain publish.
4. **Myth: AI detectors will catch you, so write nothing with AI; or the opposite, that nobody can tell.** Both are unsupported. The real risk is live follow-up and the stated sanction (withdrawal or disqualification at PwC, Goldman) [employer-stated].
5. **Mistake: treating the psychometric score as a secret pass mark.** Nobody I found publishes it. Practise on official samples so the format is not the obstacle [employer-stated].
6. **Mistake: one referee set for every school.** HEC asks for an academic referee, LBS prefers professional, St. Gallen wants one of each on the CV [employer-stated].
7. **Mistake: negotiating base pay in a cohort scheme as if it were a lateral hire.** Evidence is practitioner-level only; ask about start date, relocation and bonus instead [anecdotal].
8. **Mistake: accepting a fixed-term contract without checking the legal reason and limit.** France and Italy tie CDD/tempo determinato to reasons or durations; Germany bars a no-reason fixed term after earlier employment with the same firm [official].
9. **Mistake: ignoring non-native-English risk with AI-polished text.** Detectors misclassified non-native writing in published tests, so heavy rewriting can look suspicious even if honest [data].

## Decision rules

1. If you apply to 4+ schools, write a **fact bank** (stories, numbers, projects) once, then rewrite each letter to that school's limit and prompts (Bocconi ~4,000 characters; Imperial 500 + 350 words; LSE 1,000-1,500 words; ESCP one page), because limits and prompts differ by school.
2. If a school is St. Gallen SIM, **practise the pre-recorded video interview as seriously as the GMAT**, because it carries 35% against the test's 20% in the published weights.
3. If a school asks for one academic referee (HEC) and another prefers professional (LBS), **pick referee type per school** and brief each referee with the prompts, because the schools define the type.
4. If you plan consulting, **split preparation 50/50 between case and behavioural/fit stories**, because BCG, Bain and McKinsey all publish a behavioural or PEI component (this is a heuristic, not an evidence-backed ratio).
5. If you are told to buy a case-coaching package or a "400 questions" guide, **use the firm's own cases and the standard textbooks first**, because I found no study showing paid coaching improves offers and McKinsey says coaching is unnecessary for Solve.
6. If you face a timed cognitive screen, **do the publisher's free practice test once or twice**, not ten times, because retest gains are about a quarter of an SD and flatten after about three attempts.
7. If you use generative AI, **use it for research, structure, proofreading and practice questions; never in live assessments or to write answers**, because Deloitte, PwC, McKinsey and Goldman state this and the sanction is withdrawal or disqualification.
8. If your English is non-native and you want to polish with AI, **keep a dated draft in your own words and be able to explain every sentence**, because detectors misclassify non-native writing and live follow-ups are the real check.
9. If a firm calls an interview "structured", **expect rubric-based scoring and prepare criterion-by-criterion**; if the process is a loose conversation, expect noisier outcomes and bring more concrete evidence.
10. If you receive a CDD/fixed-term offer in France, **ask for the legal reason in writing and check the 18-month cap and the 10% end-of-contract payment**; in Italy, **check the 12/24-month limits and the CCNL**; in Germany, **ask whether you previously worked for the group** (no-reason fixed terms are barred after prior employment).
11. If your offer has a long probation (France cadre 4+4, Germany up to 6 months), **plan finances for early termination with short notice**, because probation notice is shortest in that period (Germany: 2 weeks).
12. If you want to negotiate a graduate scheme offer, **ask for start date, relocation and sign-on terms, and for the clawback repayment terms in writing**, not base pay, because published evidence supports flexibility only at the edges.
13. If a source on this topic gives a precise pass mark, hours or question count with no employer or study behind it, **do not model it**; show the employer's own published criteria instead.
