# How schools, rankings and the press mislead with numbers

## Bottom line

1. **Most misleading numbers in this market are technically true.** The trick is in what's left out: the denominator, the response rate, the time window, the comparison group or the currency adjustment. Our job is to put the missing part back, not to call anyone a liar.
2. **Ask four questions of any outcome number.**
   - **Of whom?** Who's in the denominator, and who was left out.
   - **How many answered?** The response or coverage rate.
   - **When?** At graduation, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year or 3 years.
   - **Compared with what?** The same student without this school.
3. **Verified examples:**
   - Bocconi's MSc International Management shows "100% employed at three months" in the 2026 FT table, but the figure covers 55% of the class.
   - In the same table, IIM Ahmedabad's "weighted salary" is $199,706, a PPP-adjusted figure; Poets&Quants reports actual starting pay under $45k.
   - Kozminski's "salary increase" of 78.72% beats Mannheim's 27.86% mainly because Kozminski graduates start from lower pay.
   - French grande-école employment figures at 30 months rest on a 36.6% response rate.
4. **Rankings move more from noise than from quality below the top 30.** In the FT MiM table, the median programme moved 7 places between 2025 and 2026 (the top 30 alone moved a median of 4), and 34 of 85 moved 10 or more. A press release saying "up 12 places!" is usually weather, not climate.
5. **The biggest hidden fallacy is selection.** Schools take strong students, then credit themselves for strong outcomes. The classic study (Dale & Krueger, NBER w7322) found students who attended more selective colleges did not earn more than equally able students admitted to comparable schools who went elsewhere. Newer evidence finds real effects at the very top for access to elite firms (Chetty et al., QJE 2026), so the honest answer is "smaller than the brochure implies, larger at the extreme top".
6. **The press makes the mirror-image mistakes:**
   - it treats job adverts as jobs (one UK board showed −40%, another −0.5%);
   - it treats applications per vacancy as odds (140 per vacancy does not mean 1-in-140);
   - it recycles zombie statistics (the "AI will cut junior banking classes by two-thirds" claim has no on-record source).
7. **Good practice exists, and we say so.**
   - LBS publishes its reporting rate (96–98%) and both "received" and "accepted" offer rates.
   - The CGE publishes its exact formula and its response rates by cohort.

   Credibility comes from showing both sides.
8. **We apply the same standard to ourselves.** This library corrected its own errors in public: a mis-read GMAC chart, a 91% that is really 90.5%, and an outdated fee. See the correction log in §7.

## How to use this file on the site (tone rule)

- **Say "misleading" or "incomplete", never "lie" or "fraud"**, unless a regulator, court or the institution itself has said so. Naming a school is fine when the figure is quoted accurately from its own publication or an official table, with date and link. Characterising intent isn't. This is both fairer and legally safer (see product/product-map.md §7).
- **Always show the original figure next to the corrected reading**, with the source and date, and say what *is* true about it.
- **Offer a right of reply and a corrections address**, and log every correction (§7).

## Part A — Employment reports: the denominator and response games

### A1. The shrinking denominator ("of those seeking employment")
- **Mechanism.** The rate is computed over a sub-group, not the whole class. Graduates who are sponsored, in further study, starting a business or "not seeking" are taken out before dividing.
- **Verified examples.**
  - *CGE (France), the official definition:* net employment rate = (employed + volunteering) ÷ (employed + volunteering + job-seeking). The CGE itself notes that this differs from a rate over all graduates. 7.3% of management graduates were in further study and so sit outside the measure (CGE Enquête Insertion 2026 report, PDF) [data].
  - *LBS:* the three-month rate is "calculated in compliance with CSEA reporting standards. Students returning to their sponsoring employers are not included". Salary data excludes "sponsored or self-employed students" (LBS MiM Employment Report 2025) [data].
- **Illustration (invented numbers, for arithmetic only).** A class of 400 has 60 graduates placed outside the denominator (further study, not seeking, no information). Of the remaining 340, 306 are employed. The reported rate is 306 ÷ 340 = **90%**. Over the whole class it's 306 ÷ 400 = **76.5%**. Both are "true". Only one is the number a prospective student needs.
- **Detect it.** Look for "of those seeking", "job-seeking graduates", "CSEA standards", or a "net" rate.
- **Ask.** "How many graduated in total, and how many were in the denominator?"

### A2. Who answered? (response and coverage bias)
- **Mechanism.** Surveys are voluntary. Graduates with good outcomes reply more, so the more people don't reply, the better the published picture looks.
- **Verified examples.**
  - *FT MiM 2026:* the "employed at three months" column carries the share of the class with data in brackets. Bocconi MSc International Management shows 100 (55); HEC Lausanne's coverage is 46%; TUM's is 15% (FT MiM 2026 table) [data].
  - *CGE 2026:* response rates fall with the horizon: 62.4% (class of 2025, at 6 months), 44.1% (class of 2024), 36.6% (class of 2023). The comfortable "~92–95% employed after two years" rests on about a third of that cohort (CGE 2026 report) [data].
  - *Good practice:* LBS reports its reporting rate: 98% (MiM 2024), 96% (MiM 2025) [data].
- **Detect it.** No response or coverage figure at all is itself a red flag.
- **Ask.** "What share of the class does this cover? What do you know about the non-respondents?"

### A3. Picking the flattering metric
- **Mechanism.** The headline uses the most favourable of several honest measures.
- **Verified example.** LBS MiM 2024: the director's introduction leads with "93% … secured an offer within three months". The same page shows 90% *accepted* an offer. MiM 2025: 92% received, 88% accepted (LBS MiM Employment Reports 2024 and 2025) [data]. To LBS's credit, both figures are published. The point is to read the second.
- **Ask.** "Received or accepted? Full-time or any contract?"

### A4. Time-window shopping
- **Mechanism.** Outcome numbers are quoted at different horizons, and longer horizons look better (and, see A2, often rest on fewer responses).
- **Verified horizons in use:**
  - LBS: 3 months after graduation.
  - CGE: 6 months, 12–18 months and 24–30 months.
  - AlmaLaurea (Italy): 1, 3 and 5 years.
  - FT: three years after graduation, for salary.
- **Rule.** Never compare a 3-month rate with a 6-month or 1-year rate. When horizons differ, the comparison is meaningless.

### A5. What counts as "employed"?
- **Mechanism.** Generous definitions inflate rates.
- **Verified example.** The CGE net rate counts *volunteering* (e.g. VIE/international volunteering, service civique) as employment. CGE salary averages count only full-time salaried employees (CGE 2026 report) [data].
- **Ask.** "Do you count internships, fixed-term contracts, volunteering, part-time or self-employment as employed?"

### A6. Means, highest salaries and bonuses
- **Mechanism.** A few very high salaries pull means up, and a "highest reported salary" works as an anchor.
- **Verified examples.**
  - LBS's MFA careers page features the "highest reported salary of £119,315" (LBS MFA career impact page, 2025) [data].
  - LBS separates base salary from "other compensation (sign-on and end-of-year bonus)", received by 56% of MiM 2025 graduates who accepted an offer (LBS MiM 2025) [data]. That's good practice. Many brochures merge the two.
- **Ask.** "Median base salary, by sector and country, excluding sign-on bonuses?"

### A7. Mix effects (Simpson's paradox in a brochure)
- **Mechanism.** An average rises because the *mix* of jobs changes, not because anyone is better off.
- **Verified example.** LBS 2025: MFA mean base £53,866 vs MiM £41,229, which looks like a "+31% finance premium". But 50% of MFA graduates went into investment banking, against 14% of MiM graduates. MFA graduates in non-finance roles averaged £44,972, essentially the same as MiM consultants (£44,571) (LBS MiM and MFA reports 2025, via decisions/programme-choice.md) [data].
- **Ask.** "What's the salary *within* the sector I want?"

### A8. Recruiter logos without volumes
- **Mechanism.** A wall of famous logos shows who hired *at least one* graduate, not where most graduates go.
- **Verified counterpoint.** RSM's 2026 report lists its top five employers by hires: Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG and Rabobank. That's a useful volume signal that a logo wall would hide (RSM MSc employment report 2026, via getting-in/employer-pipelines.md) [data].
- **Ask.** "How many graduates did each listed employer hire, in which country?"

### A9. Survivorship and testimonials
- **Mechanism.** Alumni profiles and quotes are chosen from successes. They're true, but they tell you nothing about the typical graduate.
- **Rule.** Treat any named-alumni story as an *existence proof* ("this is possible"), never as a *base rate* ("this is likely").

## Part B — Rankings: what the league tables actually measure

### B1. PPP makes cheap countries look rich (and Switzerland look poorer)
- **Mechanism.** The FT converts alumni salaries at purchasing-power parity and adjusts for sector mix. That's useful for comparing living standards, misleading for comparing cash.
- **Verified example.** IIM Ahmedabad's "weighted salary" is $199,706 in the FT MiM 2026 table [data]. Actual starting pay was reported under $45k by Poets&Quants [practitioner consensus]. In the other direction, PPP shrinks Swiss figures: HSG shows $150,490 (FT 2026) [data] despite a much larger cash lead (see money/salaries-and-roi.md).
- **Rule.** Compare FT salaries only within one country, or use national cash data.

### B2. "Salary increase" rewards a low starting point
- **Verified example.** FT MiM 2026 "salary percentage increase": Kozminski 78.72%, Luiss 65.01%, Mannheim 27.86% [data]. A high percentage often means low pre-programme pay (or none), not a stronger programme.

### B3. Noise dressed up as news
- **Verified example.** Between the 2025 and 2026 FT MiM tables, programmes in the top 30 moved a median of 4 places, while the median move across the whole table was 7, so it was larger still below the top 30. 34 of 85 programmes ranked in both years moved 10 or more places (FT MiM 2025/2026, analysis in decisions/school-types-and-accreditation.md) [data]. The FT itself groups schools into tiers.
- **Rule.** Below the top 30, ignore moves of fewer than ~15 places, and treat "up X places" press releases as noise unless sustained over three years. The FT's own three-year average rank column helps.

### B4. Self-reported data and gaming
- **Mechanism.** Alumni salaries are self-reported to a survey the school helps distribute, so incentives point one way.
- **Example (allegation only).** TBS Education was accused in 2025 of coaching alumni to inflate reported salaries (Poets&Quants, 4 Mar 2025) [practitioner consensus]. TBS is absent from the 2025 column of the 2026 FT MiM table and returns at 81st in 2026 [data]. *Accused*, not proven: say so.

### B5. Ranking shopping
- **Mechanism.** With dozens of rankings and sub-rankings, almost every school is "#1" in something. Press releases quote the best one.
- **Verified example.** Warwick's 2 April 2026 release highlights its MSc Marketing & Strategy as 1st in the UK and 6th globally in the QS Business Master's Rankings. The same release reports 72% employed within six months for that programme, against 91% for Warwick's MSc Management (wbs.ac.uk, 2 Apr 2026) [data]. Both facts come from the school. The ranking headline just isn't the outcome headline.
- **Ask.** "Which ranking, which year, which sub-list, and what's the outcome data for *this* programme?"

### B6. Participation bias
- **Mechanism.** Rankings include only schools that apply and meet the survey thresholds. Absence isn't evidence of weakness.
- **Rule.** "Not in the FT" means "didn't take part or didn't qualify", not "weak". Many strong public universities don't take part.

### B7. Accreditation as a prestige badge
- **Mechanism.** "Triple-accredited (top 1%)" is used as a quality claim.
- **Verified context.** AACSB accredits 1,000+ schools and 149 hold the "triple crown", while LSE, Oxford and Cambridge aren't on the triple-crown list (decisions/school-types-and-accreditation.md) [data]. Accreditation is a minimum quality screen and the gate to FT eligibility, not an elite marker.

## Part C — Selectivity and causation

### C1. Selection effect vs school effect
- **Mechanism.** Selective schools admit students who would have done well anyway, then publish their outcomes as the school's effect.
- **Verified evidence.**
  - Dale & Krueger (NBER Working Paper 7322) compared students accepted at similar colleges. Those who attended the more selective one "do not earn more than other students who were accepted and rejected by comparable schools but attended less selective colleges" [data].
  - Counter-evidence at the very top: Chetty et al. (QJE 2026) find that attending an Ivy-Plus college roughly triples the chance of working at a prestigious firm (via getting-in/employer-pipelines.md) [data].
- **Honest conclusion.** For most outcomes, the school effect is smaller than the brochure implies. For access to a few elite employers, it can be real.
- **Ask.** "What happens to students like me, with my grades and background, compared with similar students who chose elsewhere?"

### C2. Acceptance rates, applications and internal quotas
- **Verified examples.**
  - Bocconi reserves "approximately 65% of the total available spots" for Bocconi applicants, so outside applicants compete for about 35% (Bocconi admissions page, 2026) [data].
  - LSE's MiM page shows 1,125 applications for 75 places (2024) [data]. That's *applications*, not people. Cross-applications and repeat applications inflate both selectivity claims and fear.
- **Rule.** An "acceptance rate" without the internal quota and the applicant count is not your odds.

### C3. Employers' forecasts vs what they actually did
- **Verified example.** In GMAC's 2025 Corporate Recruiters Survey, 80% of employers predicted they would hire Master of Data Analytics graduates. In the 2026 survey, 70% said they actually had. GMAC notes that projections for business master's are "often … particularly optimistic" (GMAC CRS 2026 report) [data].
- **Rule.** Discount "employer demand" surveys by the gap between past predictions and outcomes.

## Part D — Money claims

### D1. Gross vs net vs disposable
- **Mechanism.** Salaries are quoted gross, in different currencies, ignoring tax and rent.
- **Verified example (author calculation, method in money/salaries-and-roi.md §5).** A median Swiss economics master's graduate (CHF 87,100 gross, BFS 2024) and an LBS MiM graduate in the UK (£44,675 mean base, LBS 2024) differ by about 75–85% gross. After tax and a central one-bed rent, they have roughly €3,500 vs €900 a month left.
- **Rule.** Compare "net minus rent" in the city where you'll work.

### D2. ROI and "payback" that forget the opportunity cost
- **Mechanism.** "Pays back in 2 years" claims usually compare post-degree salary with *no* salary, forgetting what you would have earned without the degree, the tax, the loan interest and the living costs.
- **Rule.** A fair ROI subtracts the counterfactual: what you would have earned with your bachelor's (see decisions/should-you-do-a-masters.md).

### D3. Static tax assumptions
- **Mechanism.** Calculators assume today's tax incentives will last.
- **Verified history.**
  - Italy's impatriati regime was cut from 2024 (D.Lgs. 209/2023): 50% exemption, a €600k cap and new conditions, against the earlier 70%/90%. It was then recodified as art. 225 of D.Lgs. 117/2026 (places/italy-playbook.md) [data].
  - The Dutch 30% facility now carries a 150 km/16-of-24-months condition and a reduced salary threshold for under-30 master's holders (€36,497 in 2026) (Belastingdienst) [data]. Its future rates are politically contested (verification/claims-to-verify.md).
- **Rule.** Show every expat-regime calculation twice: with the relief and without it.

### D4. Loans priced in a different currency
- **Verified example.** Prodigy Finance's own representative example is USD 40,000 at 13.26% APR over 15 years, repaying USD 114,661 (Prodigy, 2026, via money/costs-and-funding.md) [data]. A euro earner carries 15 years of currency risk on top.

## Part E — The press and social media

### E1. Applications per vacancy are not odds
- **Verified figures.**
  - UK employers received 140 applications per graduate vacancy and 290 in retail/FMCG (ISE, 8 Dec 2025) [data].
  - Goldman's acceptance rate is below 1% (Fortune, 8 Jun 2026) [employer-stated].
- **Why they mislead.** One person sends many applications, increasingly with AI, across many firms and divisions. Two-thirds of ISE employers kept or raised hiring, and 97% of graduate roles were filled (ISE 2025 via St Andrews Careers) [data]. "Harder than Harvard" headlines compare applications to one bank across all divisions with applicants to one college.

### E2. Adverts are not jobs
- **Verified example.** For the same UK period, Adzuna reported graduate adverts down ~40% year on year, while Career Connect reported −0.5% (St Andrews Careers summary, Nov 2025) [data]. Indeed's "−44% accountancy graduate adverts" is about adverts, not hires. Actual Big 4 intake fell 6–29% by firm (careers/accounting-and-corporate.md).

### E3. Zombie statistics
- **Verified examples.**
  - "AI could cut junior banking classes by two-thirds" circulates widely, but no on-record source was found. Goldman's CEO said AI will reduce starting numbers "probably not … dramatically" (Fortune, 8 Jun 2026) [employer-stated].
  - The Stanford "Canaries" estimate of a 13% relative decline for young workers in AI-exposed jobs (Aug 2025) is still quoted, but the authors revised it to 19% in Aug 2026 [data]. Old numbers outlive their papers in both directions.
  - Goldman's "45% STEM interns" is a 2021 figure, still cited as current (careers/finance.md).
- **Rule.** Every statistic needs a date. If a statistic is older than two years, look for the update.

### E4. Descriptive studies reported as causal
- **Verified example.** The Stanford authors describe their findings as "early, descriptive indicators … rather than causal estimates" (digitaleconomy.stanford.edu, rev. Aug 2026). Headlines turned this into "AI is killing entry-level jobs".

### E5. One firm stands in for the market
- **Verified example.** In the same months, McKinsey announced a 12% increase in North American entry hiring for 2026 (People Matters/Reuters, Sept 2025) and PwC UK cut its entry intake from 1,500 to 1,300 (Fortune, Sept 2025) [employer-stated]. Both are true. Neither is "the market".

## Part F — The 12 questions to ask any school (printable checklist)

1. How many students graduated in total, and how many are in the employment denominator?
2. What share of the class responded (or has verified data)?
3. Is the rate "received an offer" or "accepted an offer"? Full-time permanent only?
4. At what horizon: graduation, 3 months, 6 months or 12 months?
5. Do you count internships, fixed-term contracts, volunteering, further study or self-employment as employed?
6. What's the **median** base salary, excluding bonuses, by sector **and** by country?
7. How many graduates did each employer in your logo list actually hire?
8. What share of graduates work in this country vs abroad? What share of internationals stay?
9. For this specific programme (not the school's flagship): what is its own report, its class size and its admission rate?
10. What share of places go to internal (own-bachelor's) candidates?
11. Which ranking, which year and which sub-list are you quoting, and what's the three-year average?
12. Can I speak to three graduates *you didn't choose*, e.g. from the class LinkedIn list?

## Part G — Admetia's reporting standard (what we commit to)

- Every outcome figure shows **denominator, coverage, horizon, year and source**, plus one evidence tag: [data], [employer-stated], [practitioner consensus] or [anecdotal].
- **Medians over means** where available. "Highest salary" figures are never shown as a headline.
- **No cross-country PPP comparisons of cash pay.** We use national cash data or net-minus-rent.
- **"Chart-read", "snippet only" and "author calculation" figures are labelled** and never used as tool inputs until verified.
- **Whole-cohort reading.** Where a school publishes total class size and outcomes, we show the outcome over the whole class as well as the school's own rate. We never invent the gap where the data isn't published. (This adapts Gemini's "true cohort absorption" idea; Gemini's assumed 15–20% deflation has no source and isn't used.)
- **Dated corrections log**, public.

## Hypotheses tested

**M-1. "Headline employment rates in European business-school marketing overstate whole-class outcomes."** Verdict: **supported on mechanism, unquantified on size.** Denominator exclusions (CGE formula, LBS CSEA note) and falling response rates (CGE 62.4% → 36.6%) are documented in primary sources. The average size of the gap can't be computed from outside, because schools rarely publish total class plus all categories. Gemini's "15–20%" is unsourced.

**M-2. "Rankings track programme quality."** Verdict: **partly supported.** At the top, positions are stable (median move of 4 in the top 30). Across the table, year-on-year noise (median move 7 overall, 34 of 85 moving ≥10, 27 moving ≥15) swamps most quality differences. The salary inputs are PPP-adjusted and self-reported, with one documented gaming allegation.

**M-3. "Elite schools cause their graduates' higher pay."** Verdict: **partly supported.** Not supported for typical earnings among equally able students (Dale & Krueger). Supported for access to elite employers (Chetty et al. 2026).

**M-4. "Press coverage of the graduate market overstates the decline."** Verdict: **partly supported.** Advert-based and application-based headlines overstate it (E1, E2). But actual intakes and six-month employment rates did fall: UK Big 4 −6% to −29%; CGE 90.5% → 76%. "It's all hype" is also wrong.

**M-5. "Schools that publish more detail are more trustworthy."** Verdict: **insufficient evidence, but a sound working rule.** LBS (reporting rate, received vs accepted, CSEA notes) and the CGE (formula and response rates) make their limits checkable. Absence of a coverage figure is the strongest single red flag we can see.

## Common mistakes and myths

- *"95% employed means 95 out of every 100 graduates have a job."* Usually it means 95% of those in the denominator who answered, at a chosen horizon.
- *"Average salary €80k means I'll earn about €80k."* The mean includes outliers, sector mix and maybe bonuses. The median in your sector and country is the number that applies to you.
- *"#1 in the UK" means best for me.* It's one sub-ranking out of many, in one year.
- *"Triple-accredited = elite."* Accreditation is a minimum screen.
- *"1 in 140 odds."* Applications aren't applicants, and channels such as referrals and internships change the odds completely (getting-in/breaking-in.md).
- *"The press says the market collapsed, so nothing matters."* Intakes fell, but most employers still hire. Channels decide who gets in.

## Decision rules

1. **If** an employment rate has no denominator or coverage figure, **then** treat it as unknown, not as high. Ask for the total class size and response rate.
2. **If** coverage is below 70% (FT bracket or school report), **then** discount the rate and the salary, because non-respondents are more likely unplaced.
3. **If** comparing two programmes' outcomes, **then** align the horizon (3 vs 6 vs 12 months) and the definition (received vs accepted) first.
4. **If** a salary is quoted as a mean or "highest", **then** ask for the median base by sector and country before using it.
5. **If** a programme's average salary is high because many graduates went into one sector, **then** compare salaries *within* your target sector (A7).
6. **If** a ranking moved by fewer than ~15 places below the top 30, **then** ignore the move.
7. **If** a figure is PPP-adjusted, **then** don't compare it across countries as cash.
8. **If** a statistic is more than two years old or has no date, **then** look for the update before using it (E3).
9. **If** a calculator relies on an expat tax regime, **then** also look at the result without it (D3).
10. **If** a school quotes an acceptance rate, **then** also ask for the internal-candidate quota and whether the figure counts applications or applicants (C2).

## Claims to verify

1. The full list of CSEA "not seeking employment" categories and the current CSEA standards text (cseaglobal.org returned 403; LBS confirms that sponsored students are excluded).
2. The TBS salary-coaching allegation outcome: whether the FT or any regulator made a finding (only Poets&Quants reporting read).
3. IIM Ahmedabad's actual median starting pay in INR and USD for the same cohort as the FT figure (Poets&Quants figure, not primary).
4. Whether GMAT/GRE class-profile averages exclude test-waived admits at the schools in the calculator (asked as a question; not verified for any school).
5. The future rate path of the Dutch 30% facility (27% from 2027 reported, not shown on the Belastingdienst page read on 2026-10-01).
