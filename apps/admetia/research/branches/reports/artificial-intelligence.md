## 1. What this branch is

Artificial intelligence careers are about building, studying and governing systems that learn from data, from classic machine-learning models to the large language models (LLMs) behind chat assistants and agents. Most jobs are engineering jobs: training and deploying models, building products on top of foundation models, and running the GPU infrastructure that serves them. A smaller set are research, safety and evaluation roles at frontier labs, universities and AI safety institutes, and a growing set are customer-facing, product and governance roles that turn AI into deployed, compliant products. Pay at the frontier is extreme for a small elite, while the entry-level market is tough and crowded, and much AI data-labelling work is low-paid and precarious; the reports below separate these realities rather than averaging them.

Scope notes: researched in two parts (building AI systems; research, frontier and governance roles). General software engineering, data science and data analytics have their own reports. Part 2 drew most of its pay-band evidence from published US job postings, many of them Anthropic's because that employer publishes ranges; treat those figures as one employer's data points, not as the market, and see the gaps list in ../index.md.

### Part 1: Building AI systems

This part of the AI branch covers the people who build, run and ship AI systems rather than those who do frontier research, safety, policy or product strategy (those are in part 2). In practice it means five kinds of work: training and deploying machine-learning models (machine learning engineering), building products on top of large language models (LLM application or "AI engineering"), running the platforms and GPU clusters that models train and run on (MLOps and AI infrastructure), making AI see and act in the physical world (computer vision, robotics, edge AI), and producing the human-generated data that models learn from (annotation, RLHF, AI training work). The first four are engineering jobs with a strong programming core; the fifth is a very different, mostly contract-based labour market. The field has a split personality in 2026: a small elite at frontier labs earns exceptional pay, while the entry-level market for ordinary graduates is tougher than the headlines suggest.

Scope notes: (1) Compensation figures come mainly from Levels.fyi pages read on 9 October 2026; these are self-reported, small samples (tens to a few hundred submissions per page), skew toward big tech and well-paid people, and the dates on the pages are given as "10/9/2026". (2) The web search tool was unavailable for most of this research, so evidence comes from direct reads of Levels.fyi, ITJobsWatch, employer job postings (Anthropic), BLS, Stanford, SignalFire, Indeed Hiring Lab, Wikipedia (for company background, treated as secondary) and the earlier project file on tech careers. Statements on hours, stress, day-to-day work, tier lists and exit routes are practitioner consensus from my general knowledge and are marked as such rather than backed by a dataset. Where I found no figure I say "no reliable data found". (3) "Entry" means roughly 0-2 years of experience; "senior" means roughly 6-10 years.

#### P1-2.1 The employer landscape in 2026

**Frontier labs.** These are the companies training the most capable general models: OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind (inside Alphabet), Meta's AI groups, xAI, and in Europe Mistral AI (Paris). The Stanford AI Index 2026 reports that industry produced over 90% of notable frontier models in 2025, and the State of AI Report 2026 describes the frontier as a three-way race between Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. The State of AI Report also estimates that OpenAI's and Anthropic's combined annualised revenue reached about US$105 billion by late summer 2026, up from about US$30 billion at the start of the year (a report estimate, not audited figures). Labs hire engineers in large numbers, not only researchers: inference, training infrastructure, data pipelines, evaluation tooling and applied/customer-facing engineering are all engineering jobs. Mistral reportedly has more than 1,000 employees in 2026 and a valuation of about EUR 21 billion after a September 2026 Samsung stake (Wikipedia, secondary).

**Big tech AI groups.** Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, Apple and NVIDIA employ the largest absolute number of ML engineers, mostly on products (search ranking, ads, recommendations, assistants, cloud AI services) rather than on frontier models. The State of AI Report 2026 says the four US hyperscalers guide to roughly US$733 billion of combined 2026 capital spending, mostly data centres and chips, which is what funds the infrastructure jobs.

**AI infrastructure and chip companies.** NVIDIA is the dominant GPU maker; others include AMD, Google (TPUs), Amazon (Trainium/Inferentia, built by Annapurna Labs), Cerebras, plus "neoclouds" such as CoreWeave that rent GPUs. The State of AI report notes CoreWeave had 1.5 GW of active power at the end of Q2 2026. These employers hire engineers who write low-level GPU code, build compilers, network clusters and operate fleets.

**AI-native startups and application companies.** Companies built around foundation models: model providers beyond the big labs (Cohere, for example), coding tools, search, legal, health and customer-service agents, and data/MLOps vendors (Databricks, Hugging Face and similar). The State of AI report finds that among companies with US$1-20 million in annualised revenue, AI-native firms grew 256% year over year at the 75th percentile versus 90% for firms adding AI to existing software, though it warns this does not prove AI adoption causes the growth. Startups are where most "AI engineer" jobs sit, with high variance in pay and survival.

**Enterprises adopting AI.** Banks, insurers, pharma, retailers, manufacturers and telecoms run internal AI teams for fraud detection, credit risk, demand forecasting, document processing, drug discovery and copilots. The Stanford AI Index 2026 reports 88% organisational AI adoption (a survey-based measure). Euronews (24 July 2026, as summarised in the project's earlier tech file, not read in full) reports that 59% of AI-labelled job titles in Germany and 58% in the Netherlands sit outside tech occupations, which fits the picture of AI work spreading into ordinary firms.

**Consultancies and IT services.** Accenture, Capgemini, Deloitte, McKinsey QuantumBlack, BCG X, Infosys, TCS, Reply (strong in Italy), Almawave and others implement AI for clients. Mistral announced a partnership with Accenture in February 2026 (Wikipedia, secondary). Work is client-driven: you adapt existing models to a client's data, systems and politics.

**State-linked and sovereign AI.** In the Gulf, Abu Dhabi's G42 runs Stargate UAE (announced 22 May 2025 with OpenAI, Oracle, NVIDIA, SoftBank and Cisco; operations expected in 2026); in July 2026 the US reclassified the UAE to a group easing advanced-chip export rules (Wikipedia on G42, secondary). G42 also announced European sovereign-AI partnerships, including Italy, in May 2026, and reportedly pledged up to US$40 billion in Italy in June 2025 (a pledge, not delivered investment). Saudi Arabia has a state AI company, Humain; I could not retrieve a reliable source on it, so details are unverified.

#### P1-2.2 The hiring market and pay dynamics (dated data)

- **Two markets.** Levels.fyi (9 Oct 2026) shows US total compensation for an entry-level software engineer of about US$367K at Anthropic, US$249K at OpenAI (L2), US$227K at Scale AI (L3), US$205K for NVIDIA machine-learning engineers (IC1), US$208K at Google (L3, ML engineer) and US$187K at Meta (E3, ML engineer). Across all US ML engineers the median is US$280K (25th percentile US$200K, 75th US$386K, 90th US$495K). At the same time the median for the title "AI Engineer" is US$152K (25th US$110K, 75th US$211K, 90th US$280K). The gap between those two numbers is the real story: "AI engineer" at an ordinary company is a well-paid but normal software salary, while ML roles at the top labs pay several times more.
- **Private-equity caveat.** OpenAI's and Anthropic's pay includes shares in private companies. Levels.fyi lists an OpenAI all-level median of about US$880K and an Anthropic median of about US$883K, but these depend on the (illiquid, valuation-dependent) value of equity and on a small, self-selected sample. Treat them as paper values, not guaranteed income.
- **Entry-level squeeze.** SignalFire's State of Tech Talent 2026 (22 June 2026) reports entry-level hiring down about 65% at "Tech Majors" and about 76% at early-stage startups versus 2019 (SignalFire is a venture firm using proprietary data). The same report says the share of AI/ML engineers within engineering hiring is up 39% since ChatGPT's 2022 launch, research engineers up 28%, forward-deployed engineers up 30%. Indeed Hiring Lab (8 July 2026) found US software-development postings about 27.5% below February 2020 but up nearly 15% since late February 2025, with 71% of the May 2025 to May 2026 increase coming from senior roles. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab paper "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (revised 12 Aug 2026) reports that employment of 22-25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be if it had tracked less-exposed peers (ADP payroll data, US). Net: demand for experienced people who can ship AI is strong; demand for people with no experience is weak, and "AI engineer" is no exception.
- **US pull and talent flows.** The AI Index 2026 says the number of AI researchers and developers moving to the US has fallen 89% since 2017, including an 80% drop in the last year, and that US private AI investment reached US$285.9 billion in 2025, more than 23 times China's recorded US$12.4 billion (the report notes China's figure understates government-guided funds). This helps explain why Europe, the UK and the Gulf are courting talent.
- **Europe is cooler.** Bitkom (3 Sep 2026) counts 79,000 unfilled IT positions in Germany in 2026, down from 149,000 in 2023; the earlier project file notes press coverage that entry-level conditions have tightened. UK ML-engineer advertised salaries are noisy (see 3.1).
- **Elite wars.** Press reports of individual mega-offers (tens or hundreds of millions of dollars to a few researchers) are a signal about a tiny elite, not about engineers in general; I did not verify specific offers.
- **Working hours.** Wired (23 July 2025), the New York Times (28 September 2025) and NPR (26 October 2025), as cited on Wikipedia's "996 working hour system" page, report US AI startups adopting 9am-9pm, six-day schedules. That is journalistic reporting about some startups and not a measurement of the industry.

#### P1-2.3 Sub-areas

**Machine learning engineering.** Taking models from experiment to production: preparing data, training and fine-tuning models, evaluating them, packaging and deploying them, monitoring drift. Employers: every big tech company, labs, quant funds (Jane Street, IMC and similar hire ML researchers and engineers), banks, insurers, pharma, logistics, retail.

**Applied AI / LLM application engineering ("AI engineer").** Building products on foundation models via APIs: retrieval-augmented generation (RAG: letting a model look up your documents before answering), tool-using agents (models that call software functions and take multi-step actions), prompt design, fine-tuning (further training a model on your own examples), and evaluation (automated tests of output quality). Employers: AI-native startups, SaaS companies, enterprises, consultancies, and the labs' own "applied AI" teams (Anthropic lists Applied AI Engineer roles in London, Paris, Tokyo, Bengaluru and elsewhere).

**MLOps, ML platform and AI infrastructure.** MLOps is DevOps for ML: pipelines, experiment tracking, model registries, monitoring. ML platform teams build internal tools so other teams can train and deploy. AI infrastructure covers GPU clusters, distributed training, and inference (running trained models to answer requests) optimisation: batching, caching, quantisation (using lower-precision numbers to run faster), GPU kernels (small programs that run on the GPU, written in CUDA or Triton). Employers: labs, hyperscalers, NVIDIA, neoclouds, any company with a serious ML platform.

**Computer vision, robotics and edge/embedded AI.** Models that process images, video, lidar and sensor data, and increasingly robot-control models; "edge" means running on small devices (cars, cameras, drones, factory machines, medical scanners) with tight limits on power and latency. Employers: automotive and autonomous driving (Waymo, Tesla, Mobileye, Wayve in London), industrial automation, defence and drones (Anduril, Skydio and European equivalents), medical imaging (Siemens Healthineers, GE HealthCare, Philips and startups), robotics startups and chipmakers. The company names here are well-known examples; I did not verify their current hiring.

**AI data work.** People who label images, rank model responses, write ideal answers, grade reasoning or build test sets. Two layers: low-paid micro-task work through platforms, and higher-paid expert contracting (doctors, lawyers, coders, mathematicians). Employers: Scale AI (with Outlier and Remotasks), Surge AI (DataAnnotation), Mercor, Turing, Invisible, Appen, TELUS and the labs' own data-operations teams. See 3.5 for pay and precarity.

### Part 2: Research, frontier and governance roles

This part of the AI branch covers the jobs that decide what AI systems can do and how they are allowed to be used. These are the people who invent and train the models (research scientists and research engineers), the people who try to make them safe and measure their dangers (alignment, interpretability, evaluations and red teaming), the people who put them inside customers' businesses (forward-deployed engineers, solutions architects, AI consultants), the people who write the rules and manage the legal and ethical risk (governance, policy, responsible-AI and compliance staff), and the product managers who decide what gets built. Part 1 of the branch covers the more conventional engineering jobs (ML engineers, LLM application builders, MLOps, computer vision and robotics, data and annotation work).

Two things shape everything below. First, the top end of this field is small, very well paid and extremely selective: a few dozen organizations in a handful of cities (San Francisco Bay Area, London, Zurich, Paris, New York, Seattle) employ most frontier researchers, and the Stanford AI Index 2026 says industry produced more than 90% of notable frontier models in 2025. Second, the "AI governance" side is the opposite shape: it is a young, fast-growing, mostly non-technical-or-semi-technical profession driven by regulation (the EU AI Act, Italy's national AI law), with many more entry points and much lower pay.

Scope notes: (1) Date of research: 9 October 2026. US pay from Levels.fyi was read on that date (self-reported, so a signal, not a survey). Employer figures come from public job-board feeds read on 9 October 2026 (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Scale AI, Databricks, Cohere, Palantir, UK AISI) and, for Google DeepMind and Meta, from third-party mirrors of their postings (marked "mirror"; possibly expired, so signals only). (2) Posted ranges cover different seniority levels and mostly state base salary; equity and bonus come on top at most employers, so ranges across employers are indicative, not like-for-like. Where a posting does not say base or total, I treat it as base. Money is shown in the currency printed. (3) Where pages printed no pay I write "no reliable data found". (4) Hours and stress ratings are my own estimates; no survey of AI lab working hours exists that I could find. (5) "Entry comp" in the summary table means the lowest realistic full-time professional level, not a PhD stipend, unless stated. (6) Part 1 and the general software, data science, cybersecurity and PM branches are cross-referenced only briefly.

**AI research (the "science" side).** Definition: producing new methods or knowledge about how models learn and behave, for example a new training recipe for reasoning or a theory of why a method works. At a frontier lab (a company building the most capable general-purpose models, such as Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta's Superintelligence Labs and FAIR, Mistral, OpenAI, xAI) you run experiments on models from small to huge, track results and write internal documents; at a university you also teach, write grants and supervise students. Employers: frontier labs; big-tech research groups (Microsoft Research, NVIDIA, Apple, Amazon); AI companies (Cohere, Databricks, Scale AI, Sakana AI in Tokyo); universities and institutes. In Europe the main academic network is ELLIS (a pan-European network of AI research units); in Italy the main public programme is FAIR (Future Artificial Intelligence Research), a nonprofit foundation funded through the national recovery plan (PNRR) with one hub, ten "spokes" and seven transversal projects (FAIR website, read October 2026), plus IIT, FBK, and universities such as Politecnico di Milano, Sapienza and Bocconi. Industry is where the compute is: the AI Index 2026 reports that new AI PhDs in the US and Canada rose 22% from 2022 to 2024 but the whole increase went to academic jobs, not industry.

**Research engineering.** Definition: building and running the software that lets researchers train and test models: distributed training code across thousands of GPUs, reinforcement-learning (RL) environments, data pipelines, experiment tracking, evaluation harnesses. In practice the line between "research engineer" and "research scientist" at frontier labs is blurry: several lab postings (OpenAI "Research Engineer / Research Scientist" combined titles, Anthropic RL research engineer roles, xAI and Cohere "Member of Technical Staff" titles) describe one shared job of designing experiments, RL tasks and reward signals and diagnosing why models improve, not just plumbing. Employers: the same labs, plus NVIDIA, Meta, Microsoft, Databricks, Scale AI and well-funded startups.

**AI safety, alignment, interpretability and evaluations.** Alignment research tries to make models do what their developers and users intend and avoid catastrophic behaviour. Interpretability tries to reverse-engineer what happens inside a model. Evaluations ("evals") measure what a model can do, including dangerous capabilities (cyber attacks, biological misuse); red teaming means adversarially attacking a model or product to find failures. Employers fall in four groups: labs' safety teams (Google DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic all post dedicated safety research roles in October 2026, as do Scale AI and xAI); government institutes (the UK AI Security Institute, AISI, formed November 2023 and renamed in February 2025, with more than 100 technical staff and a stated budget of about GBP 66 million per year; the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation, CAISI, at NIST, which publishes evaluations of foreign AI models, the latest dated 17 September 2026; the EU AI Office's AI Safety unit); nonprofits (METR, Apollo Research, Redwood Research); and academic groups. Scale note: 80,000 Hours estimated in 2022 only about 300 people (90% interval 76 to 536 full-time equivalents) worked on technical approaches to catastrophic AI risk; the field has grown but is still small and entry "can be very competitive" (page updated 17 September 2026).

**Forward-deployed engineering, AI solutions engineering and AI consulting.** Definition: customer-facing technical work that gets AI to function inside a specific organization. A forward-deployed engineer (FDE) is a software engineer who embeds with a customer, builds production systems on the vendor's platform and feeds lessons back to the product; Palantir pioneered the role (internally "Delta"), and the Pragmatic Engineer (12 August 2025) describes solutions architects as more advisory, FDEs as more hands-on and long-term, and consultants as one-off recommenders. Employers with open FDE or deployment-strategist roles in the October 2026 job feeds: Palantir, Databricks, OpenAI, Anthropic, Cohere, Scale AI and Sakana AI, across the US, London, Paris, Munich, Zurich, Madrid, Tokyo, Seoul, Singapore, Sydney and the Gulf (Anthropic also lists "Applied AI Architect" roles in Milan); plus cloud providers and consultancies (McKinsey/QuantumBlack, BCG X, Accenture, Deloitte and similar; I could not open a primary page for these, so role details are general knowledge, unverified).

**AI governance, policy, responsible AI and compliance.** Definition: writing, interpreting and implementing the rules and internal controls around AI, from drafting national policy to running a model-risk review at a bank, building an inventory of AI systems for EU AI Act compliance, or writing a lab's product-use policy. Employers: regulators and governments (the European Commission's AI Office, with more than 125 staff across six units on its page; national authorities; UK DSIT/AISI; US agencies); think tanks (GovAI and others); corporate responsible-AI and compliance teams (big tech, banks such as Citi); Big Four, consultancies and law firms (Andersen in Italy announced an AI governance and compliance department in June 2026, per Milano Finanza); standards bodies (CEN-CENELEC JTC 21, ISO/IEC SC 42; general knowledge). The IAPP-Credo AI AI Governance Profession Report (16 April 2025; 671 respondents) found governance sits in many places: privacy and legal/compliance each lead it in 22% of organizations, IT 17%, data governance 10%; about half of governance staff sit in ethics, compliance, privacy or legal teams.

The EU AI Act timeline as of October 2026, after the "AI Omnibus" simplification (political agreement 7 May 2026, in force 27 July 2026, per the European Commission's AI Act page): prohibited practices and AI literacy duties since 2 February 2025; general-purpose AI model duties since 2 August 2025; most of the Act applies from 2 August 2026; a new prohibition on nudification apps and child sexual abuse material from 2 December 2026; high-risk systems in areas such as biometrics, employment, education and migration delayed to 2 December 2027; high-risk AI embedded in regulated products to 2 August 2028. The AI Office enforces rules on general-purpose models; national authorities share enforcement from 2 August 2026. Italy also passed its own AI law (Law 132/2025, in force 10 October 2025); I could not read which authorities it designates (widely reported as AgID and ACN, unverified). artificialintelligenceact.eu (updated 31 August 2026) lists the same later dates.

**AI product management.** Definition: deciding what AI products to build and how to ship them when the technology is probabilistic. What is distinctive versus general PM (covered in another branch): outputs vary, so quality is defined through evals; researchers' timelines are uncertain; capabilities change every few months so roadmaps are short; cost per query is a first-order constraint; and safety, abuse and trust issues are part of the product. October 2026 postings show the spread: OpenAI lists PMs for API agents, core models, integrity, youth and multimodal safety; Scale AI "Senior AI Product Manager" roles including cybersecurity and finance agents; Databricks PMs for its AI platform plus an associate-PM new-graduate programme; Cohere PMs for agents and integrations; Anthropic "Product Manager, Claude" and specialised PMs. Employers: labs, big tech, AI startups, enterprise software.

## 3. Role families

### Part 1: Building AI systems

#### P1-3.1 Machine learning engineer

**What you actually do**: You own the path from a model idea to a working service. Concretely: write training and data-processing code in Python with PyTorch (sometimes JAX or TensorFlow); build features and datasets from messy logs; train and fine-tune models; set up evaluation (for a ranking model, offline metrics such as NDCG, then online A/B tests); package the model behind an API or batch job; monitor it for drift (input data changing so the model degrades); and iterate. Deliverables are pull requests, trained model artefacts, evaluation reports, dashboards and design documents. At a bank you might rebuild a fraud-scoring model; at a social app, a ranking model; at a lab, a piece of the training or evaluation pipeline.

**A typical day and week**: Daily stand-up, then 3-5 hours of coding (data pipeline, training script, debugging a failing job), reading experiment dashboards, and code review. Weekly: a design review or reading group, a planning meeting with a product manager or data scientist, an experiment readout. A representative week: Monday you debug why last night's training run diverged (a bad data shard); Tuesday-Wednesday you implement a new feature pipeline; Thursday you present an offline result (say +1.2% in recall at the same precision) and agree an A/B test; Friday you write the deployment config and a monitoring alert. Long training runs mean periods of waiting and then bursts.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**: Typical 40-50 hours/week; peaks of 55-70 around launches, or at frontier labs and startups during model pushes (practitioner consensus; press reports of 996-style schedules at some AI startups are cited in section 2.2 and are not an industry average). Stress 3/5 at big tech and enterprises, 4/5 at labs and startups where a training run or launch has high stakes. Remote/hybrid: most big tech is hybrid (3 days in office is common); Anthropic's postings I read ask for an office presence of at least 25% of the time, which shows a flexible policy is possible at even the top labs. On-call is usually light for pure modelling roles but real for roles that own a production model.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. You need to explain trade-offs to product and business people, but the job is judged on code and model quality; you need working statistics, linear algebra and strong programming.

**Compensation (approximate)**: All figures total compensation (base + bonus + stock) unless stated; Levels.fyi, self-reported, 9 October 2026.
- US (all-level median for ML engineers): US$280K (25th US$200K, 75th US$386K, 90th US$495K).
- US entry: Meta E3 US$187K; NVIDIA IC1 US$205K (IC2 US$211K); Google L3 US$208K; OpenAI L2 US$249K (SWE page, includes applied AI titles); Scale AI L3 US$227K; Anthropic entry SWE US$367K.
- US ~5 years / senior: Google L4 US$312K, L5 US$445K, L6 US$646K; Meta E4 US$314K, E5 US$468K, E6 US$751K; NVIDIA IC3 US$315K, IC4 US$340K; Anthropic Senior US$598K, Staff US$1.13M; OpenAI L4 US$786K, L5 US$960K (private equity, see 2.2).
- UK: Levels.fyi ML engineer median GBP 103,596 (all levels, total comp). ITJobsWatch (advertised base salaries, 6 months to 9 Oct 2026) gives a UK median of GBP 67,500 (down 20.6% year on year, 92 permanent ads) and a London median of GBP 52,500 (down 41.7%, 46 ads); the small ad sample is volatile and the two sources disagree widely, so read "UK ML engineer roughly GBP 65-105K across the market" as a loose band. The London SWE median on Levels.fyi is GBP 104,526. Entry-level UK ML-specific figure: no reliable data found. The earlier project file found a median of GBP 29,000 across 104 graduate software-engineer ads, which is likely a low-end proxy for non-tech employers.
- Italy: Levels.fyi ML engineer median EUR 32,471 (all levels, small sample; treat with caution); Italy SWE median EUR 39,258 (25th EUR 31.1K, 75th EUR 49.9K, 90th EUR 65.6K, 384 submissions), Milan SWE EUR 50,465. These suggest an Italian ML engineer entry of roughly EUR 28-40K gross; that range is my inference from the Levels.fyi percentiles, unverified.
- Rest of Europe (ML engineer medians, Levels.fyi, total comp): Germany EUR 88,465; France EUR 75,949; Zurich CHF 152,282. Mistral SWE: entry EUR 104K total (23 submissions, one of the highest EU entry figures), median EUR 90.9K.
- Asia/Gulf: Singapore ML engineer median S$141,805 (Levels.fyi); Japan SWE median JPY 8.54M (Tokyo JPY 8.05M, all levels); UAE SWE median AED 337,547 (all levels). Hong Kong, China and India: no reliable data found in this research.
- The transatlantic gap is large: at entry, a US big-tech ML engineer's total pay is typically 2-4 times an Italian one's, and 1.5-2.5 times a Paris, Berlin or London one's (my arithmetic on the medians above).

**Career path**: Junior/ML engineer I (0-2 years) -> ML engineer II / mid (2-4) -> Senior ML engineer (4-7) -> Staff / Principal ML engineer (7-12) or engineering manager. Titles vary: Google uses L3-L7, Meta E3-E7, NVIDIA IC1-IC7. Many reach senior in 5-7 years; Staff is not guaranteed. Parallel track: moving toward research engineer, applied scientist or ML tech lead.

**Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: AI/LLM engineer roles at startups, ML platform or infrastructure roles, data science, a move from enterprise to big tech, a master's/PhD (less common), or a startup. After 7-10 years: staff/principal engineer, engineering manager or director of ML, chief-of-staff or founder paths, quant-fund ML roles, venture/technical product roles.

**Tier list of employers**: Tier 1: frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, xAI, Mistral in Europe) and NVIDIA; the top US quant funds' ML groups (Jane Street, Two Sigma, DE Shaw and IMC in Europe) are paid like this tier but work on trading problems. Tier 2: big tech product ML (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, Apple, Netflix, Uber, Spotify), well-funded AI-native firms (Databricks, Hugging Face, Cohere, Perplexity, Scale AI), European champions (Mistral, DeepL, Adyen-style fintechs). Tier 3: bank, insurer and pharma ML teams (JPMorgan, Intesa Sanpaolo, Allianz, Novartis and similar), consultancies (Accenture, Capgemini, Reply, QuantumBlack), industrial and automotive. Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 is distinguished by compute access, problem scale and pay; Tier 3 by stability, domain depth and lower pay.

**How to enter**: *Degrees and backgrounds.* Computer science, engineering, maths, physics or statistics. The BLS lists a master's as the typical entry degree for computer and information research scientists (median US$140,300 in May 2025, outlook +22% for 2025-35, 38,600 jobs), which is the research end of the field; for ML engineering, a bachelor's with strong projects works at many companies, a master's helps in Europe and at big tech, and a PhD is needed mainly for research-flavoured roles. Anthropic's Applied AI Research Engineer posting asks for a bachelor's "or equivalent experience" plus 6+ years; a Google DeepMind page lists research engineers as software engineers with deep ML knowledge. *Programs and residencies.* Anthropic Fellows (ML Systems and RL track): 4 months full-time, stipend US$3,850 / GBP 2,310 per week, about US$15K a month of compute, open to people with US, UK or Canada work authorisation, no visa sponsorship, and in earlier cohorts 25-50% of fellows received a full-time offer (the posting itself). NVIDIA runs year-round internships (minimum 12 weeks) and calls them its main new-graduate pipeline, plus a New College Grad program. Google DeepMind has a Student Researcher programme (details not retrievable). I could not retrieve OpenAI's careers page (access refused). *Hiring calendar.* Big-tech new-grad cycles in the US run August-November for the following year, internships recruit in autumn; European graduate schemes (Milan, Paris, Zurich) typically open September-January; startups hire year-round. *Interviews.* Typical loop: recruiter call, a coding screen (Python, data structures), ML fundamentals (bias-variance, loss functions, evaluation), an ML system-design round (design a recommender or fraud system), a project deep-dive, and behavioural; Google DeepMind describes a recruiter call, two or three skills interviews and final meetings with leads. *Certifications.* The Google Cloud Professional Machine Learning Engineer exam (US$200, two hours, 50-60 questions; Google recommends 3+ years of industry experience) is useful as a signal for enterprise and consulting jobs and mostly ignored at labs and top tech. *Portfolio.* One or two shipped end-to-end projects (data -> model -> deployed service with monitoring), a reproduced paper, open-source contributions to PyTorch, Hugging Face or similar, and Kaggle placements; the project should show you handled messy data and an honest evaluation. *Common mistakes:* collecting notebooks and course certificates with no deployed system; using only toy datasets; ignoring software engineering basics (tests, version control); applying to only the top labs. *Alternative routes:* software engineer -> ML engineer by joining an ML team internally (the most common route in 2026), data scientist -> ML engineer by learning production engineering, career changers via a master's in CS/ML or a hands-on bootcamp-plus-portfolio route (harder), non-target schools via open-source work and strong projects. Entry difficulty: 4/5

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: The entry market is tight (SignalFire and the Stanford "canaries" paper above). Work at labs and startups can be intense and often depends on the whims of one training run or a funding round. Much "ML engineering" at ordinary firms is data cleaning and pipeline maintenance, not modelling, and LLM APIs have removed the need to train models in many use cases. Pay at the top is very uneven, and equity at private labs is illiquid. Not a fit if you dislike programming and debugging, want predictable 9-to-5 certainty at top-lab pay, or want mostly client-facing work.

#### P1-3.2 AI engineer / LLM application engineer

**What you actually do**: You build software products whose core is a foundation-model call. You design and tune a RAG pipeline (splitting documents into chunks, embedding them as vectors, storing them in a vector database, retrieving the best passages, and prompting the model to answer from them with citations); build agents that call tools (search, databases, code execution, internal APIs) over many steps; write prompts and structured-output schemas; fine-tune smaller models when it pays off; and, critically, build evaluation: a test set of hundreds of prompts with automated and human grading, regression checks on every change, and cost/latency tracking. A concrete example: you build an evaluation harness of 500 test questions, retune chunk size and reranking so answer accuracy rises from 71% to 84%, and cut p95 latency (the time within which 95% of requests finish) from 4 seconds to 1.5 seconds by caching and streaming.

**A typical day and week**: Mornings reviewing failed evaluation cases and user feedback; midday building a feature (a new tool for the agent, a guardrail, a retrieval improvement); afternoon syncing with product and design. Weekly: ship an update, run the evaluation suite, review cost dashboards (token spend can dominate costs), talk to customers or support about failures. Because models change every few months, a regular task is re-testing your prompts and pipelines on a new model version.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 40-50 hours typical, 55-65 at startups near launches (practitioner consensus). Stress 3/5 (4/5 at fast startups and customer-facing applied roles at labs). Hybrid or remote is common at startups and software companies; consulting adds client travel. On-call exists if you run a production service.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You talk to users and stakeholders often and need judgment about what "good output" means, and you need solid software engineering; deep mathematics is less central than in 3.1.

**Compensation (approximate)**: Levels.fyi (9 Oct 2026), US, "AI Engineer" title: median total compensation US$152K (25th US$110K, 75th US$211K, 90th US$280K); no level breakdown was available. The wider "ML/AI" software-engineer focus has a US median of US$250K, so the labs and big tech pull the average up. Employer postings: Anthropic Applied AI Engineer, Startups (London, hybrid): GBP 225,000-255,000 base, 4+ years of experience as a software engineer, forward-deployed engineer or technical founder (Anthropic job posting, accessed Oct 2026). Anthropic Applied AI, Research Engineer (San Francisco/New York): US$300,000-400,000, 6+ years. These show that at a frontier lab "applied AI" roles are senior-and-customer-facing, not entry-level. Entry: US roughly US$110-150K total at non-lab firms (Levels.fyi 25th percentile to median); UK, Italy and rest of Europe: no reliable AI-engineer-specific data found, so use the SWE medians (Germany EUR 84,890, France EUR 58,419, Italy EUR 39,258, UK GBP 89,673 including all levels) as a ceiling rather than an entry guide. Senior (US): US$211-280K+ (75th to 90th percentile) outside the labs.

**Career path**: Software engineer / junior AI engineer (0-2) -> AI engineer (2-4) -> Senior AI engineer or tech lead (4-7) -> Staff AI engineer, head of AI at a small company, or engineering manager (7-10). Titles are unstable: "AI engineer", "LLM engineer", "applied AI engineer", "forward-deployed engineer" overlap.

**Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: ML engineer or platform roles, product engineering, a startup (your own or early-stage), forward-deployed/solutions engineering (covered in part 2). After 7-10 years: staff/principal, head of AI or CTO at a small company, founder, AI product leadership.

**Tier list of employers**: Tier 1: frontier labs' applied teams (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Mistral), top AI-native product companies (coding tools, search, productivity), where applied roles combine product and research. Tier 2: well-funded AI-native startups and big tech product teams adding AI (Microsoft Copilot teams, Google, Meta, Notion, Databricks, Hugging Face-type firms). Tier 3: enterprise AI teams (banks, insurers, pharma), consultancies and IT services (Accenture, Capgemini, Reply, Almawave, TCS) and agencies. Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 and 2 differ from Tier 3 mainly in how quickly the product is evolving and how much engineering autonomy you have; Tier 3 gives domain depth and stability but slower tooling.

**How to enter**: *Degrees and backgrounds.* The most open entry point in the branch: computer science is the default, but self-taught or adjacent backgrounds (data analytics, cybersecurity, physics, even business with strong coding) work if you can show shipped software. A master's or PhD is not required; a degree is often preferred but many postings say "or equivalent experience" (Anthropic's posting wording). *Programs.* Few formal graduate programmes target this title; entries are mainly via software engineering graduate schemes, internships and startups. *Hiring calendar.* Startups hire year-round; graduate cycles as in 3.1. *Interviews.* Take-home or live build of a small RAG or agent app, a system-design round on an LLM product (caching, evaluation, safety, cost), coding in Python/TypeScript. *Certifications.* Vendor courses (cloud AI engineer certificates, deeplearning.ai-type courses) are weak signals; a public project beats a certificate. *Portfolio.* Three or four shipped applications with live demos, each with a written evaluation (test set, metrics, failure analysis), an open-source contribution to an agent/RAG framework, a fine-tune with before/after numbers. *Common mistakes:* demos that never face real users, no evaluation, thin "wrapper" apps that any model release makes obsolete, ignoring cost and latency. *Alternative routes:* software engineers move in by building an internal LLM feature; data scientists add engineering; career changers from analytics start with automation projects. A caution: this is also the most crowded entry route in 2026, because it is the easiest to start, so differentiation matters. Entry difficulty: 3/5

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: The skill is partly commoditised and shifts every few months; a pipeline you built can be replaced by a model feature. Titles are inconsistent, pay varies hugely, and many startups die. Customer and stakeholder expectations of "AI magic" are often unrealistic. Not a fit if you want a deep, stable theoretical specialty or hate ambiguity and quality that cannot be measured with one number.

#### P1-3.3 MLOps / ML platform / AI infrastructure engineer (including inference and GPU performance)

**What you actually do**: Three layers. (1) MLOps/ML platform: build the pipelines that train, test, register and deploy models (Kubernetes, Airflow or similar orchestrators, MLflow/Weights & Biases-style tracking, CI/CD for models, feature stores, monitoring and alerts). (2) AI infrastructure: operate GPU clusters (scheduling, networking with InfiniBand, checkpointing so a multi-week training run survives a failed node, cost and utilisation). (3) Inference and GPU performance: make trained models serve faster and cheaper by writing or tuning GPU kernels in CUDA or Triton, using continuous batching, KV-caching (storing intermediate attention results so each new token costs less), quantisation and speculative decoding. Anthropic's Engineering Manager, Inference Infrastructure posting (US$405,000-625,000, accessed Oct 2026) gives a clear view: the team builds the control plane that decides where each request is served and how much capacity each model needs, drives throughput, latency, utilisation and cost, and runs on-call, incident response and postmortems.

**A typical day and week**: Check dashboards and overnight alerts; investigate a throughput regression or a failed node; pair on a change to the scheduler or serving stack; code review; weekly capacity-planning or cost review. A GPU performance engineer might spend days profiling a kernel to raise utilisation by a few percent that translates to millions of dollars. Incident weeks (an outage, a cluster failure) consume everything.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 40-50 hours typical; 50-65 in incidents or major launches; on-call rotations are normal and can mean night pages (practitioner consensus, confirmed in nature by the on-call and incident-response requirements in the Anthropic posting). Stress 4/5 at labs and hyperscalers where outages are costly; 3/5 on a quieter enterprise platform team. Mostly hybrid; data-centre or lab travel is occasional.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. You serve internal customers (researchers, product teams), so communication matters, but the work is systems engineering and performance numbers.

**Compensation (approximate)**: Specific MLOps/inference pay data is thin. Levels.fyi (9 Oct 2026): NVIDIA ML engineer IC1 US$205K, IC3 US$315K, IC4 US$340K; NVIDIA SWE overall: IC1 US$185K, IC2 US$240K, IC3 US$321K, IC4 US$390K, top level up to US$1.08M, overall median US$310K (2,336 submissions). Employer posting: Anthropic inference-infrastructure engineering manager US$405,000-625,000 (manager level, not entry). UK (ITJobsWatch, 6 months to 9 Oct 2026, advertised base salary): MLOps UK median GBP 80,250 (down 5.6% year on year; 10th percentile GBP 52,025, 25th GBP 62,500, 75th GBP 100,000, 90th GBP 115,000; 404 permanent ads citing MLOps, 230 with quoted salaries); London median GBP 83,500; UK outside London GBP 75,000. This means an UK mid-level MLOps salary of about GBP 62-100K. Italy and the rest of Europe: no MLOps-specific reliable data found; use SWE proxies (Italy EUR 39,258 median; Germany EUR 84,890; France EUR 58,419). Entry in US: roughly the NVIDIA/Meta band (US$185-210K for big tech; lower at non-tech firms); UK entry: no reliable data found; the 10th-25th percentile (GBP 52-62K) is a proxy for junior-to-mid.

**Career path**: Junior platform/DevOps/ML engineer (0-2) -> MLOps or platform engineer (2-4) -> Senior (4-7) -> Staff / principal (7-12), or manager of a platform team. GPU-performance specialists often follow a more research-engineer-like track.

**Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: generalist backend/SRE (site reliability engineering) roles, ML engineer, cloud architect, specialist inference teams at labs. After 7-10 years: staff/principal infrastructure engineer, engineering director, CTO of an AI infrastructure startup, solutions architect at a chip or cloud vendor.

**Tier list of employers**: Tier 1: frontier labs and NVIDIA (top tier for inference and performance engineering), Google (TPU/Brain infra), Meta, Microsoft/Azure AI, AWS (Annapurna Labs). Tier 2: neoclouds and inference providers (CoreWeave, Together-AI and Fireworks-type firms), Databricks, Hugging Face, large AI-native startups, AMD and Cerebras. Tier 3: enterprise platform teams (banks, insurers, pharma, telecoms), consultancies and systems integrators, mid-sized software firms. Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 offers the scale (thousands of GPUs) where optimisation matters most; Tier 3 offers MLOps practice with mainstream cloud tools and less exotic scale.

**How to enter**: *Degrees and backgrounds.* Computer science, computer engineering, electrical engineering or physics with systems skills. For the low-level GPU/performance end, an earlier project file notes that elite postings often prefer a PhD or demonstrated distributed-training and kernel experience, and that master's graduates can sometimes enter with verifiable open-source work (vLLM, FlashAttention-style projects); I verified that this appears in the earlier file but not in a primary posting. The MLOps end is much more open to software engineers, DevOps/SRE engineers and cloud engineers. *Programs.* NVIDIA internships (12 weeks minimum, year-round) and New College Grad program; Anthropic Fellows (ML Systems track: projects such as building a CPU simulator for accelerator workloads or adding accelerator backends to open-source projects). *Hiring calendar.* As in 3.1; infrastructure teams hire year-round. *Interviews.* Coding, systems design (design a model-serving platform that handles traffic spikes), distributed-systems and Linux questions, for GPU roles questions on memory hierarchy, parallelism and profiling. *Certifications.* Cloud certificates (AWS, Google Cloud ML Engineer at US$200, Kubernetes CKA/CKAD) are useful for MLOps in enterprises and consultancies, much less for GPU roles. *Portfolio.* A deployed model-serving system with load tests and monitoring, a contribution to vLLM/Triton/PyTorch, a kernel benchmarked against a baseline, a blog post with profiling numbers. *Common mistakes:* knowing tool names but not the underlying systems (networking, Linux, containers), treating MLOps as just "run notebooks in the cloud", skipping hands-on GPU experience. *Alternative routes:* DevOps/SRE/cloud engineers are the largest feeder group; backend engineers join ML platform teams; data engineers move toward feature/data platforms. Entry difficulty: 3/5 (4/5 for GPU kernel and inference-performance roles at labs).

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: On-call and incidents are part of the job; success is invisible when systems work. The hardware, frameworks and best practices change quickly. Infrastructure roles at labs depend on compute budgets, which are tied to the funding cycle. Not a fit if you want to work directly on model behaviour, dislike operations and pager duty, or prefer visible product work.

#### P1-3.4 Computer vision / robotics / edge AI engineer

**What you actually do**: You build models and software that interpret and act on sensor data. Tasks include training detection, segmentation or tracking models (for cars, factory defects, tumours on scans), building data collection and labelling pipelines, compressing models to run on small chips (quantisation, pruning, TensorRT/ONNX conversion), writing real-time code in C++ and Python, calibrating cameras and lidar, running simulation, and, in robotics, integrating perception with planning and control (ROS, learned policies, imitation or reinforcement learning). Deliverables: a model that hits, say, 99.5% recall on defect detection at 30 frames per second on a specific device, plus the test evidence safety and quality teams require.

**A typical day and week**: Look at failure cases from the last test drive or production line; retrain or adjust the model; profile latency on target hardware; code review (often C++); sync with hardware, mechanical or safety engineers. Weekly: a field or lab test session (a test track, a factory floor, a robot cell), a data-labelling quality review, a safety-case or validation meeting. You spend more time with physical hardware, test benches and labelling vendors than in a pure software role.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 40-50 hours typical; 55-60 near field tests, demos or product launches (practitioner consensus). Stress 3/5, higher (4/5) in safety-critical automotive, medical and defence settings or in robotics startups racing to demos. Mostly in-person or hybrid because hardware is involved; travel to test sites is common; remote work is harder than in pure software.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. You work with hardware, safety and operations teams, but the core is mathematics (geometry, probability), programming and engineering judgement about the physical world.

**Compensation (approximate)**: No dedicated Levels.fyi or BLS figure for computer vision or robotics was retrievable (the specific pages returned errors), so I use the ML engineer proxy and flag the gap. Levels.fyi, 9 Oct 2026, ML engineer: US median US$280K; Meta E3 US$187K; NVIDIA IC1 US$205K; Google L3 US$208K; these are internet and chip companies, and pay at automotive, industrial and medical-device firms is generally lower (my expectation based on practitioner consensus; no dataset found). UK: ML engineer median GBP 103,596 (Levels.fyi) or GBP 67,500 (ITJobsWatch ads); computer vision specific: no reliable data found. Italy: Levels.fyi ML engineer median EUR 32,471; automotive/industrial entry likely at the lower end of that (unverified). Germany ML engineer median EUR 88,465; Zurich CHF 152,282; France EUR 75,949; Singapore S$141,805. Robotics startups often pay lower base and more equity.

**Career path**: Junior perception/CV engineer (0-2) -> CV or robotics engineer (2-4) -> Senior (4-7) -> Staff/principal, tech lead, or perception manager (7-12). Master's graduates often enter at the same level as bachelor's with 1-2 years' experience; PhDs enter at a higher level in research-heavy teams.

**Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: general ML engineer, embedded/robotics software, autonomy and simulation, drone/defence tech, medical-imaging AI. After 7-10 years: perception lead, head of autonomy/robotics, CTO at a robotics or vision startup, technical roles in industrial AI.

**Tier list of employers**: Tier 1: autonomy and physical-AI leaders (Waymo, Tesla Autopilot, NVIDIA robotics/Drive, Wayve in London, Mobileye, Boston Dynamics, well-funded humanoid and robot-foundation-model startups, Google DeepMind robotics) and defence-tech (Anduril, Skydio). Tier 2: large automotive and industrial companies' AI/autonomy groups (BMW, Mercedes, Bosch, Siemens, ABB, Siemens Healthineers, GE HealthCare, Philips), medical-imaging startups, drone makers. Tier 3: system integrators and consultancies, smaller industrial suppliers, inspection and vision-systems vendors (in Italy, for example, groups such as Leonardo, Comau or university/IIT-linked labs are examples I did not verify as hiring now). Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 offers frontier problems and big budgets with intense schedules; Tier 3 offers stability and domain knowledge with slower tools and lower pay.

**How to enter**: *Degrees and backgrounds.* Computer science, electrical/electronic, mechatronics, robotics, physics, maths or aerospace. A master's is common in Europe, and many perception roles at serious teams prefer an MSc or PhD with a computer vision thesis; strong bachelor's graduates enter via internships. *Programs.* NVIDIA internships and New College Grad (the program lists "edge computing" and "autonomous machines" as areas), carmaker graduate programmes, EU research projects, Anthropic Fellows are not relevant here. Hiring calendar: carmakers and industrials recruit on a university cycle (autumn), startups year-round. *Interviews.* Coding (often C++), ML/vision fundamentals (convolutions, transformers for vision, detection metrics), geometry questions, a project deep-dive, sometimes a take-home on a dataset. *Certifications.* Mostly irrelevant; NVIDIA Deep Learning Institute courses and Jetson projects are mild positives. *Portfolio.* A project on a real device (Jetson, Raspberry Pi with accelerator, a small robot or drone), participation in competitions (Kaggle vision, robotics challenges, F1TENTH, RoboCup, formula-student driverless teams), a model deployed with measured latency and accuracy, open-source contributions (OpenCV, ROS, PyTorch). *Common mistakes:* working only on benchmark datasets and never on messy real data or hardware; ignoring C++ and real-time constraints; underestimating data collection and labelling. *Alternative routes:* embedded or robotics software engineers adding ML; ML engineers moving to a vision team; mechatronics/electrical engineers learning ML. Entry difficulty: 4/5

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Smaller job market than general ML; relevant employers cluster in a few cities (Bay Area, Munich/Stuttgart, London, Zurich, Tokyo, Shenzhen), so in Italy the options are fewer; safety certification and testing slow progress; the physical world (weather, hardware faults) makes failures hard to reproduce. Many robotics and self-driving ventures have burned cash for years, so job security at startups is weak. Not a fit if you want pure remote software work, avoid C++ and hardware, or dislike slow, validation-heavy engineering.

#### P1-3.5 AI data specialist / AI trainer / annotation and human-feedback work

**What you actually do**: You produce and judge the data models learn from. The work ranges from drawing boxes on images and transcribing audio (micro-tasks), to ranking pairs of model answers (RLHF: reinforcement learning from human feedback, where human preferences steer the model), writing ideal responses, grading chain-of-thought reasoning, red-teaming (trying to make a model fail), and, at the expert end, writing hard problems in medicine, law, finance, coding or mathematics that models should be tested on. Salaried versions exist inside labs and data vendors: data operations, quality assurance, data-pipeline and annotation-tooling roles. The Scale AI job board in October 2026 shows data-adjacent salaried roles such as data acquisition lead (frontier environments), data engineers and field engineers, alongside the "Human Frontier Collective" programme for advanced contributors.

**A typical day and week**: Contractors pick tasks from a platform queue, follow a long guideline document, complete assignments in a browser tool, and are scored on quality; many work on irregular schedules in the evenings. Salaried staff manage vendor quality, write guidelines, audit samples, build tooling and report accuracy metrics. A week may involve a sudden project start or a sudden stop when a client pauses (see below).

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**: Contractors set their own hours, often 10-40 per week, with pressure from task quality scores and uncertain volume; salaried data-ops staff typically 40-50 hours. Stress 2/5 for task work in routine conditions but income uncertainty and account-deactivation risk are real stressors; 3/5 for managers under project deadlines. Fully remote for contractors; salaried roles at labs are hybrid or office-based.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 2/5 for individual contributors (mostly solo, written guidelines), 4/5 for vendor/quality managers. Quantitative/technical: 2/5 for general annotation; 3-4/5 for coding, maths or domain-expert tasks. The value is in domain judgement and care, not in programming.

**Compensation (approximate)**: This is the least regulated segment. DataAnnotation's own site (accessed Oct 2026) advertises US$25-50 per hour for generalists, US$40-125+ for professional/STEM roles (finance, law, medicine, maths) and US$40-150+ for coding, ML and data-science tasks, language specialists US$25-40; these are advertised ranges, pay is tied to output quality, there is no commitment, and testimonials on the page are self-reported and unverified. Reports about Mercor (Wikipedia citing The Verge, New York Magazine and Wired) mention a "stressful work environment" and "declining pay" among contractors. Micro-task platforms such as Remotasks have historically paid far less (the Scale AI Wikipedia page notes critics saying pay can be late or very low); I found no reliable per-hour figure for those. Salaried data-operations staff at US data vendors and labs: Levels.fyi lists Scale AI software engineer entry total of US$227K but that is engineering pay, not annotation. For Italy, Europe, Asia and the Gulf: no reliable data found; in lower-income countries micro-task pay is much lower and lawfulness of contractor status varies.

**Career path**: Annotator / AI trainer (contract) -> senior or expert reviewer / quality lead -> project manager, data operations specialist or guideline writer -> data-quality manager or evaluation lead at a lab or vendor. For people with a technical background, movement into evaluation engineering, ML data engineering or tooling is a realistic step; from pure micro-task work it is rare.

**Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: data operations or quality roles, evaluation specialist, content/domain jobs, a return to one's original profession (many contractors are doctors, lawyers or academics doing this part-time). After 7-10 years: head of data operations at a vendor, data-quality or evaluation lead, product roles in data platforms, or entry to an engineering path if technical skills were developed meanwhile. Honest note: the base of the pyramid is shrinking as platforms move from volume to experts only, so long-term paths from entry-level labelling are narrow.

**Tier list of employers**: Tier 1: data teams inside frontier labs and the specialist expert-data vendors with the biggest clients (Scale AI, Surge AI, Mercor), which pay best for experts. Tier 2: Turing, Invisible, Handshake-type expert marketplaces, enterprise data-services arms (TELUS Digital, Appen), and corporate data teams. Tier 3: crowd micro-task platforms and outsourced annotation firms in Southeast Asia, Africa and South Asia (Remotasks, Toloka-type marketplaces). Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Tier 1 pays more per hour for scarce expertise but is concentrated in a few clients; Tier 3 pays least and is most precarious.

Concrete context on precarity (all from the Wikipedia pages on these firms, secondary): Scale AI faced lawsuits in December 2024 and January 2025 over wage theft, worker misclassification and psychological harm from disturbing content, and Meta bought a 49% non-voting stake for about US$14.8 billion in June 2025. Surge AI (DataAnnotation) reportedly worked with about 1 million annotators in 2025, had revenue reported at US$1.2 billion in 2024 with about 110 employees, and faced a May 2025 class action alleging misclassification of annotators; the platform has drawn criticism for unexplained account cancellations. Mercor reportedly managed 30,000 contractors in October 2025 at a US$10 billion valuation, and a reported March 2026 supply-chain data breach led to lawsuits and a temporary work pause by major clients such as Meta. The takeaway for a student: the industry's headline revenues are large; the people doing the work are mostly independent contractors without benefits or guaranteed hours.

**How to enter**: *Degrees and backgrounds.* No fixed degree; platforms screen with qualification tests, and expert tracks want a degree or professional credentials in the subject (law, medicine, finance, maths, coding). Languages other than English are in demand. *Programs.* No graduate schemes; sign up to platforms, pass assessments, and expect a waiting list or irregular projects. Internal data-ops roles at labs and vendors are advertised on their career pages. *Hiring calendar.* Continuous; project-driven bursts. *Interviews.* Skills tests, writing and reasoning tests; for salaried roles, structured interviews and a quality-audit exercise. *Certifications.* Not relevant. *Skills and portfolio.* Precision, reading long guidelines, domain expertise, strong writing; for the career-building route, learn Python and basic data analysis, spreadsheets/SQL and evaluation concepts, and document any quality-assurance or tooling work. *Common mistakes:* treating it as stable income; accepting unclear classification or unpaid training time; ignoring tax and legal status as a contractor; assuming the work leads automatically to an AI engineering job (it usually does not). *Alternative routes:* professionals and graduate students use it as a side income; people with technical skills move into evaluation or data engineering. Entry difficulty: 2/5 for contract task work (platform screening rejects many, but volume is high); 4/5 for salaried data-operations, evaluation or tooling roles at top labs.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Pay can fall without notice, work can vanish when a client pauses, accounts can be deactivated, and contractors often lack benefits, a job title or career ladder. Content can be disturbing (the Scale lawsuits cite psychological harm). It may build little transferable experience unless you actively add technical skills. Not a fit as a primary career if you need predictable income, health coverage or progression; fine as a short-term, flexible bridge or side income for someone who understands the trade-offs.

### Part 2: Research, frontier and governance roles

#### P2-3.1 AI research scientist (industry and academia)

**What you actually do:** You pick a research question (for example, how to make a model reason more reliably over long tasks), design experiments, run them on clusters, analyse results, and turn them into methods that go into the next model or into papers. Typical tools: PyTorch or JAX, internal training stacks, experiment trackers, Slurm or Kubernetes clusters. Deliverables: internal write-ups, ablation studies (removing components to see what matters), code that ships into training runs, and for academic and some industry roles, papers at NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR or ACL. Postings differ by subfield: Anthropic's interpretability scientist role is about reverse-engineering algorithms inside models; a Databricks principal scientist role is about scaling; Scale AI's research scientists cover post-training, evaluations and agents; OpenAI's researcher titles span alignment, safety training, reasoning and multimodal work. Academic scientists add grant writing, teaching and supervising PhD students.

**A typical day and week:** Industry: morning reading experiment results and dashboards from overnight runs; a few hours of coding or analysis; a team research meeting; a call with an engineer about a training bug; writing notes. A week often includes a research review where you present, plus chat-heavy collaboration on shared training runs. Academia: split between supervising students, writing papers and grants, teaching and committees; much less access to compute. Hours below are my estimates.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle:** Typical 45-55 hours a week at frontier labs, peaks of 60-80 around model releases and conference deadlines (estimate; no survey exists). Stress 3-4/5: no client deadlines but competitive pressure, release timelines and a publish-or-be-scooped dynamic. Academia: 45-60 hours, stress 3/5 (grant and tenure pressure). Most frontier-research postings I read name an office city (San Francisco, London, New York, Seattle, Zurich, Paris, Palo Alto); Anthropic's say staff should be in the office at least 25% of the time. Remote work is rare for frontier research. Travel: conferences.

**Human vs. quantitative profile:** People/communication: 2/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. You mostly work with a small team and communicate through write-ups and code review; the core of the job is mathematics, statistics and programming.

**Compensation (approximate):**

The strongest signals: first-author papers at top venues, strong open-source or replication work, and a mentor's recommendation. Routes: a PhD at a top group (ideally with a lab internship: DeepMind runs a Student Researcher Program); external safety fellowships open to people from outside the field (Anthropic Fellows, MATS, the OpenAI Safety Fellowship and others, compared in 3.3); the ELLIS PhD programme in Europe (I could not verify details). Interviews: at DeepMind, a recruiter call, two or three skills interviews, then leadership interviews (careers page); other labs test coding and research discussion (Anthropic's alignment posting says Python). Alternative routes: physics, maths or neuroscience career changers; non-target-school candidates can win with replications, open-source work and fellowships; 80,000 Hours notes some people moved into safety research from other quantitative fields in under a year. Entry difficulty: 5/5.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for:** The work is concentrated in a few cities and companies, hours can be punishing around launches, and pay is tied to equity in private companies whose value is uncertain. Most experiments fail, and projects get cancelled when strategy changes. Academia pays far less than industry and gives limited compute, and competition for posts is rising. In Europe the pay gap versus San Francisco is large (section 4). Not a fit for people who need predictable hours, want to work mainly with people and clients, will not relocate, or want guaranteed outcomes.

#### P2-3.2 Research engineer

**What you actually do:** You make the research possible and fast. Typical work across labs: designing RL environments and coding tasks, building reward signals and verifiers, running training experiments on frontier models and diagnosing why models do or do not improve, and building the pipelines that let research iterate quickly. October 2026 examples: Anthropic's Research Engineer, Code RL and Interpretability roles; OpenAI Research Engineer roles in retrieval and search, frontier evals and environments, Codex and privacy; Scale AI's ML Research Engineer, ML Systems; xAI and Cohere "Member of Technical Staff" roles in training infrastructure, RL frameworks and inference. Other work: distributed training across GPU clusters, profiling and CUDA kernels, data platforms for RL, evaluation tooling.

**A typical day and week:** Debugging a failed training run or a slow job; reviewing a colleague's pull request; pairing with a researcher on a new experiment; writing infrastructure code; on some teams an on-call rotation (one Anthropic cybersecurity RL posting mentions a possible on-call rotation). A week mixes coding (60-70%), meetings with researchers, and diagnosing experiments.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle:** Typical 45-55 hours; peaks 60-70 near launches or when a big run breaks (estimate). Stress 3-4/5: expensive runs make failures costly and on-call can interrupt evenings. On-site or hybrid: postings I read name an office city (San Francisco, Palo Alto, New York, London, Paris, Toronto, Zurich). Travel: low.

**Human vs. quantitative profile:** People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 5/5. Collaboration with researchers is constant, but the hard part is distributed systems, performance and ML engineering.

PhD not required but helpful. The practical filter is evidence of strong engineering plus familiarity with LLMs: shipping projects with PyTorch/JAX, reproducing a paper, contributing to open-source training libraries (reimplementing a small transformer, writing a fast attention kernel). Entry difficulty: 4/5.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for:** You are a support function for researchers: credit goes to the papers' authors and priorities can change quickly. On-call and launch crunches exist. Pay at the top is very high but concentrated in equity of private firms. Not a fit for people who want to set the research agenda, dislike infrastructure work, or cannot live in a few expensive cities. Not a fit for those wanting a standard regulated-industry career with predictable hours.

#### P2-3.3 AI safety, alignment, interpretability and evaluations / red-teaming researcher

**What you actually do:** Alignment researchers run experiments on whether models behave as intended (October 2026 examples: Anthropic's London role testing safety techniques by training models to subvert them; OpenAI's "Researcher, Alignment CoT Monitorability" and "Researcher, Safety Oversight"; Scale AI's "Research Scientist, AI Controls and Monitoring"). Interpretability researchers analyse model internals (features and circuits). Evaluations researchers design tasks that measure capabilities and risks: METR's roles include "evaluation execution" and "task development engineer" with a roughly two-day paid work trial; Scale AI lists "Frontier Risk Evaluations" and OpenAI "Data Scientist, Preparedness". Red teamers attack products: Anthropic's Red Team Engineer, Safeguards role asks for penetration-testing and jailbreak experience and a public body of work; OpenAI lists a "Red Team Specialist - Cyber"; AISI has alignment, control and misuse red teams. Government evaluators (UK AISI, US CAISI) test models before and after release; AISI open-sources its evaluation tool Inspect.

**A typical day and week:** Running and analysing experiments; writing evaluation tasks and scoring rubrics; reviewing transcripts of model behaviour; discussing findings with policy or product teams; writing up a safety case or a blog post. In evaluations roles, deadlines come from model release dates: pre-deployment testing windows can be short. Red teamers spend time crafting attacks, building automation, and reporting.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle:** Typical 45-55 hours, peaks 60-70 before model releases (estimate). Stress 3-4/5: high-stakes releases and the sense that errors matter. Government institutes are closer to 40-45 hours with hybrid working (AISI offers hybrid, with offices in London and other UK government sites). METR and Apollo are mostly office-based in Berkeley, London or San Francisco; Apollo offers flexible hours and visa sponsorship.

**Human vs. quantitative profile:** People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. Strong written communication is needed because the output is arguments and evidence; interpretability and theory roles are heavily mathematical; red teaming leans on security skills.

#### P2-3.4 Forward-deployed engineer / AI solutions engineer / AI consultant

**What you actually do:** You take a general-purpose model or data platform and make it solve a customer's real problem: production applications, agents and connectors (for example MCP servers, which let models use tools), evaluation sets, and reusable deployment patterns fed back to product teams. **A typical day and week:** On site or on video call with the client's engineers; scoping a use case with a business owner; building a prototype (Python, APIs, vector databases, evaluation sets); presenting to executives; writing up reusable patterns for the product team. Monday-Thursday at the client, Friday at base is a common rhythm at Palantir-style firms (practitioner commentary; unverified).

**Hours, stress and lifestyle:** Typical 45-55 hours; peaks of 60-70 during go-lives or customer escalations (estimate). Stress 4/5: client-driven deadlines, ambiguity, and responsibility for production systems. Travel: 25-50% (Anthropic FDE posting); Palantir expects about 25% on site and another employer cited up to 50% (Pragmatic Engineer, 12 August 2025). Paths: software engineer who has built LLM applications (Part 1 branch) plus customer exposure; consultant who can code. A bachelor's or equivalent is enough per Anthropic; computer science, engineering, data or maths are common, but economics, management and finance graduates enter via consulting and sales engineering. Portfolio: two or three real applications on LLM APIs (agent with tool use, retrieval, evaluation set) and a short case study; cloud certifications help for solutions-architect roles at cloud firms (not at labs). Interviews: Python technical interview, customer scenario role-play, system design, executive communication. Alternative routes: management consultants and data scientists who upskill in coding; technical account managers. Entry difficulty: 3/5 at consulting and integrators; 4/5 at frontier labs and Palantir.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for:** Travel and context-switching are real; the Pragmatic Engineer notes the role can feel like two jobs (customer delivery and platform engineering) and that expectations vary. Not a fit for people who want deep research time, dislike travel, or prefer a single focused technical problem. Not a fit if you cannot present to executives.

#### P2-3.5 AI governance, policy and responsible-AI specialist

**What you actually do:** Four sub-jobs. (a) Public policy: drafting or analysing legislation, consultations, standards input and briefings; at the EU AI Office you support the Act's rules for general-purpose models (units for regulation and compliance, AI safety, and innovation policy). (b) Lab and company policy: OpenAI's "Model Policy Manager" roles, "Senior Product Policy Lead, Regulation" and "Research Program Manager, Governance"; Anthropic's Policy Design Manager (owns the usage policy, builds threat models) and Product Policy Manager; Google DeepMind's "Frontier Public Affairs Lead"; Cohere's public policy roles. (c) Corporate responsible AI / compliance: inventorying AI systems, risk-classifying them under the EU AI Act, writing governance policies, running impact assessments with legal, privacy, security and data teams; Citi's AI Business Risk and Control Director (October 2026) holds approval authority for AI use cases and aligns the framework to the EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF. (d) Think-tank or consulting research: reports, workshops, client advice.

**A typical day and week:** Reading regulation and draft guidance; drafting a policy paper or compliance checklist; meetings with legal, engineering and business teams; stakeholder calls with regulators or civil society; in the lab setting, reviewing a product launch against policy. Week-ending output: memo, briefing or risk assessment.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle:** Typical 40-50 hours in regulators, think tanks and corporate roles; 50-60 at labs and law/consulting firms; peaks of 60+ around regulation deadlines, consultations and legislative sessions (estimate). Stress 3/5 (4/5 at law firms, labs during incidents, and policy shops during legislative crunch). Hybrid is common outside labs. Travel: moderate (Brussels, DC, Geneva, summits).

**Human vs. quantitative profile:** People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 2/5 (rising to 3/5 for technical policy and evaluation-oriented roles). The job is persuasion, writing and interpretation; technical policy roles require reading model evaluations and understanding ML basics.

#### P2-3.6 AI product manager

**What you actually do:** You define what an AI product should do and how to measure it: owning strategy, roadmap and delivery for a product area, turning model capabilities into usable experiences with metrics, experiments and research, and working with design, engineering, data science, research and marketing. Distinctive AI-PM work: defining evals that stand for product quality, deciding when a model is "good enough", managing cost per request, handling prompt and model changes, working with safety teams on misuse (OpenAI lists PMs for multimodal safety and integrity; Scale AI for cybersecurity and finance agents; Anthropic for Safeguards and Public Sector), and shaping what research should build next.

**A typical day and week:** User and data review; writing a product requirements document with eval criteria; syncing with engineers and researchers; reviewing launch readiness with safety teams; customer calls; prioritisation meetings. Weekly rhythm: launch planning, metrics reviews, and unscheduled escalations when model behaviour changes.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle:** Typical 45-55 hours, peaks 60-70 around launches (estimate). Stress 3-4/5: fast-changing technology, high visibility, and launch deadlines. Hybrid at big tech; frontier-lab and AI-startup roles are mostly on-site in San Francisco, New York or Seattle (Cohere's PM roles list Toronto). Travel: low to moderate.

**Human vs. quantitative profile:** People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. You need to read experiment results and understand model limitations, but the main skill is alignment of people and judgment.

Entry difficulty: 4/5 at frontier labs and top big tech; 3/5 elsewhere.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for:** Roadmaps get overturned when models change; you are accountable for outcomes you do not control; and AI PM is a crowded aspiration, so many candidates compete for few roles. Not a fit for people who need stable, long-horizon plans, or who dislike ambiguity about technical feasibility. Not a fit for those who want to do the technical work themselves.

**Commentary.** Labs and big tech pay several times more than government, academia and nonprofits for comparable technical skill, but nonprofits like METR post US salaries ($328,000-$578,000 for evaluation roles) competitive with big tech. Within the labs, posted bands overlap heavily (OpenAI's $380,000-$500,000 research bands sit inside Anthropic's wider $350,000-$850,000 interpretability band), so no single lab sets the market. Governments offer stability and hybrid working at lower pay. Consulting and enterprise buyers are where the volume of governance and solutions jobs is, because every regulated company must comply with the EU AI Act from August 2026. In forward-deployed roles, labs pay more than consultancies for the same hours (Palantir, Databricks and Scale AI sit between) but ask for more experience.

**Regional differences.**
- US versus Europe: London lab postings (Anthropic GBP 260,000-630,000; Cohere CA$250,000-535,000) are high by UK standards, but the UK research scientist median on Levels.fyi is GBP 116,501, Switzerland CHF 119,153, and UK AISI tops out at GBP 145,000, so the lab premium over the local market is large. Italy's software engineer median is EUR 39,258 total and I found no Italian frontier-lab research roles; ambitious Italians work in London, Zurich, Paris, the US or remotely.
- London, Zurich, Paris: London hosts DeepMind, Anthropic, OpenAI and Cohere offices, AISI, Apollo and the London Initiative for Safe AI (host of Pivotal and LASR); Zurich hosts DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic postings; Paris hosts Mistral, DeepMind, Cohere and customer roles at Anthropic, OpenAI and Databricks. Brussels is the centre of EU policy; Washington DC of US policy (CAISI, NIST, Horizon placements, lab policy roles).
- Italy: strong universities and the FAIR network give PhD and postdoc routes but low pay; governance and compliance demand is rising with Law 132/2025 and the EU AI Act (Il Sole 24 Ore reports Hays seeing more searches for AI governance specialists); I found no priced Italian posting beyond Anthropic's Milan role (which requires Italian).
- Gulf and Asia: published pay is thin. Levels.fyi (self-reported) shows G42 research scientists at a median AED 429,653 (tax-free), UAE software engineers AED 337,547, Singapore research scientists SGD 90,299 and Japan about JPY 10.5 million; MBZUAI, TII and Sakana AI advertise roles without pay. Palantir, Databricks, OpenAI, Cohere and Scale AI post FDE or applied-AI roles in Tokyo, Seoul, Singapore, Sydney, Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Riyadh and Doha without ranges. China has its own frontier labs (not covered). Hong Kong: no reliable data found.
- Immigration and talent flows: the AI Index 2026 reports that the number of AI researchers and developers moving to the US fell 89% since 2017, including 80% in the last year. Horizon does not sponsor visas and many fellowships need local work authorisation; Anthropic Fellows do not sponsor visas; Astra offers visa support; the UK AISI can sponsor visas for technical roles.
