# Finance, part 1: Investment banking and advisory

## 1. What this branch is

Investment banking and advisory is the part of finance where professionals help companies, governments and investors do large, infrequent financial transactions: selling a business, buying one, going public on a stock exchange, borrowing through bonds or loans, or fixing a balance sheet that has run into trouble. Banks and advisory firms are paid mainly through fees tied to those deals, not by trading or by managing money. This is the "sell side" of the industry (it serves issuers and corporate clients; the "buy side" means investors such as private equity funds and asset managers, which are covered in another part of this finance branch). The work is among the best paid and the most demanding in early careers, and it is also one of the most selective: junior pay is high, but hours are long and the entry funnel is narrow, so it pays to understand the whole map, including the adjacent and lower-intensity doors (corporate banking, Big 4 deal advisory) before committing.

## 2. Map of areas and sectors

### 2.1 What investment banking is, and where it sits

An **investment bank** (or the investment banking division, IBD, of a larger bank) is an adviser and fundraising intermediary for large organisations. Three broad things happen in a bank that people loosely call "investment banking", and it matters which one you mean:

- **IBD / "banking" (the "private side"):** bankers who advise clients on mergers and acquisitions (M&A), restructurings, and on raising equity and debt. They work with confidential information, which is why this is the "private side" of the bank. This report is about this side. IBD is organised into **coverage** groups (relationships by industry or region) and **product** groups (execution expertise: M&A, equity capital markets, debt capital markets, leveraged finance, restructuring).
- **Markets / "sales & trading" and research (the "public side"):** traders and salespeople who buy and sell securities for clients, and analysts who publish research. Revenue comes from spreads, commissions and financing. A deal launched by IBD (a share offering, a bond) is sold to investors through the markets side (the "syndicate" desk is the bridge). Covered in another part of this branch.
- **Corporate and transaction banking:** the lending, cash management and trade finance relationships a bank has with companies (section 2.7). It is bank-balance-sheet business, not deal advisory, but it sits next to IBD and is a common alternative entry point.

**Sell side vs buy side.** The *sell side* (banks, advisers) creates, advises on and distributes financial products and transactions and earns fees or spreads. The *buy side* (private equity, hedge funds, asset managers, pension funds, insurers) invests capital. In deals, bankers frequently sit across the table from private equity buyers. IB analysts commonly exit to the buy side after two to three years (sections 3 and 4).

**How the industry makes money (plain language).**

| Activity | Who pays | How the bank/adviser is paid | Typical shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| M&A advice | Seller or buyer | Advisory fee, mostly a "success fee" paid when the deal closes, sometimes plus a monthly retainer and a fee for an opinion | Lumpy; depends on deal completion; fee as a small percentage of deal value (percentages vary widely with size; no figure verified this session) |
| IPO and share offerings | The issuing company (and sometimes selling shareholders) | "Gross spread", a percentage of money raised, split among the underwriters | In the US, academic work (Chen and Ritter 2000; Ritter's later SEC appendix) found more than 90% of mid-sized IPOs paid exactly 7% and that almost half of large IPOs paid below 7%; spreads outside the US are reported to be about half that level |
| Bond issuance (DCM) and leveraged loans | The borrower | Underwriting/arrangement fees and syndication spread, usually a fraction of a percent of the amount | Lower % than equity, but large volumes |
| Restructuring | Troubled company, or its creditors | Monthly fees plus a completion fee | Counter-cyclical to M&A: busier when credit is stressed |
| Corporate/transaction banking | Corporate clients | Net interest margin on loans and deposits, plus fees for payments, cash management, trade services | Steadier, lower margin per client |

**Where the market stands (October 2026).** Global M&A reached about US$3.9 trillion in the first nine months of 2026, up 28% on a year earlier, even though the number of deals fell 8%; the first half was the highest on record at US$2.85 trillion, and the third quarter slowed sharply (LSEG, via Investment Executive, accessed October 2026). Mega-deals above US$10 billion account for much of the value (57 so far in 2026), which is good for the biggest banks and less so for the broader market. Global equity issuance passed US$1 trillion in the first nine months of 2026, up 52.6% (Dealogic via ION Analytics, 6 October 2026, snippet-level), led by technology and AI-related deals and a US$86.2 billion SpaceX IPO in the second quarter; IPO counts, however, are well below the peak. Investment banking revenue is cyclical: it rises and falls with deal volumes, and so does hiring and bonus size. Treat any single year's pay as a data point, not a promise.

### 2.2 M&A advisory: sell-side, buy-side, fairness opinions

- **Definition.** M&A bankers advise on buying, selling, merging or splitting companies and business units.
- **Sell-side mandate.** The client wants to sell. The bank prepares the business for sale (a financial model, an information memorandum), approaches potential buyers, runs a competitive process, and helps negotiate price and terms. The sell-side process is the "bread and butter" of boutique and mid-market banks.
- **Buy-side mandate.** The client wants to buy. The bank helps find targets, values them, structures the offer (cash, shares, debt), and helps negotiate. Often runs alongside a financing from the same bank's DCM/LevFin teams.
- **Defence and activism work.** Advising a company that is being approached by an unwanted bidder or an activist shareholder (common at the elite boutiques).
- **Fairness opinion.** A formal letter in which a bank states that a deal price is fair, "from a financial point of view", to the shareholders or the board. It exists because directors want independent support for a decision, and it is often a separate fee line (a bank can be paid for it even if it is not the lead adviser). The analyst work behind it is valuation: discounted cash flow (DCF), comparable companies ("comps"), precedent transactions.
- **Who the clients are.** Large corporates, private equity firms (financial sponsors) buying and selling portfolio companies, families and founders selling businesses, governments (privatisations), and sovereign/state-backed investors.
- **In Italy.** The M&A market is concentrated in a handful of very large deals each year. In the first nine months of 2026 Italian-involved announced M&A was US$168.7 billion, up 122% on a year earlier, across 1,571 deals, up 9% (LSEG via Teleborsa, 6 October 2026). By value, the top ten advisers were Bank of America, Vitale&Co-IMAP, UBS, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, BNP Paribas, Goldman Sachs, Deutsche Bank, Lazard and Stifel/KBW; by number of deals among them, Lazard led with 26, followed by JPMorgan with 21 and Vitale&Co-IMAP with 19. Each deal's full value is credited to every adviser, so shares sum to well above 100%; the near-identical shares of the leaders imply that a few very large deals are shared by the same advisers (my inference). Vitale&Co-IMAP is the only Italian independent boutique in the top ten.

### 2.3 Capital markets and product groups

**ECM (equity capital markets)** helps companies raise money by selling shares: IPOs (first listing), follow-on offerings (a listed company sells more shares, often an overnight "accelerated bookbuild"), rights issues, and **convertible bonds** (bonds that can turn into shares, a hybrid of debt and equity). ECM bankers sit between the issuer (via the coverage banker) and investors (via the sales and trading floor's syndicate and sales desks). They advise on timing, valuation, size, investor targeting and structure, and lead the legal/marketing process. Fee: gross spread on proceeds. Clients: listed companies, companies preparing to list, private equity sponsors selling shares post-IPO, governments privatising assets. 2026 context: record US dollar volume but concentrated in a few huge deals; Dealogic reports 1H26 global ECM issuance of US$729.4 billion (+73% y/y), global IPO volume US$207 billion, and record first-half convertible volumes (ION Analytics/Dealogic, 1 July 2026).

**DCM (debt capital markets)** advises borrowers with strong credit ratings ("investment grade", rated BBB-/Baa3 and above) on issuing bonds, and on loans and other debt products. Bankers advise on timing, maturity, currency, size, price ("spread" over a government benchmark), covenants and documentation, then work with the **syndicate desk**, which runs the live order book from investors and recommends pricing and allocations. Clients: large corporates, banks and insurers, governments and agencies, supranationals. Many issuers come to market repeatedly, so DCM is relationship- and execution-driven, with faster deal cycles than M&A.

**Leveraged finance (LevFin)** serves sub-investment-grade borrowers (rated BB+ or below, or unrated), most importantly companies being bought by private equity funds in **leveraged buyouts (LBOs)**, where a large part of the price is financed with debt. The bank underwrites or arranges **leveraged loans** (bank/private-market loans) and **high-yield bonds** (junk bonds). It earns arrangement/underwriting fees and syndication spreads, and it takes balance-sheet risk if it commits to fund debt before selling it down to investors (CLOs, credit funds, insurers). More credit analysis than investment-grade DCM, and strong ties with the private equity sponsor community, which makes it a classic pipeline to private credit funds.

**Other product teams** (varies by bank): **sponsor coverage** (relationships with private equity firms, found at most banks), **financing solutions/ structured finance**, **liability management** (helping companies tender for or restructure existing bonds), **private placements** and **derivatives/risk solutions**.

### 2.4 Coverage groups vs product groups, and how they work together

- **Coverage (or "industry/sector") groups** own the client relationship. Typical groups: technology/media/telecom (TMT), healthcare, financial institutions (FIG), industrials, consumer/retail, energy/power and infrastructure, real estate, and sponsors. Regional or country coverage (for example "Italy coverage", "Gulf coverage") exists where relationships depend on geography, language and local regulation. The coverage banker knows the client's strategy and generates ideas.
- **Product groups** (M&A, ECM, DCM, LevFin, restructuring) own the technical expertise in executing a particular transaction type and the relationships with the investor side.
- **On a deal.** A typical flow: a coverage MD builds the relationship and pitches an idea; a product team joins (for an acquisition financed with debt: M&A plus DCM or LevFin; for an IPO: ECM plus the industry team and the equity syndicate). The coverage team handles the client; the product team handles execution and market knowledge. At **elite boutiques** and many smaller banks, M&A and industry coverage are merged: the same team covers the client and executes the sale, so juniors see more of the deal.
- **Why this matters for a career.** At a bulge bracket (BB), analysts are often placed in either an industry group (more varied, builds financial models and pitch decks for many clients) or a product group (more technical). At boutiques there are fewer silos.

### 2.5 Restructuring advisory

Restructuring bankers advise companies in financial distress, or their lenders and shareholders. Work includes **debtor-side** mandates (advising the troubled company on liquidity, negotiations with creditors, and formal court processes such as US Chapter 11 or UK restructuring plans), **creditor-side** mandates (advising banks, bondholder groups or credit funds on recovery and terms), and **liability management exercises (LMEs)** (negotiated transactions to extend or swap existing debt without a bankruptcy, such as amend-and-extend, "uptier" exchanges and drop-downs). Prospect Rock describes debtor-side mandates as higher-fee and creditor-side as less lucrative (2023). Restructuring is counter-cyclical: demand rises when credit stress rises, and boutiques with large restructuring practices (Houlihan Lokey, PJT Partners, Lazard, Rothschild & Co, Evercore, Perella Weinberg, Moelis, Ducera and others) have been consistently busy. In 2026: PJT's CEO described restructuring demand as elevated and expected it to stay so (earnings call, Q2 2026, snippet-level); Houlihan Lokey called fiscal 2026 "one of the strongest years on record" for restructuring but had a softer fiscal fourth quarter; Debtwire counted 136 European restructuring mandates in H1 2026 involving EUR 50.3 billion of debt (14 August 2026), with PJT leading by mandates (8), then Houlihan Lokey (7) and Rothschild (6). Mandate counts were slightly below the 147 of H1 2025, but the work is concentrated in fewer, bigger situations.

### 2.7 Corporate and transaction banking (adjacent)

- **Corporate banking** (called "commercial banking", "global corporate banking" or "relationship banking" depending on the bank) means lending and relationship management for medium and large companies. Relationship managers (RMs) own the client; they bring in loans, revolving credit lines, and cross-sell products from the investment bank (bond issues, M&A advice, derivatives for hedging).
- **Transaction banking** (also "global transaction banking", "treasury and trade solutions", "cash management") is the plumbing: payments, cash pooling, liquidity management, trade finance (letters of credit, guarantees) and custody. Citi calls it Treasury and Trade Solutions (TTS); Deutsche Bank calls it Global Transaction Banking (GTB).
- **Earns:** net interest margin on loans and deposits, plus fees for services. Revenue is steadier and the work is less cyclical than M&A.
- **Employers:** global banks (HSBC, Barclays, Citi, JPMorgan, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, Santander), national champions (Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit in Italy; ING, ABN AMRO), and regional banks.

### 2.8 Big 4 and boutique deal advisory (alternative entry)

The Big 4 accounting firms (Deloitte, EY, KPMG, PwC) have "deals" or "deal advisory" teams that advise buyers, sellers and lenders. The main teams:

- **Transaction services (TS) / financial due diligence (FDD):** before a deal closes, the buyer (often private equity) hires TS to test the target's numbers: "quality of earnings" (adjusting reported profit to underlying earnings), net debt and "debt-like" items, working capital, forecast risk. Output: a due diligence report and a call with the buyer. Also sell-side "vendor due diligence".
- **Valuation / modelling / purchase price allocation:** valuations for accounting rules, tax, disputes and deals.
- **Corporate finance (CF) advisory:** smaller M&A advisory work for mid-market clients, run similarly to a mid-market bank but with a Big 4 brand and fee structure. In Italy and much of Europe, Big 4 CF teams run real sell-side processes.
- **Restructuring / turnaround:** operational and financial advice to troubled businesses (parallel to restructuring bankers).
- **Independent boutiques:** firms such as Alvarez & Marsal and FTI Consulting (restructuring and transaction advisory) appear in Debtwire's European restructuring adviser rankings (Q2 2026).

Fees are typically time-based (hourly), unlike banks' success fees; one source (Mergers & Inquisitions, figures as of 2022) quotes US$200k-800k per engagement. This is an important structural difference: pay is lower but steadier and hours shorter than in banking. A well-trodden path from TS into banking and the buy side exists but is narrower than bank-to-buy-side (section 3.6).

### 3.1 M&A and industry/coverage banker (analyst to MD)

**What you actually do**
You work on the sell-side and buy-side advisory mandates described in section 2.2. As an *analyst* you build and update financial models in Excel (three-statement models, discounted cash flow, merger models showing the effect on earnings per share, leveraged buyout models), run comparable-company and precedent-transaction analyses (valuation "comps"), and produce the pitch decks and client presentations in PowerPoint (heavily formatted, revised many times a day). On a live sell-side you also prepare the confidential information memorandum (a marketing document), management presentations, buyer lists, data-room indexes, and track bids and diligence questions. Tools: Excel, PowerPoint, FactSet/Capital IQ/Bloomberg for data, Datasite/Intralinks for data rooms, and increasingly internal AI tools for first drafts of comps and summaries. As an *associate* you manage the analyst, own the model and the process timetable, and are the day-to-day contact for the client's finance team and the lawyers. A *VP* runs the execution of one or more deals and writes the view for the MD; *directors/MDs* bring the relationship, advise the board and negotiate. In coverage groups (TMT, healthcare, industrials, etc.) a large part of the junior's time is pitching: "ideas" decks for clients that may never turn into mandates.

**A typical day and week**
A day in a live process (my composite of practitioner accounts; the structure is common but there is no single survey): arrive 8:30-9:30; revise a model or deck from overnight comments; midday calls with the client or buyers; in the afternoon, turn comments from the associate/VP; evenings are when senior comments arrive, so 8-11pm is the main working block; weekend work comes when a deadline lands (bid dates, board meetings, signing). In a quiet week, analysts may leave by 8-9pm and have a free weekend; in a bid week, all-nighters and 100+ hour weeks occur. Work is lumpy: busy-busy-quiet. Travel is limited for juniors (client sites and data-room visits are rare; most senior travel is to clients), and hybrid work is accepted at some firms but in-office presence is the norm for juniors.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**
Hours: typical 75-85 per week, peaks of 100-120. Evidence: the 2025 Odyssey survey average of 78 (81 at bulge brackets, 82 at elite boutiques); court filings in the Centerview case describing 60-120 hours on active deals (Prospect Rock page); a Wall Street Oasis survey of March 2023 found 77 hours and under six hours' sleep; policies (JPMorgan 80-hour guideline, BofA logging, Goldman's Saturday rule) exempt live deals. Stress: 5/5 for juniors on live deals (shared scale: "junior M&A on live deals"). Lifestyle: little predictability, last-minute cancellations, limited sleep during peaks; vacations are possible but often disrupted. Flexibility: low at junior levels; a 2-week stretch of 4am finishes is possible. Remote/hybrid: limited. Regional differences: Hong Kong bankers are reported to work around four hours more per week than Singapore peers (Robert Walters, as reported in 2024; snippet-level); Italian and other Continental offices of US banks usually follow the global firm's hour policy but local firms (Mediobanca, Intesa IMI) have historically had somewhat more predictable weeks (practitioner view; not measured).

**Human vs. quantitative profile**
People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. As a junior you spend more time with Excel and PowerPoint than with clients, but the role is judged by communication: crisp summaries, handling feedback from five people at once, and, as you advance, relationships. The quantitative bar is solid financial modelling and accounting understanding, not advanced mathematics.

**Compensation (approximate)**
*United States (New York and other major hubs).* Base for first-year analysts: US$105,000-110,000 (Prospect Rock Partners self-reported survey, August 2025; flat versus 2024); average first-year bonus about US$62,000 (range US$49,000-90,000), second-year about US$91,000, implying first-year total roughly US$167,000-172,000 (derived, not stated by the source). A different Prospect Rock dataset (866 responses, March 2026) shows all-bank first-year analyst total compensation of about US$148,000 rising to US$160,000 over three cycles. WSO self-reported bonuses via eFinancialCareers (21 August 2025): about US$67,000 first-year, US$88,000 second-year; about US$75,000 at bulge brackets, US$80,000 at boutiques and US$65,000 at middle-market firms (self-reported and likely biased upwards, said the article). Bucket matters: top bucket (15-25% of analysts) bonuses are 95-105% of base, mid-bucket 70-85%, bottom 45-65% (Prospect Rock, Aug 2025). Global average analyst total pay in 2025 was about US$114,000 (+14%), with base down 4% and bonus up 44%, in eFinancialCareers' Compensation & Lifestyle Report 2026 (23 April 2026; 2,700+ responses, global, so lower than the US-only figure).
*Associates and above (US).* Associate 1 total: about US$400,000 at elite boutiques versus US$280,000 at bulge brackets (Prospect Rock, March 2026); Associate 2 about US$340,000 (mid-market) and US$339,000 (BB); Associate 3 up to US$410,000-450,000. VP 1: about US$255,000 base plus US$250,000 bonus; VP 2: US$500,000 mid-market, US$580,000 bulge, US$660,000 elite boutique. M&A Director 2 about US$1.03 million. MD with 4+ years: median about US$1.6 million at both EBs and BBs; all-banks MD median US$1.1 million with wide spread. About a quarter or more of senior bonuses are deferred (same source).
*United Kingdom (London).* Entry totals from PrepLounge (updated 27 August 2026; aggregator, method undisclosed): about £90,000-95,000 at most bulge brackets (base £50-70k), around £103-125k at several boutiques (Evercore £103k, Rothschild £104k, Houlihan Lokey £115k, Moelis £115k, PJT £110k, Perella £119k, Centerview £125k); associate £155,000-255,000, VP £235,000-345,000, director £350,000-575,000, MD £500,000+. Dartmouth Partners reports London bulge-bracket analyst total compensation up 5-10% in summer 2025 after two flat or compressed years (10 November 2025). Banks' pay filings show averages for London material risk takers (the most senior front-office staff, not typical MDs) of US$1.46-2.2 million in 2025 (eFinancialCareers from Pillar 3 filings, 8 July 2026). Evercore's London regular staff averaged £114,000 in 2025 (flat) (eFinancialCareers, 16 September 2026).
*Italy (Milan).* No reliable data found for analyst base plus bonus at the Italian offices of US banks and boutiques. Domestic-bank graduate programmes quote EUR 40,000 gross from the start (Intesa Sanpaolo I'M IN, article of 8 July 2026; UniCredit Milan graduate analyst, undated posting). One aggregator lists a EUR 51,100-73,900 band for a six-month Citi placement analyst in Milan (not verified; internship). Expect Milan analyst pay to sit materially below London and New York (unverified magnitude).

**Career path**
Analyst (2-3 years; some firms run two-year analyst programmes then an up-or-out review) → Associate (about 3 years; MBA hires enter here) → VP (about 3 years) → Director/Executive Director (2-4 years) → Managing Director (typically 10-14 years from start; many leave before; promotion is competitive and revenue-based). These timelines are practitioner consensus rather than surveyed. In coverage groups an MD's value is their client relationships; in product groups, execution and market knowledge.

**Exit opportunities**
After 2-3 years (analyst): private equity associate (the classic "on-cycle" route in the US, with headhunters recruiting well in advance; in Europe more staggered and off-cycle), growth equity and venture, credit and hedge funds, corporate development, top MBA programmes. After 7-10 years: private equity VP/principal, corporate development heads or CFO tracks, partner at a boutique, entrepreneur/search fund, or staying on the MD track. The project's earlier research (Oct 2026) stresses that mega-fund and upper-middle-market PE hire almost no direct graduates in Europe; the standard route is two years in M&A, LevFin or restructuring, then headhunter-led recruiting.

**Tier list of employers**
Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.
- **Tier 1 (elite boutiques and top bulge brackets):** Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan (top global league tables; Goldman led the nine-month 2026 M&A table per LSEG); Centerview, Evercore, Lazard, PJT, Perella Weinberg, Moelis, Rothschild & Co (elite boutiques; Europe-strong). Distinguishing features: biggest mandates, best exit placement (especially into mega-fund PE), highest junior selectivity.
- **Tier 2 (other bulge brackets, large mid-market and top European banks):** Bank of America, Citi, Barclays, UBS, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, HSBC, Jefferies, Houlihan Lokey, Wells Fargo, RBC, Nomura, Qatalyst, Guggenheim, Stifel. Strong brand and solid training; exits to PE are good but depend on the group and sector. Bank of America ranked first by Italian M&A value in the first nine months of 2026 (LSEG via Teleborsa).
- **Tier 3 (middle-market and regional/local):** Lincoln International, William Blair, Baird, Raymond James, Piper Sandler, Harris Williams, Alantra, Stout, Clairfield, Equita, Vitale&Co-IMAP (Italy's leading independent M&A boutique by 2026 value), Mediobanca and Intesa IMI as Italian domestic leaders, plus national banks (Santander, Commerzbank, ING, ABN AMRO). More deals per junior, faster ownership, more varied exits, lower exit prestige for mega-funds. The range is wide: the best mid-market franchises place analysts in upper-middle-market PE.
- **Asia/Gulf notes:** Nomura, Daiwa, Mizuho Securities, SMBC Nikko dominate domestic Japanese M&A; FAB, Emirates NBD and SNB Capital lead in the Gulf alongside global banks. Gulf equity markets cooled in 2026 (IPO proceeds below US$1.1 billion year to date per a September 2026 report cited in search results, snippet-level), while Gulf state-backed investors' transactions rose almost 200% in H1.

**How to enter**
*Degrees and backgrounds:* Finance, economics, accounting, business and engineering/maths graduates all appear; the earlier project research notes that classic IBD analyst classes skew toward economics and finance (2016-17 data, old) while whole-bank intern classes are more STEM. *Programmes:* internships first. Spring weeks (UK), sophomore programmes (US), summer analyst programmes (10-12 weeks) and, in Continental Europe, 6-month internships and off-cycle programmes. *Recruiting calendar:* see the box at the start of section 3. *Certifications:* none required to apply; the CFA is rarely rewarded in M&A (see earlier project file); FINRA SIE and Series 79 are taken after joining in the US. *Skills to build:* accounting (reading three statements), valuation (DCF, comps, precedents), LBO logic, Excel shortcuts and discipline, short crisp writing, commercial awareness; Italian/French/German for local coverage. *Common mistakes:* applying late (open date is the deadline), generic applications, over-investing in CFA or certifications at the expense of networking and technical prep, not rehearsing technical questions, ignoring boutiques and the middle market, and treating a spring week as an automatic summer offer. *Alternative entry routes:* from a non-target school, aim at middle-market banks and boutiques with year-round hiring and less rigid pipelines, network aggressively (alumni, cold emails), take a Big 4 TS or valuation role and lateral after 1-2 years (see 3.6), or do a specialised master's (for example, a one-year MSc Finance in London) with a strong careers office; the London Business School's Masters in Financial Analysis reports 92% received an offer within three months in 2025 (project earlier file). A prep-site claim that 65% of BB analyst hires come from target schools and 9% from non-target schools could not be verified. Entry difficulty: 5/5 at bulge brackets and elite boutiques (Goldman's 2026 intern acceptance rate was below 1% for the third year, per Fortune, 8 June 2026, as noted in the project earlier file); 4/5 at middle-market and regional banks.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**
Hours and sleep deprivation are the core cost; the work is repetitive and detail-heavy in the first two years (formatting, version control, corrections), and you have little control over your schedule. Pay is high but per-hour pay is not (some sources estimate analysts' per-hour income falls below that of better-paid professions); bonus swings with the deal cycle, and 2026's mega-deal boom benefits large firms more than small ones. AI may reduce junior roles. Not a good fit if you need predictable evenings and weekends, dislike repetitive precision work, are highly uncomfortable with hierarchy and late-night feedback, or want deep technical specialisation or independent research. Also not great if your main goal is to be a hands-on investor in your first five years: you will mostly advise, not decide.

### 3.2 Equity capital markets (ECM)

**What you actually do**
ECM bankers (often called "ECM origination") advise companies on raising equity: IPOs, follow-on and block trades, convertible bonds, spin-offs and rights issues. An analyst builds valuation work (comparable-company analysis, IPO valuation frameworks, "equity story" support), prepares pitch materials with the industry team, maintains a pipeline of potential issuers, builds transaction models (dilution, pro forma ownership), keeps track of recent offerings and their post-listing performance, and during a live deal manages timelines, drafts, prospectus changes with lawyers, and investor feedback. On a day when a transaction launches (for example an accelerated bookbuild), the process is fast and compressed: soundings with investors before launch, a book that builds overnight and pricing in the morning. ECM sits between the industry coverage team (who owns the client) and the equity syndicate desk (who owns the investors). Tools: Excel, PowerPoint, Bloomberg/FactSet/Dealogic, internal syndicate/book-building systems, and legal documentation workflows.

**A typical day and week**
A typical non-live day: morning market review (equity indices, IPO and follow-on calendar, recent deals' trading), internal calls with syndicate and the industry team, comps and valuation analyses, model updates. A live-deal day can start with an overnight launch, a pricing call with the issuer, and finishing paperwork the same evening. Weeks are driven by market windows: busy periods before and after earnings seasons and before summer and Christmas, quiet when volatility spikes. Hours follow market opening; deals can go from idea to execution in days (follow-ons) or months (IPOs). Travel is limited for juniors; senior bankers travel to roadshows. In 2026, the market is unusually active in large technology deals (Dealogic reports a record share of Q3 flow from technology).

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**
Hours: typical 65-75 per week, peaks of 90-100 around IPO launches and busy windows. This is a practitioner estimate; I found no 2025-2026 survey that separates ECM from M&A hours. Stress: 4/5, driven by live-market risk (deals can be pulled or re-priced mid-flight) and compressed timelines. Lifestyle: less predictable than DCM, usually somewhat less intense than M&A, but a live IPO is a multi-week sprint. Remote: limited. Travel: low for juniors.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**
People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. You need market intuition (how investors will value a story) more than advanced mathematics, but you must be comfortable with valuation and with explaining a company's "equity story" succinctly.

**Compensation (approximate)**
Analyst base pay is the same as M&A at the same bank (US$105,000-110,000 in the US, London roughly £50,000-70,000 at bulge brackets, from the sources in 3.1). Bonuses tend to be a little lower than M&A: Prospect Rock's August 2025 data show average bonuses by group of US$74,000 (M&A, all levels), US$69,000 (industry coverage) and US$66,000 (product groups, which include ECM, DCM and LevFin), with wide outliers; that report gives no ECM-specific figure, and a forum summary also says ECM/DCM bonuses are generally below M&A or coverage (anecdotal). 2025 was a strong ECM year for revenue: eFinancialCareers' 2026 report cites Dealogic revenue growth of 25% in M&A, 48% in ECM and 11% in DCM in 2025 (23 April 2026), which plausibly helps explain why analyst bonuses rose 44% on average that year (my inference). Senior ECM: no reliable ECM-specific data from Prospect Rock's public excerpt; ECM MDs are generally paid between DCM and M&A (practitioner view, unverified). UK, Italy, Hong Kong, Singapore, Dubai: no ECM-specific figures found; use the M&A analyst ranges in 3.1 as an upper reference.

**Career path**
Analyst (2-3 years) → Associate → VP → Director → MD, similar timeline to M&A. Specialisation increases: IPO origination, equity-linked/convertibles, or sector ECM. Moves between ECM and M&A or the syndicate desk (markets side) are possible and common; internal moves from ECM into coverage groups happen but are not guaranteed.

**Exit opportunities**
After 2-3 years: corporate development, equity buy side (long-only asset managers, hedge funds) where the market knowledge is valued, private equity (possible but less standard than from M&A), growth equity, investor relations, or lateral to M&A/coverage. After 7-10 years: head of ECM or syndicate roles at banks, corporate treasury or CFO office roles at issuers, buy-side investment roles, exchange/listing roles. Exits to mega-fund PE are weaker than from M&A (practitioner consensus).

**Tier list of employers**
Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. ECM requires a distribution machine (equity sales and trading), so the strongest ECM franchises are at large banks.
- **Tier 1:** Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Citi, Bank of America, UBS (strong in Europe and Switzerland), Barclays, Deutsche Bank. Large global distribution networks, lead-left roles on the largest IPOs (Goldman was lead left underwriter on the SpaceX IPO per press cited in search results).
- **Tier 2:** BNP Paribas, HSBC, Société Générale, Mizuho, Nomura, Jefferies, Wells Fargo, RBC, Santander, Intesa Sanpaolo IMI and UniCredit (Italian ECM), Mediobanca (historically the lead Italian ECM house; see the 2025 change of control).
- **Tier 3:** Regional/local brokers and boutiques such as Equita (Italy), Stifel, Piper Sandler, Cantor, and Gulf and Asian local houses (SNB Capital, EFG Hermes, Riyad Capital, CICC, Nomura/Daiwa in Japan). Smaller placement power, more local specialisation.
Elite advisory boutiques (Centerview, PJT etc.) have little or no ECM franchise; a few (Lazard, Rothschild) offer limited equity advisory.

**How to enter**
Same programmes and calendar as 3.1; at most banks you apply to "Investment Banking" or "Capital Markets" and are allocated or choose a product group on joining. Citi lists London ECM placement-analyst roles (June 2026). *Degrees:* finance, economics, business; engineering with finance coursework works. *Skills:* equity valuation, understanding of IPO mechanics (book-building, pricing, stabilisation), market awareness (read the FT/Bloomberg daily), and a clear point of view on a recent IPO. *Mistakes:* treating ECM as a "lighter M&A" (it is not), not knowing recent deals, ignoring market-level risk. *Non-target routes:* ECM roles are rarely advertised outside the large banks; lateral moves from M&A, equity research or the syndicate/sales desks, or from a Big 4 corporate finance or capital markets team, are the realistic alternative. *Certification:* the FINRA SIE and Series 79 after joining in the US. Entry difficulty: 4/5 (smaller intake than M&A at each bank; competes for the same seats).

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**
Your income is tied to the equity market window: when markets sell off, IPOs and follow-ons stop and the team is idle but the bank may cut bonuses. The work is less analytical than M&A (more template-driven) and you have less control over the client relationship. In 2026 the market is dominated by a few giant deals, and a long-term hiring slowdown is possible if AI reduces the amount of junior work. Not ideal if you want deep company analysis (research or M&A fits better), strong sector expertise, or a predictable schedule. If you dislike market-dependent income, prefer corporate banking.

### 3.3 Debt capital markets and leveraged finance (DCM/LevFin)

**What you actually do**
*DCM (investment grade):* you help large, highly rated borrowers issue bonds. An analyst prepares market updates for clients (where comparable companies have recently priced), builds debt capacity and credit-ratio analyses, drafts pitch books and "mandate" materials, tracks the new-issue calendar and spreads, prepares term sheets and supports documentation and rating agency processes. On pricing day you coordinate with the syndicate desk (the people who talk to investors and build the order book): initial price thoughts, order-book progress, final pricing and allocation. *LevFin:* you structure and sell financing for sub-investment-grade companies, especially private equity buyouts: analyse a target's cash flows and capital structure to find how much debt it can carry, build the debt side of an LBO model, negotiate terms (pricing, covenants) with the sponsor and with lenders, and manage a "marketing" process to place the loan and bond with investors (CLOs, credit funds, insurers). LevFin is slower and heavier than investment-grade work: LBO financings can take months of credit analysis, covenant negotiation and syndication, whereas IG DCM can move in days or weeks (interview-prep source; consistent with structural differences). Syndicate roles on the markets side (running the book, allocating, publishing price thoughts) are a related, trading-floor-oriented career.

**A typical day and week**
DCM: morning market update (rates, credit spreads, issuance calendar), client calls to flag windows, building comparable-bond tables and sizing analyses; a pricing day compresses everything: launch in the morning, book updates every hour, final pricing at midday or afternoon, then documentation and settlement. Quiet periods before and after holidays (August, Christmas) and around central bank decisions. LevFin: long sponsor-driven weeks, with periods of intense negotiation on documents and "marketing" (lender meetings, calls with investors), then quieter weeks between deals. Hours follow bond market hours and deal timetables; weekends are mostly free in IG DCM and more affected in LevFin.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**
Hours: DCM typical 60-70, peaks 80-90 on pricing weeks; LevFin typical 70-80, peaks 95-100. Prep-site estimates for DCM run about 55-70 hours per week versus 70-90+ for M&A, and LevFin is described as "consistently among the longest in banking", comparable to M&A (one prep-site guide; all anecdotal). Stress: 4/5 (DCM: pricing days are high-stakes; LevFin: sponsor pressure and balance-sheet risk if debt cannot be sold). Lifestyle: DCM is usually described as the more lifestyle-friendly product group; LevFin is closer to M&A. Remote/travel: limited.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**
People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5 (DCM) to 4/5 (LevFin, because credit analysis and LBO debt modelling are central). Strong client and investor communication matters; credit judgement is the extra skill.

**Compensation (approximate)**
Same entry base as M&A at the same bank in each region (sources in 3.1); bonus typically somewhat lower than M&A (product groups average US$66,000 versus US$74,000 for M&A in Prospect Rock's August 2025 data; forum discussions say ECM/DCM bonuses are generally lower; anecdotal). A prep-site comparison estimated DCM analyst total comp at about US$230,000 for 60 hours versus M&A about US$250,000 for 80 hours; the numbers are unsourced and likely too high for the US median, but the direction (similar pay, fewer hours, higher per-hour pay) is what practitioners repeat. Senior: Prospect Rock's March 2026 report puts leveraged finance MDs with 4+ years at a median of about US$1.6 million, matching EB and BB medians. London, Milan, HK, Singapore, Dubai, Tokyo: no DCM/LevFin-specific data found; use 3.1 ranges as the ceiling.

**Career path**
Analyst (2-3 years) → Associate → VP → Director → MD, about the same pace as M&A. At the associate/VP level, cross-moves between DCM, LevFin and sponsor coverage are common. A bank may also place LevFin within a broader "Global Capital Markets" division.

**Exit opportunities**
After 2-3 years: private credit and direct lending funds (the most direct route; Ares, Blackstone Credit, Blue Owl, HPS, Oaktree and others hire from DCM, LevFin and restructuring - named as typical hirers in the project's Europe file, Oct 2026), credit hedge funds, CLO managers, fixed-income asset managers, corporate treasury, and private equity (possible from LevFin, less so from IG DCM). A forum-sourced observation: PE is atypical from DCM because modelling exposure is lighter. After 7-10 years: head of DCM/LevFin, credit fund partner or portfolio manager, corporate treasurer/CFO tracks, sponsor-side finance.

**Tier list of employers**
Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. Debt franchises depend on balance sheet and on distribution, so the biggest banks dominate.
- **Tier 1:** JPMorgan, Citi, Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Barclays, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, HSBC, UBS (LevFin: JPMorgan, Goldman, Morgan Stanley, BofA, Citi, Barclays, Deutsche are the large players; ranking varies by year).
- **Tier 2:** Wells Fargo, RBC, Credit Agricole CIB, Société Générale, Natixis, Santander, ING, UniCredit, Intesa Sanpaolo IMI, Mizuho, MUFG, SMBC, Jefferies, Nomura. In Italy, UniCredit and Intesa are the natural domestic bond/loan franchises (they sit within the CIB divisions that run graduate programmes, for example Intesa's I'M IN programme includes roles from rates solutions to credit trading, 2026).
- **Tier 3:** Regional banks, private-credit-adjacent boutiques and independent debt advisers (Houlihan Lokey's capital markets group, Lincoln International, PJT's Park Hill is a fund-placement arm, not DCM). Smaller deals, mid-cap focus.
Elite M&A boutiques (Evercore, PJT, Perella, Moelis) have debt advisory groups, but debt underwriting needs balance sheet.

**How to enter**
Join through the general IBD analyst programmes (same calendar as 3.1) and specify capital markets/DCM/LevFin, or via a "Global Capital Markets" programme; at some banks, applying through corporate banking or markets and lateral-ing in is possible. *Degrees:* finance, economics, accounting, quantitative degrees. *Skills:* credit analysis (leverage, coverage ratios), bond maths (yield, spread, duration) and LBO modelling; awareness of credit markets (spreads, high-yield index, CLO demand). *Mistakes:* talking only about equity-style valuation in interviews, not knowing basic bond concepts, and treating DCM as "easy". *Non-target routes:* start in corporate banking, ratings agencies, credit risk, or a Big 4 debt advisory/restructuring team and move in at the associate level; mid-market debt advisory firms hire more broadly. *Certifications:* FINRA SIE and Series 79 in the US; the CFA is occasionally useful for credit (project earlier file notes it helps more in asset management/credit than M&A). Entry difficulty: 4/5 (smaller group than M&A, with the same selectivity at large banks).

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**
LevFin hours are close to M&A, and DCM can feel formulaic once you master the process. Exits to PE are narrower than from M&A, though credit funds are an excellent destination if you want them. Revenue depends on credit-market windows and on refinancing waves; during a credit squeeze, mandates dry up. Banks that take balance-sheet risk can suffer losses, and bonuses fall with them. Not a good fit if you want a creative, advisory role or deep business strategy exposure; also not good if you dislike repeated documentation and process work.

### 3.4 Restructuring

**What you actually do**
You advise companies in or near distress, or the creditors who hold their debt (section 2.5). Typical analyst work: building a 13-week cash-flow forecast and liquidity model (distressed companies run out of cash, so weekly cash is the central number), a "recovery waterfall" (who gets paid what, in order of priority, under different valuation outcomes), a capital-structure summary (every loan and bond, security, maturity, covenants), a business plan model, and comparisons of options: refinancing, an out-of-court amend-and-extend, an LME such as an uptier exchange, a sale of assets, or a court process (Chapter 11 in the US, UK restructuring plans or schemes of arrangement). You also prepare presentations for creditors and the board, support negotiations, and, in court cases, support expert-testimony materials. The work blends M&A (distressed sales), debt advice and legal/process knowledge. Because restructuring touches documents and law, you work with restructuring lawyers constantly.

**A typical day and week**
Highly situation-driven. In a live case: early morning call with the company's CFO and advisers about cash; mid-morning update of the liquidity model; afternoon meeting with creditors' advisers; evening work on a term sheet or court filing materials. Many cases run in sprints around bond payment dates, covenant tests, grace periods and court deadlines; weekends are common during a filing. When the cycle is slow, the team does pitches to companies approaching maturity "walls" and monitors credit markets. Travel to client sites and to court locations is more frequent than in M&A (UK/US/European cases). Hybrid working is limited in live cases.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**
Hours: typical 70-85, peaks of 100+ around a filing or deadline; I found no 2025-26 survey isolating restructuring hours, so this is a practitioner estimate. Stress: 4-5/5 (high stakes, liquidity running out, emotionally charged negotiations, but fewer pitch-book revisions than in coverage). Lifestyle: lumpy; busy when credit is stressed. Restructuring groups are often said to have a more mature, less formatting-driven culture, and they are one of the places where analysts get early responsibility; this is practitioner perception, not measured.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**
People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You need strong accounting and credit logic (cash, covenants, priority of claims) and the ability to negotiate and manage tense meetings; it rewards people who like puzzles combining numbers, legal structure and people.

**Compensation (approximate)**
Entry pay follows the same bank/boutique analyst scales as M&A at the same employer; Prospect Rock (2023) states that restructuring analyst pay is "competitive with other IB roles" and gives no figures. The best proxies are analyst totals at restructuring-heavy firms in London: Houlihan Lokey about £115,000 (base £61-70k plus £30-80k bonus), PJT Partners about £110,000, Rothschild & Co about £104,000, Lazard about £90,000, Perella Weinberg about £119,000 (PrepLounge, 27 August 2026; aggregator). US figures as 3.1 (base US$105-110k for juniors; total around US$150-170k+ depending on year and bucket). Senior-level restructuring MD pay: no group-specific figure found. Italy: no reliable data found; Milan restructuring teams at Rothschild, Lazard, Houlihan Lokey, UniCredit/Intesa special situations and Big 4 pay on the local scales in 3.1 and 3.6. Hong Kong, Singapore, Dubai, Tokyo: no reliable data found.

**Career path**
Analyst → Associate → VP → Director → MD, with specialisation often starting at associate or VP level (Prospect Rock). Many teams are small, so promotion depends on revenue from a few mandates; lateral moves between restructuring and debt advisory or M&A are common. In Europe, restructuring bankers often sit within "financing solutions and restructuring" groups (Rothschild & Co's Global Financing Solutions & Restructuring group has over 200 bankers in 14 countries and advises on over US$200 billion of capital raises and restructurings each year, per its own programme page).

**Exit opportunities**
After 2-3 years: distressed credit and special-situations hedge funds, direct lending/private credit, distressed or special-situations PE, M&A or coverage groups, turnaround consulting, corporate development. Exit evidence is mostly from career guides and forums; the common claim is that credit funds are more typical than classic buyout PE, and that creditor-side experience helps for distressed funds (anecdotal). After 7-10 years: partner at a restructuring boutique, credit fund portfolio manager or head of a special-situations team, chief restructuring officer roles, or in-house at distressed investors.

**Tier list of employers**
Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.
- **Tier 1:** Houlihan Lokey, PJT Partners, Lazard, Rothschild & Co, Evercore, Perella Weinberg, Moelis. Houlihan Lokey topped LSEG's 2025 global distressed and restructuring tables by deal count (83 versus PJT 55, Lazard 50, Rothschild 44, Evercore 33; figures from Houlihan Lokey's own site, snippet-level). In Debtwire's Q2/H1 2026 Europe table, PJT led by number of mandates (8), ahead of Houlihan Lokey (7) and Rothschild (6).
- **Tier 2:** Large bulge-bracket restructuring/liability-management groups (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan, Citi, Barclays, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, Lazard-like European teams), Ducera, Solomon Partners, Centerview (restructuring group), Greenhill (now part of Mizuho), Jefferies, Guggenheim, Gordian, GLC Advisors (recruiting for restructuring in its 2026 analyst class per a job-board posting, snippet-level).
- **Tier 3:** Operational restructuring and turnaround advisers (Alvarez & Marsal, FTI Consulting, Big 4 restructuring teams: they were among the Q2 2026 European leaders by mandates), mid-market and regional boutiques (Stephens, Raymond James, Oppenheimer, local Italian and German advisers). Different work: more operational and process-heavy, lower pay, shorter hours.

**How to enter**
Programmes and calendar as in 3.1: Rothschild's Global Financing Solutions & Restructuring summer analyst programme (London, 10 weeks from June 2027, requires an expected 2:1 and interest in corporate finance and debt markets; interns work on refinancings, acquisitions, LBOs, IPO financings and restructurings; applications opened 19 August 2026, rolling). Most boutiques hire into an "M&A or restructuring" pool and you choose a team after joining. *Skills:* accounting and credit, reading debt documents, cash-flow forecasting, knowledge of bankruptcy and restructuring processes (a plus), and recent case study knowledge (interviews reportedly ask about recent bankruptcies and recovery waterfalls; anecdotal). *Degrees:* finance, accounting, economics, law with finance, engineering. *Mistakes:* assuming restructuring is "losers' banking" (it is core at elite boutiques), not knowing basic bankruptcy mechanics, or not following current LME developments. *Alternative routes:* Big 4 restructuring or transaction services, credit rating agencies, or law/accountancy then lateral into advisory at associate level (post-grad restructuring talent is in demand: lateral hiring is strong amid LMEs and private credit, per a June 2026 trade article, snippet-level). Entry difficulty: 4/5 overall (5/5 at the best boutiques with few seats; 3/5 at Big 4 restructuring).

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**
Counter-cyclical demand means a long, unpredictable swing; the work can be emotionally heavy (job losses, creditor fights, litigation), with high-intensity periods; it is a narrower market than M&A, so transferability depends on the cycle. Hours rival M&A. Not a good fit if you dislike conflict, legal complexity, working with troubled clients, or very lumpy workloads; also not ideal if you want a classic private equity buyout exit as the main goal (exits to credit and special situations are the strongest).

### 3.5 Corporate and transaction banking

**What you actually do**
*Corporate banking / relationship management:* you manage relationships with companies, from medium-sized firms to large multinationals, and sell them loans, credit lines, payment and cash management services, trade finance, currency and interest-rate hedging, and, through the investment bank, bond issues or M&A advice. As an analyst or graduate you prepare credit papers (the internal credit approval memos with financial analysis, covenants, risk grading), monitor client financials, price loans, support client meetings, and coordinate with product specialists, credit, risk and legal teams. Barclays describes its UK corporate bank graduates as working with clients from mid-sized businesses to FTSE 350 firms, rotating through support functions, and aiming for a career as a corporate banker or Relationship Director (Barclays careers, accessed October 2026); HSBC's two-year Corporate and Institutional Banking programme rotates through relationship banking and product teams, including trade finance, cash management and capital markets. *Transaction banking:* product specialists and sales who design and deliver cash management, payments, liquidity, supply-chain finance and trade finance solutions; at Citi it is called Treasury and Trade Solutions (TTS), at Deutsche Bank Global Transaction Banking. Work is more operational and client-service oriented, with technology and regulation (sanctions, anti-money-laundering) as important parts.

**A typical day and week**
Meetings with clients' finance teams (CFOs, treasurers) and internal credit teams; preparing credit approvals and annual reviews; responding to client requests (new facilities, covenant waivers, payment issues). Weeks follow client and credit-committee calendars with moderate peaks around large financings or year-end reviews. Travel is common for relationship managers (client visits), minimal for support analysts. Hybrid/remote work is more accepted than in IBD.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**
Hours: typical 45-55 per week, peaks of 60-65 for large financings or reviews. I found no survey for these roles; this is a practitioner-based estimate consistent with the general view that corporate banking is "generally less intense" than IBD (no hard data found). Stress: 3/5 (credit risk, client expectations, regulation), lower than IBD. Lifestyle: more predictable, hybrid friendly, greater long-term stability. Flexibility: higher than banking at junior levels.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**
People/communication: 5/5 (this is a relationships job). Quantitative/technical: 3/5 (credit and financial statement analysis, not advanced maths; transaction banking adds product, payments and regulatory knowledge).

**Compensation (approximate)**
*United States:* JPMorgan's Global Corporate Banking summer analyst role in New York listed US$100,000 a year (2025 posting); an experienced analyst in the Consumer, Retail & Business Services group listed a US$105,000-110,000 base; Wells Fargo's Financial Institutions Group corporate banking analyst role listed US$100,000-110,000 base (February 2026 posting); bonus not disclosed. Self-reported bonuses are generally lower than IBD analysts (WSO data on middle-market and bulge-bracket analysts of US$65-75k are for IB, not corporate banking; the closest proxy, anecdotal). *United Kingdom:* HSBC's Commercial Banking graduate programme listed £35,000 plus a £5,000 welcome bonus (2025 intake, not London-based); Barclays does not publish a figure; Indeed's HSBC analyst average is about £43,700 (generic title; unreliable for this role). No reliable corporate-banking-specific London figure found; practitioner talk is that CIB-aligned corporate banking graduates in London earn below IBD analysts (unverified). *Italy:* Intesa Sanpaolo I'M IN CIB programme from EUR 40,000 gross a year (8 July 2026, secondary source); UniCredit Milan graduate analyst EUR 40,000 gross base (posting, undated). Both programmes also include non-corporate-banking roles (rates, credit trading, M&A). Bonuses are smaller than in IBD (unverified). *Hong Kong/Singapore/Dubai/Tokyo:* no reliable data found; a Michael Page Singapore guide shows a corporate banking relationship manager, executive director band of S$250,000-350,000 (2026; snippet-level), which indicates that senior relationship managers can earn comfortably but tells you nothing about entry pay. Seniors: Michael Page (Singapore 2026) as above; no reliable data for other regions.

**Career path**
Graduate/analyst (2-3 years rotation) → Associate/Assistant relationship manager (credit paper authorship) → Relationship Manager or Vice President (own a portfolio, 3-5 years) → Director/Senior RM (large corporates or sector heads) → Managing Director/Head of corporate banking. Transaction banking: analyst → sales/product specialist → head of region/product. Promotions are slower and more stable than IBD; many people stay in the same bank for long periods. A common internal move is into investment banking coverage or credit risk.

**Exit opportunities**
After 2-3 years: coverage or capital-markets roles in the same bank, credit risk, treasury or corporate finance at a corporate, private credit and direct lending (analyst/associate), leveraged finance at the same bank, fintech and payments companies (for transaction banking). After 7-10 years: head of a corporate banking team, corporate CFO/treasurer, direct lending portfolio manager, chief risk roles, or senior fintech roles. Moves into mega-fund private equity from corporate banking are unusual (my assessment; unverified).

**Tier list of employers**
Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.
- **Tier 1 (global corporate banking franchises):** JPMorgan, Citi (its TTS franchise), HSBC, Bank of America, Barclays, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, Santander, Wells Fargo (US). Large-cap clients, global product range, integrated with investment banking.
- **Tier 2:** National champions: Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit (Italy), ING, ABN AMRO, Commerzbank, Société Générale, BBVA, Lloyds, NatWest, Mizuho/MUFG/SMBC (Japanese megabanks, global reach), FAB and Emirates NBD (Gulf). Mid and large domestic corporates, strong local relationships.
- **Tier 3:** Regional and savings banks, mid-sized domestic banks (Italy's Banco BPM, BPER, banche popolari, Germany's Landesbanken, US regional banks). Smaller corporates, lower pay, less international exposure, but often more local autonomy.

**How to enter**
Graduate programmes are the main route and are large and open to a wide range of degrees: Barclays UK Corporate Bank Graduate programme (rotations, 2027 intake listed), HSBC Corporate and Institutional Banking Graduate Programme (two years) and Commercial Banking Graduate Programme (UK; three six-month rotations), Citi corporate banking and TTS summer and graduate analyst programmes, Deutsche Bank Corporate Bank graduate programmes, and, in Italy, the CIB graduate schemes of Intesa and UniCredit (master's in economics, finance, maths or statistics, English B2). *Degrees:* any degree is common in the UK (business, economics, accounting, finance and others); Italian programmes expect an economics/finance/quantitative master's. *Calendar:* similar to UK graduate recruitment (applications from August-September for the following year; deadlines vary; I found no consolidated calendar for 2026-27). *Skills:* financial statement analysis, credit basics, communication and negotiation, sector knowledge, product knowledge for transaction banking (trade finance instruments, payments). *Certifications:* none required; a banking/credit certificate (such as the Chartered Banker or credit qualifications) can help in the UK (unverified); the CFA is of limited use. *Mistakes:* treating it as a fallback with no preparation, ignoring the importance of knowing clients' sectors, and not asking about rotation structure. *Alternative routes:* come from audit, credit risk or sector roles, or from a regional bank. Entry difficulty: 2/5 to 3/5 (many openings and large intakes, still competitive at global banks).

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**
Lower pay ceiling than IBD for equivalent seniority; more regulation, credit committee processes and paperwork; slower promotions; revenue depends on interest rates (margins fall when rates fall) and on credit losses. Roles are often tied to a portfolio or geography. Not a good fit if you want the intensity and deal exposure of M&A, are driven by the largest possible pay quickly, or dislike credit and compliance. Better fit if you want client relationships, stability, hybrid work, and a pathway into credit, treasury or corporate finance.

### 3.6 Transaction services / valuation / corporate finance advisory at Big 4 and boutiques

**What you actually do**
*Transaction services (TS) / financial due diligence (FDD):* you test a company's numbers for a buyer (often private equity), a seller (vendor due diligence) or a lender. Typical tasks: reconciling management accounts to statutory accounts, adjusting EBITDA to "quality of earnings" (removing one-offs, normalising costs), analysing revenue by customer and product, working capital seasonality and "normal" level, net debt and "debt-like items" (pensions, leases, deferred payments), cash conversion, and the reasonableness of the business plan. You interview management, work in data rooms, produce a written report and workbooks, and present to the client. In the US, work is heavily historical; in Europe teams also review forecasts and do more modelling (Mergers & Inquisitions, article with 2022 figures). *Valuation:* purchase price allocation after an acquisition, impairment tests, valuations for tax, disputes and financial reporting. *Corporate finance advisory:* sell-side and buy-side advice for mid-market deals (Big 4 CF teams and independent boutiques), similar to a mid-market bank's M&A work but with accounting-led diligence and fixed-fee/time-based economics.

**A typical day and week**
FDD: a deal timeline drives the pace: 2-6 weeks of intense diligence, with daily calls with the deal team and the client, then a report deadline, then quieter weeks or the next project. Team structure is a pyramid (analyst/associate, senior associate, manager, director, partner). Weeks are more predictable than banking outside report deadlines; late nights cluster around report delivery and signing (Finbound UK guide, 27 June 2026, no numbers). Valuation teams have steadier schedules with peaks at year-end reporting. Client site visits and some travel, particularly for cross-border deals (KPMG describes a high volume of cross-border deals and looks for second European languages in its Deal Advisory pages, older 2019 PDF). Hybrid working is normal.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle**
Hours: typical 50-60 per week, peaks of 70-80 during report deadlines (Mergers & Inquisitions suggests 50-60 hours with occasional late nights and weekends; no recent survey found). Stress: 3/5 (regular deadlines, steady pressure). Lifestyle: noticeably more predictable than banking, more training and structure, hybrid and part-time options. The "Big 4 busy season" is audit-specific; TS follows deal flow.

**Human vs. quantitative profile**
People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5 (accounting is the main skill: strong understanding of financial statements, Excel, data analysis; valuation teams use more modelling).

**Compensation (approximate)**
*United Kingdom (London):* Big 4 graduate starting salaries around £30,000-36,000, with KPMG typically about £34,500, EY £30,000-36,000, PwC and Deloitte £30,000-35,000 (City AM, 29 September 2026; firm-wide, not TS-specific); Grant Thornton will pay £40,000 to London graduates from 2027 (same article). I found no published TS-specific graduate pay. Mid-tier firms pay about 10-20% less than the Big 4 (search summary, unverified). Bonuses are modest. *United States:* Mergers & Inquisitions (figures as of 2022, a dated source) quotes TS associate base US$80,000-100,000 (total US$100,000-130,000), senior associate US$115,000-145,000 base, manager US$150,000-190,000 base, and director US$220,000-300,000 base (total US$290,000-390,000); a Boston advisory-firm posting listed US$100,000-130,000 for a transaction services senior associate (job-board listing, undated). *Italy:* Big 4 entry-level gross pay of EUR 24,000-28,000 (Trend Online, 12 June 2024; across Big 4 service lines, no TS-specific figure); Indeed shows a Deloitte junior finance and accounting analyst in Milan at EUR 25,000-28,000; internships pay a reimbursement of EUR 400-800 a month. *Continental Europe, HK, Singapore, Dubai, Tokyo:* no reliable TS-specific data found. A Singapore Michael Page 2026 guide lists an M&A Associate band of S$105,000-175,000 (corporate finance role, snippet-level). Seniors: US director to partner figures above; UK partner pay: no reliable data found.

**Career path**
Analyst/associate (2-3 years; professional qualification study, for example ACA/ACCA in the UK or CPA in the US, often alongside) → senior associate/senior consultant → manager (4-6 years) → senior manager/director (7-10 years) → partner (12+ years, competitive). Lateral exits are common at manager level.

**Exit opportunities**
After 2-3 years: investment banking at middle-market or boutique banks (moderately plausible; the Mergers & Inquisitions article says it is possible at boutique and middle-market banks in the first one to three years and harder after four to six), corporate development, FP&A, other advisory roles, transaction advisory at PE-backed businesses. After 7-10 years: private equity in mid-market funds (moderate to strong plausibility per the Finbound guide, harder for mega-funds), corporate development head or CFO, or staying for partner. Evidence base for exits is career guides and coaching sites, not placement data; the claims that "TS is a springboard to IB" and "TS to PE is possible but harder than IB to PE" are consistent across them.

**Tier list of employers**
Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.
- **Tier 1:** Big 4 transaction/deal teams, ordered by practitioners variously: PwC Deals, EY-Parthenon Transaction Advisory (TAS), KPMG Deal Advisory, Deloitte Financial Advisory. Differences between them are small and vary by country; the major point is brand, deal flow with PE, and training. Also top corporate-finance boutiques and valuation specialists: Kroll (valuation), Houlihan Lokey's valuation arm, Duff & Phelps (now Kroll), Alvarez & Marsal (transaction advisory and restructuring), FTI Consulting, Lazard's financial advisory arm.
- **Tier 2:** Mid-tier accounting firms with strong deal teams: Grant Thornton, BDO, RSM, Mazars/Forvis Mazars, PKF (BDO and PKF list deal advisory graduate roles), Baker Tilly, plus independent corporate-finance houses such as Clearwater International and Cavendish in the UK (unverified list; Italian mid-market advisers are covered in section 2.6).
- **Tier 3:** Regional accountancies, local valuation shops and small corporate-finance boutiques; narrower deal flow, less brand recognition, more generalist work.

**How to enter**
Big 4 graduate schemes recruit autumn to winter for the following autumn start, with spring/summer internships and industrial placements as pipeline stages (Finbound UK guide, 27 June 2026). KPMG's Deal Advisory UK offers streams that include Transaction Services, Corporate Finance and Restructuring (KPMG careers pages; older programme PDFs). PwC Deals trainees and graduates are recruited in many countries (for example, a Finnish PwC FDD team of about 40 people and a 2-6 month trainee programme). In Italy the entry is via stage (internship) at Big 4 deal advisory practices followed by a fixed-term/permanent contract; EUR 400-800 monthly internship reimbursements are common (Trend Online, 2024). *Degrees:* accounting, finance, economics and business are best, but engineering, maths or law with strong Excel and accounting skills are accepted; many graduates start in audit and then move into TS after 12-24 months (an internal route that takes 2-3 years, per M&I). *Qualification:* ICAEW ACA or ACCA (UK), CPA (US), dottore commercialista/revisore legale (Italy, my understanding, unverified); a qualification is not mandatory for TS in all firms but strengthens your progression. *Skills:* accounting and Excel, data analysis (increasingly Alteryx/Power BI), concise report writing, commercial curiosity, and interviewing management. *Mistakes:* applying only to banks and ignoring the Big 4 as a route; assuming TS is "easy IB"; not understanding what a diligence report is. *Alternative routes:* start in audit and rotate; join a mid-tier firm and lateral to a Big 4 deals team; or join a corporate-finance boutique through direct graduate or internship applications. Entry difficulty: 3/5 (large intakes and open to many degrees, but a competitive assessment process and a push for strong academics and internships; 4/5 for EY-Parthenon or top boutique roles).

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**
Pay is materially lower than in banking at equivalent seniority (a forum poster estimates a 1.5-2x gap; anecdotal); the work is often retrospective ("what happened"), not strategic; you are an adviser to an adviser, as TS is often brought in late in a deal and can be dropped from the timeline; firms are pyramid-shaped and promotion to partner is highly competitive; conflicts of interest (audit independence rules) can restrict which clients you serve. Not a good fit if you want the prestige and pay of top investment banking and are unwilling to take the extra step later, or if you dislike detailed accounting work and reports. Better fit if you want deal exposure with more predictable hours and a stronger accounting grounding.

**Commentary.**
- *Bulge bracket vs elite boutique:* Base pay is similar; the difference is in bonus, deal quality and exit story. Prospect Rock's March 2026 report shows boutiques ahead at associate level but bulge brackets catching up from executive director level. Boutiques are all advisory, so juniors see more strategic deals and more senior contact. Bulge brackets offer product breadth (lending, ECM, DCM), bigger training programmes and wider internal mobility, including to markets.
- *Middle market:* the best entry for non-target candidates, with wider hiring and faster responsibility; exits to PE exist but are more concentrated in mid-market and smaller funds.
- *Regional/European banks:* better stability and local relationships; lower pay; Italian and other domestic banks hire through structured graduate programmes with permanent contracts, which is unusual in London.
- *Big 4 deal advisory:* a real alternative for people who want deal exposure at lower intensity, with a recognised accounting qualification; lateral moves into banking are most plausible in the first years.
- *In-house corporate development:* the common "lifestyle" landing spot for bankers after 2-4 years (eFinancialCareers, 2022, reported a post-pandemic exodus to corp dev); an entry-level corp dev job straight from university is rare because companies usually hire from banks, consulting and TS. The best data are a forum commenter and older articles; treat the numbers as indicative only.
