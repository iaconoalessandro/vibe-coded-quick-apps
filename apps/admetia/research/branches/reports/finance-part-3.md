# Finance, part 3: The buy side

## 1. What this branch is

The "buy side" is the part of finance that manages other people's money and decides what to buy, hold or sell: asset managers, hedge funds, private equity, venture capital, private credit funds, the big institutions that hand money to those managers (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, endowments, insurers), and the private banks that look after wealthy families. It is called the buy side because these firms are the clients of investment banks and brokers (the "sell side", covered in parts 1 and 2). Buy-side employers are usually small, pay a lot for a few people and ask for evidence of judgment rather than just hard work. Entry is competitive and often indirect: many of the most-wanted roles (private equity, credit, hedge fund analyst) are filled mainly from people who first spent two to three years at an investment bank, a consultancy or another fund.

Context for 2026: global assets under management reached about US$147 trillion in 2025, up 11%, but BCG estimates that more than 80% of revenue growth came from market gains rather than new client money (BCG, 24th Global Asset Management Report, via Alternatives Watch, 28 April 2026). Hedge fund industry capital hit a record of about US$5.6 trillion in Q2 2026 (HFR, Q2 2026, via Hedgeweek). Private equity (PE) is busy on paper but cash is not coming back to investors: distributions have been under 15% of net asset value for four straight years (Bain, Global Private Equity Report 2026, 23 February 2026). Private credit, the fastest-growing area of the last decade, is under real stress in 2026 (see 2.6).

## 2. Map of areas and sectors

### 2.1 Buy side vs sell side, and who the end investors are

The **sell side** (investment banks, brokers) advises companies, creates and trades securities and earns fees and trading spreads. The **buy side** takes capital from investors and invests it. The money ultimately comes from:

- **Pension funds** (public and corporate): collect contributions from workers and employers and pay retirees decades later. Examples: CalPERS, CPP Investments, Dutch APG and PGGM, Japan's GPIF (about ¥260 trillion in assets, Bloomberg, 11 August 2025).
- **Insurers**: invest premiums to meet future claims; they like predictable income, which is one reason insurers have become big buyers of private credit (BCG via Alternatives Watch, 28 April 2026).
- **Sovereign wealth funds (SWFs)**: state-owned pools funded by commodity income or reserves. Norway's fund (managed by NBIM) is above US$2 trillion; ADIA is estimated at US$1.18 trillion, Mubadala US$358 billion and ADQ US$251 billion (Global SWF 2026 figures, as reported by secondary sources; several funds, including ADIA, GIC and SAFE, do not publish official figures).
- **Endowments and foundations** (Yale, Harvard, university and charity funds) and **family offices** (private investment teams run for one or several wealthy families).
- **Retail investors and wealthy individuals**: buy mutual funds, ETFs and, increasingly, private-market products through banks and advisers.

How the buy side earns money differs by business: **management fees** (a percentage of assets, e.g. 0.05% on an index fund up to roughly 1-2% on active or alternative funds), **performance fees** (a share of profits, commonly 20% in hedge funds), **carried interest** or "carry" (the manager's share, typically 20%, of a private fund's profit after investors get their money back plus a minimum return), and **interest margin** (lending at a spread above the cost of funds, in private credit and banks).

### 2.2 Asset management (long-only and passive)

**Long-only active** managers (Capital Group, Fidelity, Wellington, T. Rowe Price, Schroders, Amundi, Eurizon) buy securities they believe will beat a benchmark and cannot short. They earn a management fee on assets, so revenue rises when markets rise and when clients add money, and falls when they leave. Teams specialise by asset class: equities, fixed income, multi-asset.

**Passive/index** managers (Vanguard, BlackRock iShares, State Street) track an index at a tiny fee. In the US, passive funds and ETFs overtook active funds in total assets around the end of 2023 (about US$13.29 trillion vs US$13.23 trillion, Morningstar data as cited by Walnut Invest); the average index equity mutual fund charged about 0.05% in 2024 against 0.64% for active (same secondary source, to be checked against ICI). In 2025 only 38% of active strategies both survived and beat their passive peers, and over ten years only 21% (Morningstar, 2025 data via Walnut Invest). **Why it matters for careers:** fee pressure shrinks revenue per employee, so traditional active managers hire fewer analysts, push for cost cuts, and value distribution and product skills more than before. BCG describes distribution and scalable models as the deciding factor for future growth (BCG via Alternatives Watch, 28 April 2026). The industry's global fee yield fell from 0.25% of assets in 2015 to 0.22% in 2023 (BCG, 2024 edition). Italy's managed-savings market is large: a record €2,722 billion at end-June 2026 (Assogestioni via Teleborsa, 18 September 2026).

### 2.3 Hedge funds, strategy by strategy

A hedge fund takes money from institutions and rich individuals, uses short selling, leverage and derivatives, and charges a management fee plus a performance fee. Typical convention is "2 and 20" historically; large multi-managers now often pass through costs to investors instead (Citadel's bond prospectus reportedly shows about US$17 billion of pass-through expenses, almost 90% employee pay; paid Substack summary, unverified).

- **Long/short equity**: buys stocks expected to rise and shorts those expected to fall. Analysts spend time on company models, management meetings and channel checks. HFR's equity hedge index returned +9.6% in the first half of 2026, with technology funds +19.0% (HFR, H1 2026).
- **Global macro**: bets on interest rates, currencies, commodities and equity indices based on economic views. HFR's macro index returned +6.1% in H1 2026, systematic CTA (trend-following) strategies +9.7%. Few seats exist; many macro PMs are former bank traders.
- **Event-driven / merger arbitrage**: trades situations tied to corporate events (mergers, spin-offs, activism, bankruptcies). Merger arbitrage buys the target at a small discount to the deal price and bets the deal closes. Event-driven returned +7.4% in H1 2026, distressed +12.4% (HFR). Legal reading (merger agreements, antitrust) is a big part of the job.
- **Credit / distressed**: invests in corporate bonds and loans, from performing credit to bankrupt companies. Overlaps with private credit and restructuring advisory.
- **Relative value / fixed-income arbitrage**: exploits small price gaps between related instruments (for example government bond vs futures) using high leverage. Very quantitative, small number of seats, and prone to sudden losses when markets seize up.
- **Multi-strategy / multi-manager "pod" platforms** (Millennium, Citadel, Balyasny, Point72, ExodusPoint, Schonfeld): the firm allocates capital to dozens or hundreds of independent PM teams ("pods"), each with strict risk limits, and charges investors a fee including pass-through costs. Pod PMs are paid a percentage of their own pod's profit, reported at roughly 12-25% (eFinancialCareers, 25 August 2026, citing a former Balyasny analyst's post) or 12-20% of net P&L at Millennium (Quantt estimate, 2026). Stop-loss rules are strict: sources repeat that a drawdown near 5% halves capital and about 7.5% ends the pod, but that comes from LinkedIn posts, not from the firms (unverified). Pods are cut mercilessly: practitioners cite PM median tenures of roughly 2-3 years (unverified, LinkedIn). In March 2026 the model was stress-tested: Balyasny fell 4.3% in the month and was down 3.8% year to date, ExodusPoint fell 4.5% (HedgeCo, April 2026). By Q2 2026 industry capital recovered strongly; investors favour big platforms: firms above US$5 billion took US$38.1 billion of Q2 net inflows against US$6.3 billion for mid-sized and US$0.7 billion for small ones (HFR).
- **Quantitative / systematic funds** (Two Sigma, D. E. Shaw, Renaissance, AQR, Man AHL): trade on models. Covered in the quant part of this branch.

### 2.4 Private equity

PE firms raise "blind pool" funds from institutions, buy whole companies (usually using borrowed money), improve them over about 4-7 years and sell them. Fees: roughly 1-2% management fee on committed capital plus 20% carry over a hurdle, typical industry convention.

- **Buyout**: large-cap (mega-funds such as Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, CVC, EQT), mid-market (companies worth a few hundred million to a couple of billion) and lower mid-market (smaller family businesses, many in Italy). Large funds mostly rely on financial engineering and leverage; smaller funds rely more on operational improvement and sourcing.
- **Growth equity**: minority investments in fast-growing profitable companies, with less leverage (General Atlantic, TA Associates, Summit).
- **Secondaries**: buying existing fund stakes or "continuation vehicles" from other sponsors. Record volume of about US$240 billion in 2025, up 48% (Jefferies Global Secondary Market Review, 2025; other houses report US$220-229 billion).
- **Infrastructure**: long-life assets such as airports, grids, data centres; infrastructure fundraising was one of the few bright spots of 2025 (Bain, 2026).
- **Real estate PE** (brief): funds buying and developing property; more sensitive to interest rates.

State of the market: global buyout deal value rose 44% to about US$904 billion in 2025 and exit value rose 47% to about US$717 billion, but around 32,000 unsold companies worth about US$3.8 trillion sit in portfolios, average holding periods have stretched to about seven years and fundraising was about US$1.3 trillion, flat on 2024 (Bain, 23 February 2026). Pay is flat because the money is not flowing back: Johnson Associates projected 2025 PE bonuses flat to up 5% at large firms and flat at midsize and small firms (Johnson Associates via AI-CIO, 10 November 2025). In Italy, 2025 PE and VC investment was €11.61 billion, down 22%, fundraising fell 46% to €3.57 billion, and 73% of invested value came from foreign operators (AIFI-PwC via Teleborsa, 23 March 2026).

### 2.5 Venture capital

VC funds invest in young companies, in stages: **seed** (first cheques, often €0.5-3 million, on a team and idea), **early stage** (Series A/B, proving a product and revenue), **growth** (later rounds before an IPO or sale). **Corporate venture capital** (CVC) is the venture arm of a large company (Intel Capital, Salesforce Ventures, and in Italy, corporate funds of banks and utilities), investing for strategic as well as financial reasons.

VC returns follow a **power law**: a very small number of investments produces most of the profit. In Horsley Bridge data shared with Andreessen Horowitz, roughly 6% of investments produced about 60% of returns (secondary summaries); Correlation Ventures' 2004-2013 dataset found 65% of financings returned less than 1x and only 4% returned 10x or more (older data, repeated in blogs; I could not verify the originals). Practically: a VC job is judged on finding rare outliers, you will be wrong most of the time, and the first signals of success take 7-10 years. In 2025 Johnson projected VC bonuses flat (AI-CIO, 10 November 2025). Italy's VC deals rose 30% to 568 in 2025, but at €1.35 billion invested, the market is small (AIFI-PwC, 23 March 2026).

### 2.6 Private credit

Private credit is lending to companies by funds rather than banks, mainly to mid-sized companies owned by private equity firms. **Direct lending** (senior loans, the largest part); **mezzanine** (junior, higher-yield, sometimes with equity kickers); **special situations / opportunistic** (rescue financing, distressed buys); **asset-based finance** (loans against receivables, inventory, equipment, consumer loans, aircraft). The manager earns a management fee, often a performance fee, and the loans earn an interest margin.

Why it grew: after 2008, tighter bank capital rules made banks cut risky leveraged lending, and pension funds and insurers, facing low yields, wanted more income (BlackRock's explanation, 2025). Estimates of size vary from about US$1.7 trillion to US$3.5 trillion depending on definitions: the Alternative Credit Council says about US$3.5 trillion at end-2024; Preqin passed US$2 trillion in 2024; BlackRock forecasts US$4.5 trillion by 2030. The second leg of growth since 2022 came from higher interest rates (floating-rate loans paid more) and banks pulling back again.

2026 is the first real stress test: non-traded BDC (business development company, a listed or semi-listed lending vehicle) redemption requests averaged 4.8% of net asset value in Q4 2025 (up from 1.6% in Q3), with one large fund seeing about 14% in early 2026; software loans, about 18% of S&P-rated mid-market CLO assets, were bid at about 87.7 in May 2026, roughly 990 basis points wider than non-software loans (Alvarez & Marsal, 15 July 2026; Alternative Credit Investor, 27 July 2026). Sources disagree on defaults: Mercer cites trailing direct-lending defaults of 1.1% and KBRA forecasts 2.0% for 2026, while another source cites UBS predicting a doubling (Mercer, 16 April 2026; A&M). Hiring: a London headhunter said private credit jobs were down up to 25% in October 2024 (eFinancialCareers, 16 October 2024); I found no 2026 hiring data. A secondary report says Johnson projects 2026 private credit bonuses down 2.5-7.5% (unverified; Johnson's 2025 projection was +5-10%).

### 2.7 Institutional allocators and consultants

Allocators decide how much goes to which asset class and which manager. In-house teams of **pension funds** (CPP Investments, Ontario Teachers', CalPERS, APG, GPIF), **insurers' investment arms** (Allianz, AXA, Generali), **SWFs** (NBIM, GIC, Temasek, ADIA, Mubadala, PIF), and **endowments** (Yale, Harvard) both select outside managers and invest directly. **Fund-of-funds** (e.g., Hamilton Lane, StepStone, Partners Group) pool client money into many private funds. **Investment consultants** (Mercer, WTW, Cambridge Associates, Aon) advise pension schemes and endowments on managers and strategy, and are a common first job (see 3.7). Gulf SWFs are very active: record commitments of US$53.9 billion across 108 deals in H1 2026 (Global SWF via Khaleej Times). Global state-owned investors, including pensions and central banks, hold US$62.5 trillion (Global SWF, H1 2026).

### 2.8 Private wealth management and private banking

Private banks and wealth managers (UBS, Julius Baer, Pictet, Lombard Odier, Fideuram-Intesa Sanpaolo Private Banking, Mediobanca Private Banking, UniCredit Private Banking, Morgan Stanley, Merrill, JPMorgan Private Bank) manage portfolios and offer lending, tax-aware and succession advice for wealthy individuals. They earn fees on assets (often 0.5-1.5% a year) plus lending margin, and the relationship manager (RM) brings in the clients. It is the largest buy-side-adjacent employer of client-facing graduates, and the wealth segment is growing: Johnson projected 2025 wealth-management bonuses up 8-10% (AI-CIO, 10 November 2025). In Italy many "consulenti finanziari" in networks (reti) are self-employed agents paid by commission, not employees, which changes both risk and pay.

## 3. Role families

### 3.1 Asset management investment roles (research analyst to portfolio manager), long-only

- **What you actually do**: Analysts cover a sector (e.g. European banks, US healthcare) and decide whether stocks or bonds in it are worth owning. You build and update financial models, read filings, listen to earnings calls, visit companies, talk to management and industry experts, write recommendations (often a 2-10 page note or a pitch to the PM) and defend them. Tools: Bloomberg, FactSet, Excel, increasingly Python and alternative data. Fixed-income analysts do credit work (covenants, leverage, recovery) and macro analysts follow rates. Portfolio managers (PMs) decide position sizes, manage risk limits and client mandates, and answer to investment committees.
- **A typical day and week**: 7:00 read overnight news and broker notes; morning team meeting; results days mean updating models within hours; afternoons on company meetings or sector deep dives; Friday on a pitch or portfolio review. Quarterly reporting seasons are the busy phase. PMs spend more time on risk, client meetings (as the "investment specialist" in 3.2) and trade sizing.
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 45-55 hours typical; 55-60 in earnings season or market selloffs (practitioner estimate; the earlier project file also puts asset management at 50-55). Stress 3/5. Hybrid working (2-3 office days) is common at large firms (unverified). Travel: company visits and conferences, a few trips a quarter. Lifestyle is the best of the investing roles, but performance reviews against benchmarks are public and constant.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. You need clear writing and persuasion to convince a PM, and solid modelling, but few firms require advanced maths outside fixed income and quant-driven houses.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: Entry: London graduate analysts in investment banking and investment management £32-52k base, average about £41k (TargetJobs, May 2025); Italy: portfolio/fund managers ("gestori") from about €35k gross annual salary below five years' experience (PageGroup survey via We Wealth, December 2024); US: no reliable entry figure found. ~5 years: US financial and investment analysts at investment-pool and fund firms had a median wage of $146,640 in May 2023 (BLS via search summary; wages only, all experience levels, older than two years); Italy 5-10 years: no AM-specific figure. Senior: Italy portfolio managers from €70k and above (PageGroup 2024); US mutual fund PMs expected $251k base and $1.12 million variable on average in 2018 (Institutional Investor, stale). Incentives: Johnson projected 2025 asset-management incentives +7-12% (equity-focused +9-12%, fixed income +6-9%) (via AI-CIO, 10 November 2025). Hong Kong, Singapore, Tokyo, Gulf: no reliable data found.
- **Career path**: Analyst/associate analyst (0-3 years), analyst/senior analyst (3-7), assistant PM or sector head (6-10), PM (8-15 years, fewer than one in five analysts get there in practice, practitioner view, unverified). At bank-owned managers titles follow bank grades (Analyst, Associate, VP, Director).
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: another asset manager, hedge fund analyst (with a strong track record), equity research at a bank, MBA, corporate strategy/IR. After 7-10 years: PM or CIO elsewhere, hedge fund PM, family office or allocator CIO, investor relations at listed companies, start your own fund.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Tier 1 (research-driven, selective, strong training):* Capital Group, Fidelity, Wellington, T. Rowe Price, PIMCO (fixed income), BlackRock (active), J.P. Morgan Asset Management, Schroders, Amundi. *Tier 2 (large bank- or insurer-owned, solid brand, more bureaucratic):* DWS, UBS Asset Management, Allianz Global Investors, Generali Investments, Eurizon, Mediolanum. *Tier 3 (boutiques and regional/Italian independents):* Anima, Azimut, Algebris, local SGRs, regional UK/Nordic houses; smaller and more varied, can offer earlier responsibility.
- **How to enter**: Degrees: finance, economics, accounting, engineering; strong quantitative skills are rewarded. Graduate programmes: Capital Group's Capital Associates Program (CAP) targets final-year undergraduates or non-MBA master's students with up to one year of experience; the Asia round has a 1 November 2026 deadline with Super Day interviews October to December 2026, the US round opened mid-January and closed early February 2026 (per third-party guides; confirm on the firm site). Fidelity (Equity Research Associate internship, 10 weeks, researches three stocks and pitches to funds; Summer 2027 listings in Tokyo and Hong Kong), T. Rowe Price (Associate Analyst internship) and Wellington (applications expected September to late October per a third-party guide) hire mainly through summer internships, usually with a few places and heavy competition. In Europe, graduate schemes at Amundi, Schroders and Generali exist, and many Italian firms hire through 6-month internships. CFA: widely valued here; Level I pass rates were 39-45% across the four windows from November 2025 to August 2026, and all three levels cost US$3,520-4,570 plus about 300 study hours each (CFA Institute figures in the project career file, 2026; secondary guides quote slightly different fees). The charter needs 4,000 hours of relevant work over at least 36 months (secondary guides). Build: a stock-pitch portfolio, a written equity research sample, modelling skills, and investing club or student fund experience. Common mistakes: generic "I love markets" pitches without a view on a real stock; applying only in the final year. Alternative routes: start in equity research or credit at a bank or rating agency, in performance/risk or operations at an asset manager, or in a boutique, and move to investing after 2-3 years. Entry difficulty: 4/5.
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Fee pressure from passive limits headcount and pay growth in traditional active management; PM seats are scarce; investment style can go out of favour for years. Not for you if you want deal excitement or very high early pay, dislike public performance comparison, or are not curious about reading industry detail. Not a good fit if you want to avoid being measured against an index for a decade.

### 3.2 Asset management client and distribution roles (sales, client portfolio manager/investment specialist, product)

- **What you actually do**: Sales/relationship managers sell funds and mandates to pension funds, insurers, banks' distribution networks and wealth advisers; investment specialists or client portfolio managers (CPMs) explain the portfolio managers' views and performance to clients; product managers design funds, set fees and manage launches. Deliverables: pitch books, RFP (request for proposal) responses, due-diligence questionnaires, performance commentary, market updates, client reviews. Tools: CRM systems, Excel/PowerPoint, Bloomberg, increasingly data dashboards.
- **A typical day and week**: Morning market call, then client emails and calls; two or three meetings a day (in person or video); writing RFP answers; liaising with PMs. Travel to clients and conferences; month-end and quarter-end reporting cycles. Sales teams are measured on flows (net new money).
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 45-55 hours; 60 around pitches and travel (practitioner estimate). Stress 3/5, driven by sales targets and fund redemptions. Travel 20-40% for institutional sales (unverified). Hybrid common.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 2/5. You must understand portfolios well enough to explain performance, but the job is trust, storytelling and responsiveness.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: Hardly any public entry data. Averages across all seniorities (54% of respondents were managing directors) in a Heidrick survey of 186 alternatives marketing and IR professionals (spring 2025): US$416k base for private equity and private credit sales/IR, US$419k for product specialists, US$287k for individual hedge fund sales producers and US$272k for hedge fund sales/IR (Heidrick via Funds Society); this is not entry pay. Italy: relationship managers from about €35k, up to more than €90k with 10+ years (PageGroup 2024). UK/US entry: no reliable data found. Bonus and incentives follow sales: Johnson projected 2025 incentives +7-12% in traditional asset management (AI-CIO).
- **Career path**: Sales/product analyst or associate (0-3 years), VP/senior sales (4-7), director/head of a region or client segment (8-12), managing director/head of distribution (12+). Investment specialists often come from the investment side after a few years.
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: product or sales at another manager, fund selector roles at banks, wealth management, fintech sales. After 7-10 years: head of distribution, chief commercial officer, move into alternatives fundraising (investor relations at PE, credit and hedge funds), consulting to managers.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Tier 1 (global distribution machines with best training):* BlackRock, J.P. Morgan AM, Fidelity, Amundi, Schroders. *Tier 2:* DWS, UBS AM, Allianz GI, Eurizon, Generali Investments. *Tier 3:* boutiques, local SGRs, bank wealth distributors. Alternatives fundraising (IR) at big PE and private credit firms (Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Ares, EQT) pays more but hires mostly experienced people.
- **How to enter**: Business, finance, marketing, economics and languages all work; fluency in the local language matters (Italian for Italian institutional clients, German for DACH, Japanese for Tokyo). Graduate schemes at large managers (BlackRock, Amundi, DWS, UBS AM, Schroders) include distribution tracks; internships in sales support/marketing at managers are a common start. Certifications: CFA is helpful, not required; in the EU, local registration (e.g., EFPA/MiFID II knowledge requirements) matters for advice roles. Show: client-facing work (summer jobs in sales, wealth, consulting), strong writing, and some product knowledge. Common mistake: dismissing sales as "less prestigious" in the interview. Alternative routes: start in operations, client service or performance reporting and move into sales support. Entry difficulty: 3/5.
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Targets are partly out of your control (flows follow performance and markets); the job can feel like you are selling someone else's product; senior jobs are scarce. Not for you if you dislike travel, repetitive questionnaires or being measured on flows, or if you want to make investment decisions.

### 3.3 Hedge fund analyst / portfolio manager (with strategy notes, especially pod platforms)

- **What you actually do**: Analysts generate long and short ideas, build earnings models, track "what the market expects" versus what is happening, meet companies and experts, and size trades with the PM; in pods, analysts run a sector for a PM and can get their own capital after proving themselves. PMs set the risk budget, trade daily and are measured on profit and loss (P&L), often daily. Event-driven analysts read legal documents; macro people follow central banks and flows; credit analysts model recoveries.
- **A typical day and week**: Start around 6:00-7:00 with news and overnight positions; market open, constant trading and risk checks; earnings calls after close; evenings on models. Friday: weekly P&L review. A bad month tightens the PM and trims analysts' allocation. Strategy notes: fundamental long/short is the slower, research-heavy end; macro and RV are market-driven and episodic; merger arbitrage involves reading deal documents and monitoring regulators; distressed involves months-long legal processes and negotiation.
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 55-70 hours typical; 70-80+ in earnings season and market crises (practitioner estimate; no survey found). Stress 5/5 at pods (daily P&L, strict loss limits, quick firing), 4/5 at slower fundamental funds. Mostly in the office. Travel is modest, except for company or expert meetings. The risk is job insecurity rather than raw hours.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You need conviction and the ability to argue with a PM, but numbers, probabilities and risk dominate.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: Survey (almost 3,000 respondents across financial services, 2025 pay, eFinancialCareers Compensation & Lifestyle Report 2026, reported 25 August 2026): hedge fund junior (analyst and associate) average total US$227k; seniors (VP/director) more than three times that; managing directors US$2.2 million; base salaries rarely above US$300k; no breakdown by city. Entry programmes: Millennium's Equities Analyst Program (run with UBS: a first year in UBS equity research, then a Millennium PM team) listed a New York base of US$125,000 in May 2025 (job listing via MIT CDO; I could not open the live page); Point72 Academy summer internship base of US$120-140k prorated (Point72 posting) and a New York Academy listing at about US$125,000 (Indeed, unverified). Mid-career: pod-level estimates of US$300-700k for a New York analyst and US$500k-1.5 million+ for a senior analyst (Quantt Millennium guide, 2026; estimates from public and anecdotal sources); a former Balyasny analyst says senior analysts on a good pod make US$1-3 million in a normal year (X post as relayed by eFinancialCareers; anecdote). PMs commonly receive 12-25% of their P&L; top PMs earn tens of millions; one report says offers above US$100 million have been made to star PMs (Hedgeweek/WSJ, relayed by search; unverified details). Johnson projected hedge fund bonuses +2.5% to 10%+ for 2025 (AI-CIO). Europe, Asia, Gulf: no reliable breakdown found; the pay surveys exist but are paywalled.
- **Career path**: Analyst (0-3 years), senior analyst (3-6), PM or junior PM with own capital (5-10, if at all), senior PM. At single-manager funds the path is analyst, senior analyst, sector head, partner. Most pods work on a "up or out" basis.
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: move between funds, go to long-only asset managers (often for lifestyle), PE/growth equity, a corporate role, or business school. After 7-10 years: start your own fund (rarely with outside money now), family-office CIO, allocator, operating roles, or a long-only PM role.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Tier 1:* multi-manager platforms (Millennium, Citadel, Balyasny, Point72, ExodusPoint, Schonfeld) and elite single managers (Bridgewater, D. E. Shaw, Elliott, Viking, Marshall Wace, Brevan Howard in Europe). *Tier 2:* well-known mid-sized fundamental or macro funds, such as Lansdowne, Egerton, Capula, Pharo, Hillhouse in Asia. *Tier 3:* small, specialist and emerging managers (often under US$1 billion), where resources are thinner and survival risk is higher. Italian hedge fund jobs are few: Milan has some boutiques, but London and Switzerland are the main European hubs (practitioner view; unverified).
- **How to enter**: Graduate programmes are rare but growing at platforms: Point72 Academy is a paid ten-month programme (eight months of training, two of rotations with teams) for students graduating between December 2026 and July 2027, with more than 230 graduates placed as of March 2026 (Point72 posting, via search); Millennium's analyst programme and 2027 summer internships (applications opened 3 August 2026, closed 3 September 2026) asked for a GPA of 3.5+ (listing); Citadel and Citadel Securities took about 350 interns out of more than 115,900 applicants, an acceptance rate of about 0.36% (Fortune via AlphaMaven, undated); Balyasny received over 40,000 applications for about 100 intern places (Pensions & Investments, undated). Most hires still come from sell-side research, investment banking, consulting or long-only funds after two to four years. Degrees: finance, economics, maths, engineering, accounting; STEM is valued at systematic or data-heavy pods. Certifications: CFA is optional; track record matters more. Build: a documented stock-picking record, a published pitch, or a student fund. Common mistakes: pitching only large caps with consensus views, no short idea, ignoring risk. Alternative routes: sell-side research, a credit or restructuring job, or an internal move from a bank's markets desk. Entry difficulty: 5/5.
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Pay is extreme in the best cases and zero in the worst: pods cut people quickly after drawdowns; bonuses depend on a PM you do not control; there are few seats for graduates. March 2026 shows the model can lose money for the largest names. Not for you if you need stability, dislike public daily scoreboards or are not comfortable being wrong in front of colleagues several times a week.

### 3.4 Private equity associate to partner

- **What you actually do**: Associates build LBO (leveraged buyout) models, review confidential information memoranda, run due diligence calls with consultants, accountants and lawyers, manage data rooms, write investment committee memos (often 30-40 pages), and work with banks on financing. After the deal, you monitor portfolio companies (monthly KPI packs, board materials). VPs and principals source deals and lead execution; partners decide on the investment and raise funds.
- **A typical day and week**: Morning: model updates and calls with management or advisers; afternoon: diligence sessions or lender discussions; evenings and weekends: IC memo and model. Weeks differ: a "live deal" can mean 80-100 hours near signing, while portfolio monitoring and sourcing weeks are calmer.
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 55-70 hours typical, 80-100 during live deals (a recruiter's guide says 60-80 regularly with longer stretches at closing, Wall Street Careers, 2026; lower mid-market is often 50-60, practitioner estimate). Stress 4/5. Travel to portfolio companies and targets. Hybrid is partly possible, but most junior work is in the office.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Modelling must be solid, but the job at VP level is judgment, negotiating with sellers, managing management teams and winning deals.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: US: Associates (typically hired after 2-3 years in banking) earn base US$140-175k and first-year total US$250-400k+ (Leland, July 2025); Glassdoor averages US$246,462 total with a 25th-75th percentile range of US$186,657-332,138 (February 2026, via Wall Street Careers); one third-party estimate puts mega-funds at US$325-425k and middle-market at US$250-340k (Refonte Learning, 2026, an estimate). Carry is rare at associate level and begins around senior associate/VP (Wall Street Prep, via Wall Street Careers; recruiter material). Heidrick's 2025 North America survey (656 respondents, 19 November 2025) found half of respondents had base increases of 10% or less and three-quarters said bonuses are discretionary; the public page gives no dollar tables. UK: associates total £170-300k in London (Jobrise, 2026; low-reliability estimate; an older Emolument survey from March 2020 gave £100k+ base, bonus up to £36k, and carry up to £300k, stale). Italy: the PageGroup survey puts "M&A analyst/associate/manager (investment banking, private equity cluster)" at €27-60k gross below five years, up to about €100k at 5-10 years and above €100k beyond, with bonus 50-100% (via We Wealth, December 2024); this mixes banking and PE, so treat as a floor. Continental Europe: Heidrick's Europe survey (302 participants, 11 December 2025) shows managing partners with an average base of €339k and bonus of €459k (finews); other levels not public. Hong Kong/Singapore: managing partners average US$891k, up 14% (Heidrick 2025 APAC/Middle East survey via Gulf News, 6 April 2026), with average carry allocations of US$5.487 million; Middle East managing partners average US$750k total cash (up from US$624k); associate-level 2025 data: no reliable data found (older Heidrick data is stale). Tokyo: one recruiter listing for an associate to senior associate at a global PE fund quoted ¥14-17 million (Morgan McKinley, undated listing).
- **Career path**: Analyst (pre-MBA, at some funds 0-2 years), associate (2-3 years), senior associate or VP (3-6 years), principal/director (6-10), partner/managing director (10-15+). Promotion timelines have widened as holding periods lengthened to about seven years (Bain, 2026).
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: MBA, move to a bigger or different fund, growth equity, venture, corporate development, portfolio company operating roles. After 7-10 years: partner at the same or another fund, start a fund or independent sponsor, CFO or CEO of a portfolio company, family office.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Mega-fund (largest funds, global, biggest deals, most structured):* Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle, TPG, CVC, EQT, Bain Capital, Advent, Warburg Pincus, Permira, Hellman & Friedman, Thoma Bravo, Silver Lake. *Upper-middle-market / large mid-market:* Leonard Green, Veritas, Francisco Partners, Apax, Ardian, PAI Partners, Bridgepoint, Cinven. *Middle-market:* regional and sector funds such as Riverside, HIG, Audax; in Italy Investindustrial, Clessidra, Wise Equity, Xenon, Ambienta, and the state-backed Fondo Italiano d'Investimento. *Lower middle-market / independent sponsors:* small funds with fewer resources and more operational involvement, where hiring is less structured.
- **How to enter**: Almost all associate hires at top funds come from top-tier banks' M&A or leveraged-finance analyst classes, strategy consulting (especially in Europe), or occasionally other funds. US "on-cycle" recruiting means that large funds interview junior bankers very early (historically within months of starting as an analyst) for jobs 18-24 months ahead, through headhunters such as Henkel, Amity, CPI and Oxbridge, in processes that can go from call to offer in days. This was disrupted in 2025: JPMorgan warned that analysts accepting future-dated PE jobs in their first 18 months could be fired; on 11-12 June 2025 Apollo and General Atlantic announced they would not formally interview for the class of 2027 (Banking Dive, 12 June 2025); recruiting resumed on 5 January 2026 with Blackstone, Apollo, KKR and Thoma Bravo among a dozen-plus firms interviewing (reporting via PE Insights/Business Insider summaries); Apollo says it fills under half of its associate class on-cycle. Several firms now allocate about half their classes to off-cycle hiring (Prospect Rock Partners). The 2028-class calendar has not been reported; an undated forum post says it had not started (anecdote). In Europe and Asia, I found no source documenting a fixed on-cycle calendar; listings from European mid-market funds look rolling and year-round and ask for 1-3 years in investment banking, strategy consulting or M&A boutiques (job listings via CFA Institute career centre, Munich/Frankfurt/Zurich). That suggests continental mid-market PE is off-cycle, but this is my inference, not documented. Certifications: CFA is not required; some value a Master's. Skills: LBO modelling tests, case studies, a view on industries. Common mistakes: applying without any transaction experience, weak understanding of leverage, thinking MBA alone is a route (the earlier project file notes that post-MBA PE seats go almost exclusively to people with prior PE or elite M&A experience). Alternative routes: transaction services (Big 4), strategy consulting (Bain, BCG, McKinsey), credit funds, restructuring, or joining a smaller or independent sponsor and moving up. Entry difficulty: 5/5.
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Pay is very high but bonuses are discretionary; realised carry depends on exits that are now slow (distributions under 15% of NAV for four years, Bain 2026). Junior work is repetitive modelling and diligence. Partnership seats are few: many associates exit. Not for you if you want predictable hours, hate negotiating, or want to run operations rather than make bets.

### 3.5 Venture capital analyst/associate to partner

- **What you actually do**: Source start-ups (cold outreach, events, founder referrals, data tools), screen decks, take first calls, write investment memos, run reference calls with customers and former colleagues, track portfolio KPIs and help with follow-on rounds. At seed stage there is little modelling; at growth stage you build revenue-cohort models. Partners decide on investments and sit on boards.
- **A typical day and week**: Founder meetings in the morning, internal pipeline meeting, evenings at events or calls with founders in other time zones. Mondays: partner meeting; the rest is sourcing and diligence. Rhythm is irregular but less intense than PE.
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 45-60 hours; peaks around a deal. Stress 3/5; the pressure is long-term (fund performance, fundraising) not daily. Flexible and often hybrid; travel to hubs (San Francisco, London, Berlin, Milan). Many firms are small, so culture varies widely.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 2/5. Relationships, taste and speed to a decision count more than modelling; technical depth in your sector is a big advantage.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: Data are weak. A 2025 survey run by Gannon/Venture5 (700+ respondents, as relayed by a search summary, unverified) reports average base of about US$78k (analyst), US$126k (associate), US$154k (senior associate), US$206k (VP/principal) and US$317k (partner), with total cash of roughly US$90-170k (analyst), US$130-300k (associate), US$250-700k (principal) and US$400k-1.8 million (partner). Another table (VCBeast, 2026) lists carry as rare for associates, meaningful for principals and the main reward for partners. Because many VC funds have no preferred-return hurdle, carry is paid on all profit (VCBeast). Johnson projected flat VC bonuses for 2025 (AI-CIO). A reported "median total cash of about US$125k for a VC analyst in 2025" attributed to Carta could not be verified. UK, Italy, Singapore, Hong Kong, Tokyo, Gulf: no reliable data found. Carry in venture pays out only after 7-10 years and depends on rare outliers (power law, 2.5).
- **Career path**: Analyst (0-2 years), associate (2-4), senior associate/principal (4-7), partner (7-12; often by bringing in a winning investment or raising a fund). Many people also start their own fund ("scout" or angel route) or become operators.
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: start-up operating roles (strategy, business development, product), growth equity, PE, a founder role. After 7-10 years: partner or GP at another fund, founder, CVC head, family office.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Tier 1 (brand and deal access; extremely selective, very few hires per year):* Sequoia, Andreessen Horowitz, Accel, Benchmark, Founders Fund, Lightspeed, General Catalyst; in Europe Index Ventures, Atomico, Balderton, Lakestar; in Asia Peak XV and HongShan. *Tier 2:* strong specialist or regional funds (e.g., in Italy P101, United Ventures, Primo Ventures, and the state-backed CDP Venture Capital). *Tier 3:* emerging managers, angel syndicates, accelerators and university funds. CVCs (Intel Capital, Salesforce Ventures, bank and utility ventures) form a separate group where jobs are more stable but investment mandates are strategic.
- **How to enter**: There is no standard path. Typical hires come from start-ups (operators), investment banking or consulting (especially tech M&A and strategy), PE and growth equity, or from founding a company. Some funds run analyst programmes (e.g., through Kauffman Fellows or fellowships, often with part-time roles for students). Recruiting is year-round and unstructured: you pitch yourself via cold emails, a portfolio of writing (a market map, a newsletter, a deep dive on a sector), and a record of building (side projects, hackathons, student venture funds). Certifications: none matter; the CFA is rarely relevant. Skills: sourcing, sector knowledge (AI, climate, fintech, defence), clear memos. Common mistakes: applying with no sourcing evidence; pitching "I like start-ups"; ignoring that most funds hire one or two people a year. Alternative routes: join a start-up and become a "platform" or scout; join a student venture fund; work in a university tech-transfer office. Entry difficulty: 5/5.
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Very few seats; low base for junior roles compared with PE or hedge funds; the reward is carry, which takes a decade and may be zero; many funds under-deliver (power law). Not for you if you need quick, measurable feedback, financial security early on, or dislike constant networking.

### 3.6 Private credit analyst/associate

- **What you actually do**: Analyse borrowers (usually PE-owned mid-sized companies): model cash flows and leverage, study covenants and legal documents, speak with sponsors and management, write credit memos for the investment committee, negotiate terms with other lenders and lawyers, and monitor the portfolio after funding (quarterly compliance and amendment requests). Special-situations teams do restructuring, rescue financing and distressed purchases; asset-based finance teams analyse pools of receivables or consumer loans and value collateral.
- **A typical day and week**: Review new deals from sponsors, build a model, speak to lawyers about the term sheet, and prepare an IC memo. During a closing, 14-hour days; in monitoring weeks, updating covenants, calling borrowers, and approving amendments. In stressed periods (2026 software loans) much time goes on "watch-list" names.
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 50-65 hours; 70-80 around closings (practitioner estimate). Stress 4/5, higher in 2026 with valuation and redemption pressure. Mostly in-office; some travel to sponsors.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Downside analysis, documentation and sponsor relationships matter more than complex maths.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: US: a posted direct lending associate role lists base US$125-150k excluding bonus (Marathon Fund, listing); a recruiter guide gives entry-level base US$90-130k with 30-80% bonus (Wall Street Careers, 2026); a Wall Street Oasis benchmark summary (forum, unverified) puts Tier-1-city associates at about US$300k total (US$150k base plus US$150k bonus) and VPs at about US$505k. Heidrick's first North American private credit survey (297 respondents, 10 June 2025) says carry commonly vests over five years, clawbacks are rare, and deferral is common above the most junior levels (20-30% of bonus). One recruiter blog says a direct-lending principal averages about US$2.9 million of carry over a five-year fund cycle (attributed to Heidrick; not verified). UK/Europe: Dartmouth Partners' 2025 guide has London figures for analyst to director (paywalled) and says Paris and Frankfurt pay is "typically 10-20% lower"; actual numbers: no reliable data found. Italy: no reliable data found (Milan private credit teams are small; Tikehau Capital, for example, advertises a private equity internship in Italy without pay). Johnson projected 2025 private credit bonuses +5-10% (AI-CIO), and a secondary report says -2.5% to -7.5% for 2026 (unverified). Asia/Gulf: no reliable data found.
- **Career path**: Analyst (0-2), associate (2-4), VP (4-7), principal/director (7-10), partner/MD (10+). Carry usually starts around VP/principal.
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: another credit fund, PE, restructuring advisory, bank leveraged finance, hedge fund credit. After 7-10 years: partner, head of a strategy, CIO of an insurer's credit book, CFO/treasury roles, start own lending platform.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Tier 1 (large platforms with proprietary origination and insurance capital):* Apollo, Ares, Blackstone Credit & Insurance, HPS (now part of BlackRock), Blue Owl, Oaktree, Sixth Street, Golub, Goldman Sachs Asset Management; in Europe ICG, Hayfin, Arcmont, Tikehau, Pemberton. *Tier 2:* mid-sized specialist lenders and the credit arms of PE and asset managers (Barings, Churchill, Muzinich, Marathon). *Tier 3:* small, regional and BDC-style lenders, where deal flow is less proprietary.
- **How to enter**: Hired mainly from leveraged finance, DCM (debt capital markets), restructuring and credit research at banks, and from Big 4 transaction services; some firms hire analysts directly from undergraduate programmes (e.g., Ares and large platforms' analyst classes, check each firm's site). In Europe, internships at Tikehau or Muzinich in Milan, Paris and London are a way in. Certifications: CFA is respected for credit; the CAIA is a secondary signal for alternatives. Skills: credit analysis, loan documents, covenant logic, financial modelling. Common mistakes: treating credit like equity (upside) rather than downside protection; ignoring legal documents. Alternative routes: bank credit risk, rating agencies, restructuring and insolvency practice. Entry difficulty: 4/5.
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: The asset class is in its first major test since scale; if defaults rise, the junior staff of weaker platforms are the first to be cut (headhunter: jobs down up to 25% in 2024). Upside is capped by the loan's interest rate, so pay depends on fees and scale. Not for you if you dislike legal documents, prefer fast-moving equity stories, or dislike the monitoring of problem loans.

### 3.7 Investment professional at an institutional allocator (pension, insurer, sovereign wealth fund, endowment, fund of funds)

- **What you actually do**: Allocators manage large asset pools. Roles include asset allocation and portfolio construction, manager selection and monitoring (due diligence meetings with funds, performance and risk analysis, ESG and governance checks), co-investments and direct deals (at SWFs and large Canadian pensions), liability matching (pensions and insurers) and reporting to boards. Consultants and fund-of-funds analysts do the same for many clients. Deliverables: manager due-diligence reports, investment committee papers, allocation models, reports for trustees.
- **A typical day and week**: Calls and meetings with fund managers, internal committee preparation, risk and performance reviews, writing papers. Cycles follow board and committee calendars. Direct teams at SWFs behave like PE teams on live deals.
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 40-50 hours; up to 55 during committee cycles or deals (practitioner estimate). Stress 2/5 (3 on direct investing teams). Hybrid is common (Cambridge Associates requires 12-16 office days a month for analysts, per listings). Travel: manager visits and conferences; larger SWFs have international offices.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. You evaluate other people's decisions: you need judgment on people and process, solid statistics and writing.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: Public-sector pay is lower than hedge funds and PE and published only in some places. CalPERS Investment Officer I: US$4,238-8,049 per month (about US$51-97k a year), Investment Officer II US$7,760-9,713 per month (about US$93-117k), a 5% differential for the CFA, incentives of 0-10% of salary, and a 3% pay reduction through the Personal Leave Program from July 2025 (CalPERS postings; annualised by me). CalPERS commissioned a March 2025 McLagan benchmark review (percentile tables not retrieved). Cambridge Associates (consultant) Investment Analyst Program: base US$80,000 (Boston, Dallas, Arlington) or US$88,000 (San Francisco), four-year programme, any major with GPA 3.3+, US work authorisation without sponsorship (listings via TheLadders, Class of January 2027). Canadian pensions publish senior pay in annual reports (not retrieved). GIC, Temasek, NBIM, ADIA, Mubadala, PIF: no reliable data found. UK and Italy: no reliable data found. Johnson Associates reporting does not cover allocators in the public material I found.
- **Career path**: Analyst/associate (0-3 years), senior associate or investment officer (3-6), principal/portfolio manager/director (6-12), head of an asset class or CIO. Consultants: analyst (2-4 years), senior analyst, investment director/consultant.
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: PE/VC fund investor-relations or fund-of-funds roles, asset-manager distribution, private markets managers (as the "other side of the table"), MBA. After 7-10 years: CIO roles at endowments, family offices, insurers, or senior roles at managers; moving to a PE firm's fundraising team.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Tier 1 (prestige, scale, direct investing):* NBIM, GIC, Temasek, ADIA, Mubadala, CPP Investments, Ontario Teachers', Yale and other large endowments. *Tier 2:* large public pensions and insurers (CalPERS, APG, PGGM, USS, GPIF, Allianz, AXA, Generali), and top consultants (Cambridge Associates, Mercer, WTW). *Tier 3:* smaller pension funds, regional foundations (Italy's Cassa Depositi e Prestiti, bank foundations, professional pension funds such as Cassa Forense or Enpam), and regional consultants, which offer stability and a narrower investment scope.
- **How to enter**: SWFs run graduate programmes: NBIM's graduate programme lasts 12 months (nine on projects with the AI team, then three in a business area) for final-year students or graduates with up to two years' experience, in Oslo, London or New York; applications for the 2027 programme ran 1-15 August 2026 (NBIM). Temasek's Associate Programme in Singapore runs 20 months from 3 August 2026 to 31 March 2028; applications opened 2 March 2026 and closed 27 March 2026, with a video interview and a half-day case-study assessment (Temasek listing); summer 2027 internship information was due in July 2026. GIC's Professionals Programme takes final-year students from any discipline with less than a year of work experience on a rolling basis, and its 12-week internship targets penultimate-year students (GIC, via a mirror page; verify on the official site). PIF's Graduate Development Program is for Saudi nationals only (PIF). Japan's GPIF is expanding a small team (187 staff, Bloomberg, 11 August 2025). Gulf SWFs often prioritise nationals for graduate schemes (I found no eligibility page for ADIA or Mubadala graduate programmes; treat as unverified). For consultants, Cambridge Associates and Mercer hire graduates directly. CFA and CAIA are valued here; CFA is explicitly rewarded at CalPERS. Skills: statistics, portfolio theory, private markets knowledge, writing. Common mistakes: expecting the same pay as hedge funds; applying without a view on asset allocation. Alternative routes: asset-manager client roles, performance/risk teams, auditing of funds. Entry difficulty: 4/5 (very high at SWFs, moderate at consultants).
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Slower progression, public-sector pay, committee decision-making and political constraints; at SWFs, nationality and visa rules can restrict access; you rarely own the investment decision. Not for you if you want to be a stockpicker with a big bonus, or want to move fast.

### 3.8 Private banker / wealth manager

- **What you actually do**: A private banker (relationship manager) is the single point of contact for wealthy clients: understand their goals, propose portfolios, advisory or discretionary mandates, lending (Lombard loans, mortgages), and coordinate specialists (tax, estate planning, family governance). Juniors work as assistants/analysts: prepare reviews, execute trades, KYC (know-your-customer) and AML (anti-money laundering) paperwork, and learn the client book. Real income depends on bringing in new money.
- **A typical day and week**: Market review, client calls, portfolio reviews, documentation, client meetings or dinners; time on compliance. Business development (prospecting new clients) grows with seniority. Month-end reports.
- **Hours, stress and lifestyle**: 45-55 hours plus client events (practitioner estimate). Stress 3/5, with spikes in market falls and under asset-gathering targets and compliance scrutiny. Travel depends on client geography (Swiss bankers cover clients abroad). Hybrid possible, but client contact is in-person.
- **Human vs. quantitative profile**: People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 2/5. Trust, discretion and sales skills dominate; you need enough finance to explain portfolios.
- **Compensation (approximate)**: Italy (best data): Magstat (updated January 2026, via We Wealth): junior private bankers (under five years, no fully consolidated book) earn €50-68k gross a year in total, with variable pay up to 50% of fixed; senior bankers (10+ years) have fixed pay of €55-100k and total pay that can roughly double; top performers with ultra-high-net-worth portfolios exceed €500k. Another Magstat-based report puts junior fixed pay at €45-60k (Citywire). PageGroup (via We Wealth, December 2024): private banker €35-55k (under five years), €55-110k (5-10 years), above €110k (10+ years). Glassdoor (January 2026) averages about €68k in Italy and warns it underestimates variable pay. UK: entry private bankers about £50.4k (range £41,000-60,800, Salary.com) and Glassdoor average of about £73,900 (City Wealth, 2025 update; aggregator data). US: wirehouse trainees (Merrill, Morgan Stanley) are paid a salary for training, then a mix of salary and commissions; Payscale estimates about US$47k (modelled, 2026), older reporting says Merrill trainee pay was raised by US$10k in 2019 to about US$65k; the failure rate of rookie advisors was reported above 72% (Cerulli, older), though Merrill says about three-quarters of its roughly 2,400 trainees are on track (American Banker, undated). Switzerland: the only figure I found is a recruiter advertisement of CHF 200-300k for an experienced private banker with a book in Geneva or Zurich (Selby Jennings listing); entry pay: no reliable data found. Hong Kong, Singapore, Tokyo, Gulf: no reliable data found. Johnson projected wealth-management bonuses +8-10% for 2025 (AI-CIO).
- **Career path**: Assistant/analyst (0-3 years; 12-24 months to exams and licences in Italy at Fideuram: 12 months assistant, 12 months specialist, 24 months partner stage), junior RM (3-6), RM/senior RM (6-12), team head or area manager (12+). In Italy, networks (reti) of financial advisers (consulenti) may work as self-employed agents; area managers can earn €160-400k (Citywire/Magstat).
- **Exit opportunities**: After 2-3 years: another private bank, family office, asset management client roles, wealth tech. After 7-10 years: head of a desk or region, independent wealth manager (often taking clients), family office head, move to fund distribution.
- **Tier list of employers**: Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact. *Tier 1 (global brands with large platforms):* UBS Global Wealth Management, Julius Baer, Pictet, Lombard Odier, J.P. Morgan Private Bank, Goldman Sachs Private Wealth, Morgan Stanley Wealth Management; in Italy Fideuram-Intesa Sanpaolo Private Banking, Mediobanca Private Banking. *Tier 2:* UniCredit Private Banking, Banca Generali, Azimut, Fineco, EFG, DBS and HSBC Private Banks in Asia, wirehouses outside the top. *Tier 3:* regional banks, independent wealth firms and multi-family offices (smaller platforms, entrepreneurial but fewer resources). In Italy, change at Mediobanca after its absorption into Monte dei Paschi: wealth management lost €1.1 billion in the final quarter of 2025 after banker exits, with 28 net departures from the distribution network in the half (Teleborsa, 9 February 2026; via project file).
- **How to enter**: Business, economics, finance or law; languages and cultural fluency are major assets. Programmes: UBS and Julius Baer run graduate and trainee schemes; Fideuram's "Private Wealth Assistant" programme pairs young graduates with senior consultants and prepares them for professional registration exams (bluerating/We Wealth reporting); US wirehouses run advisor training programmes of 18 months to several years (Merrill reduced its to 18 months in 2021). Italian roles typically need the albo (financial adviser register) exam and IVASS (insurance) registration for advisers. Certifications: CFA helpful; in Switzerland, local private-banking and advisory certifications that vary by employer (not researched), and in the UK the CISI qualifications; US advisers need Series 7 and 66. Build: client-facing experience, language skills, a network, and a credible personal story. Common mistakes: expecting a salary regardless of new money; ignoring licensing requirements. Alternative routes: bank branch, financial planning, insurance, family business networks. Entry difficulty: 3/5 (higher at Swiss top-tier banks, where banks prefer experienced bankers with a client book).
- **Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for**: Income depends on winning and keeping clients; very high attrition in US wirehouse training; compliance and AML burdens; layoffs and consolidation after mergers (UBS-Credit Suisse). Not for you if you dislike sales or networking, or want purely analytical work.

## 4. Banks vs. other employer types

The same investing roles exist at very different employers. The table gives my synthesis from the sources above plus practitioner consensus; nothing in it is a survey result unless stated.

| Employer type | Hours | Stress | Pay | Culture | Autonomy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bank-owned asset manager (UBS AM, DWS, Eurizon, Amundi) | 45-50 | 2-3 | Lowest of the group; salary-heavy; grades follow the bank | Hierarchical, stable, process-driven | Low-medium; committee decisions |
| Independent asset manager (Capital Group, Fidelity, Wellington, Schroders) | 45-55 | 3 | Moderate-high; strong bonuses in good markets (Johnson: +7-12% for 2025) | Research culture, long-term, less politics | Medium-high for PMs; analysts have real influence |
| Hedge fund (single-manager) | 55-70 | 4 | High; wide dispersion (average junior total US$227k; MD US$2.2m, eFC 2026) | Meritocratic, intense, small teams | High but accountable to P&L |
| Hedge fund (multi-manager pod) | 60-75 | 5 | Highest upside; analysts US$300k-3m depending on pod (estimates and anecdote); fired fast | Competitive, siloed, strict risk | High within risk limits; none outside |
| PE / private credit firm | 55-70 (80-100 in deals) | 4 | High base plus discretionary bonus; carry after years; flat bonuses in 2025 (Johnson) | Deal teams, apprenticeship, partnership track | Increases steeply at VP/principal |
| Sovereign wealth fund / pension fund | 40-50 | 2-3 | Lower cash, strong benefits; US public pensions much lower (CalPERS IO I US$51-97k) | Institutional, long horizon, committee-driven; politics at the board level | Low junior, higher on direct teams |
| Family office | 40-55 | 2-4 | Wide; US single-family office medians reported at about US$125k (analyst) to US$242k (associate) and US$480k (PM), from a Morgan Stanley/Botoff survey as relayed by a secondary source (unverified) | Personal; depends on the principal | High, but dependent on one family's preferences |
| Sell side for comparison (IB/S&T, parts 1-2) | 70-100+ (IB), 50-60 (S&T) | 5 | High and more formulaic; analyst bands published | Hierarchical, apprenticeship | Low at junior levels |

**Commentary.** (1) The biggest trade-off is autonomy versus security: pods and PE give the most freedom and upside, but pods can eliminate your seat after a bad quarter; allocators and bank-owned managers offer security and lower pay. (2) Buy-side pay is more variable than sell-side pay: Johnson's 2025 projections ranged from flat (PE midsize/small, VC) to +12% (asset management equities). (3) Hours: PE and IB share deal-driven peaks, but PE's average week is lower than junior IB; asset management and allocators are the most predictable.

**Regional differences.**
- **New York**: largest pods and mega-funds; US on-cycle PE recruiting; highest pay (hedge fund and PE pay figures in this report are mostly US). US wirehouses dominate private wealth.
- **London**: Europe's hub for hedge funds (Marshall Wace, Brevan Howard, Egerton), private credit (ICG, Hayfin, Arcmont) and growth/PE (CVC, Permira, Cinven). Recruitment looks more rolling than the US on-cycle model and often hires from consulting as well as banks (inference from listings). Pay for credit in Paris/Frankfurt is typically 10-20% below London (Dartmouth Partners 2025).
- **Continental Europe / Milan**: Italian buy side is smaller and more domestic: 73% of PE and VC investment value came from foreign operators in 2025; Lombardy accounts for 47% of deals (AIFI-PwC). Large asset managers (Eurizon, Generali Investments, Amundi SGR, Anima, Azimut, Mediolanum) and private banking (Fideuram, Mediobanca, UniCredit) are the main graduate employers. Italian pay is much lower than London or New York (PageGroup).
- **Switzerland**: private banking centre (UBS, Julius Baer, Pictet, Lombard Odier); banks prefer RMs with a client book or language/market expertise; non-EU/EFTA work permits are quota-bound (project file).
- **Hong Kong and Singapore**: regional hubs for PE, hedge funds (Hillhouse, Millennium Hong Kong) and SWFs (GIC, Temasek). Heidrick's 2025 survey shows rising pay (78% expect base increases over 18 months). Hong Kong and Singapore recruiting follows no documented on-cycle model in my sources.
- **Tokyo**: bilingual talent is scarce in private capital (Heidrick); asset managers are expanding (Tokio Marine AM plans about 100 extra staff; Nomura AM is moving staff to London and New York); GPIF hiring.
- **Gulf (Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Riyadh)**: SWFs and PE/credit funds are growing; the Middle East managing partners' average total cash rose to US$750k in 2025 (Heidrick via Gulf News). National programmes (PIF, ADIA scholarship) often prioritise nationals; expatriates mostly enter via experienced hire.
