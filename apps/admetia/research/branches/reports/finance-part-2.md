# Finance, part 2: Sales & trading, markets and research

## 1. What this branch is

This part of the finance branch covers the people who buy and sell financial products all day: the "markets" side of an investment bank (sales, trading, structuring and sell-side research), the physical commodity trading houses (Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore and similar), and the discretionary traders at hedge funds who are often recruited from bank desks. Unlike investment banking advisory (covered in part 1), these jobs are paid mainly for managing risk and serving investors in live markets, not for advising companies on deals. The work is fast, numerate and tied to the market clock, and the pay swings with trading revenues and volatility. Banks' markets divisions had a very strong 2025: the 12-bank Coalition Greenwich index earned US$174.7bn in markets revenue in 2025, up 14.5% (equities US$71.5bn, up 20.7%; fixed income, currencies and commodities, "FICC", US$103.1bn, up 10.5%) (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026). But the same year, headcount did not grow with revenue (see section 2.7).

**Scope notes:** (1) "Today" is 9 October 2026 and figures are dated; where a figure is from before October 2024 it is flagged. (2) Quant traders at proprietary trading firms, quant researchers and strats are covered in the quant part; I only compare them briefly in section 4. (3) Buy-side investing roles (portfolio managers at asset managers, PE, VC, private credit) are in the buy-side part; here I cover only discretionary trader/PMs at macro and multi-manager hedge funds and execution traders at asset managers. (4) Employer career pages for Goldman, Morgan Stanley, J.P. ## 2. Map of areas and sectors

### 2.1 What "Global Markets" is, and sell side vs buy side

A bank's markets division (called Global Markets, Markets, Global Banking & Markets or "Sales & Trading", depending on the bank) is the part of an investment bank that buys and sells securities and derivatives for clients and manages the resulting risk. Its clients are the "buy side": asset managers, pension funds, insurers, hedge funds, sovereign wealth funds, central banks, corporate treasurers and wealthy families. The bank is the "sell side" because it sells products and services to those investors. Sell-side employees work for the intermediary; buy-side employees work for the people whose money is invested.

How a markets desk earns money (in plain terms):
- **Bid-ask spread and market making.** The desk quotes two prices, one at which it will buy ("bid") and a slightly higher one at which it will sell ("offer"). If it buys from one client and sells to another, it keeps the difference. Per trade the margin is tiny; the business works on volume and good risk management.
- **Commissions.** In cash equities (ordinary shares) the bank often charges a small fee per share or a percentage of the trade rather than a spread.
- **Financing.** The bank lends cash or securities to hedge funds and earns an interest margin (repo, securities lending, prime brokerage, see 2.6).
- **Structuring margin.** For a tailor-made derivative or note, the bank builds a profit margin into the price, plus the later hedging and funding carry.
- **Carry and positioning.** The desk holds inventory (bonds, shares, derivatives) to serve clients and earns the yield on it, or loses when prices move.

**Market making vs proprietary risk-taking, and post-2008 rules.** A market maker holds positions in order to be ready to trade with clients. Proprietary ("prop") trading means taking positions purely to profit from price moves, with the bank's own capital and no client reason. After the 2008 crisis, the US Volcker rule (part of the Dodd-Frank Act) generally prohibits US banking entities from proprietary trading and from investing in or sponsoring hedge funds and private equity funds, while permitting market making, hedging and certain other activities (US Federal Reserve, Volcker Rule page, last updated 30 January 2020; agencies finalised simplifications in October 2019). The practical consequence is that most "prop desks" left banks around 2010-2014: their traders moved to hedge funds or to proprietary trading firms. Alongside it, tougher capital rules (Basel III and its trading-book revisions) made it costlier for banks to hold bond inventory, which is one reason electronic market makers outside banks became large (see 2.7). In the UK and EU the equivalent constraints are capital rules, ring-fencing in UK retail banks, and MiFID II conduct rules rather than a Volcker-style ban (general industry knowledge). Today's bank trader is therefore normally a flow trader: you price for clients and manage the resulting risk, not a bank-funded macro speculator.

**Regulatory note on pay (UK).** The UK removed the EU-derived bonus cap (variable pay limited to 100% of fixed pay, or 200% with approval) with effect from 31 October 2023 (FCA PS23/15). The cap still applies in the EU (relevant for Milan, Paris, Frankfurt). In practice, EU banks can pay higher base salaries to compensate, so Continental base salaries are often relatively higher and bonuses relatively lower than in New York or London (practitioner consensus).

### 2.2 Rates

**Definition.** Rates desks trade government bonds (US Treasuries, German Bunds, Italian BTPs, UK gilts, Japanese JGBs), interest rate swaps (agreements to exchange fixed for floating interest payments), futures and rates options. Sub-areas: government bond trading, swaps (vanilla), "exotics"/rates options, inflation-linked, repo (secured short-term lending, often sits with a funding/financing desk), and emerging-market local rates.

**In practice.** Clients such as pension funds, insurers, asset managers, banks and central banks use rates desks to hedge interest-rate risk and to express views on central-bank moves. The BIS Triennial Survey (April 2025) found over-the-counter interest rate derivatives trading of about US$7.9 trillion per day, up 59% from three years earlier (BIS, 30 September 2025). Coalition Greenwich reported double-digit revenue growth in G10 rates in 2025 (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026).

**Who:** the big global banks plus strong European players (Barclays, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas, Société Générale, UBS, and in Italy Intesa Sanpaolo and UniCredit, which are major Italian government bond (BTP) market makers; Italian bank postings such as Intesa's "Rates Solutions Analyst" graduate role appeared in Milan in 2026, unverified dates). Rates is the most electronic of the big FICC businesses for vanilla products.

### 2.3 Credit (investment grade, high yield, distressed, securitised/structured)

**Definition.** Credit desks trade corporate (and bank and sovereign) bonds and credit derivatives (credit default swaps, CDS, which work like insurance against default) and loans.
- **Investment grade (IG):** bonds of companies rated BBB-/Baa3 or higher; high volumes, thin margins, increasingly electronic.
- **High yield (HY):** lower-rated issuers; wider spreads, more analysis-heavy, bigger per-trade risk.
- **Distressed:** bonds and loans of companies in or near default; trades are few, large and legal/analysis-heavy, and the work overlaps with restructuring and hedge funds.
- **Securitised/structured credit:** mortgage-backed and asset-backed securities (pools of loans turned into bonds) and CLOs (pools of corporate loans). Coalition reported double-digit growth in securitised products in 2025, while plain credit was "stable" (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026).

**In practice.** Credit bonds trade "over the counter" (OTC, bilaterally, not on an exchange) in many thousands of different issues, so liquidity is patchy and traders hold inventory. Clients: asset managers, insurers, hedge funds, pension funds. Credit has seen the largest shift from phone to electronic trading in the past decade, especially in IG.

### 2.4 FX (foreign exchange: spot, forwards, options, e-FX)

**Definition.** FX desks trade currencies: spot (immediate exchange), forwards and swaps (exchange at a future date), and options. "e-FX" means automated pricing and execution through electronic platforms.

**In practice.** The BIS 2025 Triennial Survey found OTC FX turnover of US$9.6 trillion per day in April 2025, up 28% from 2022; the US dollar was on one side of 89% of trades; spot was 31% of turnover (BIS, 30 September 2025; final results published June 2026, so figures could be revised). Clients are corporates hedging foreign revenues, asset managers, hedge funds, central banks, and other banks. A big share is flow: tiny spreads on huge volume, so FX is the most automated of the human-run desks. Coalition reported double-digit growth in G10 FX in 2025.

**Where:** London is the traditional global hub for FX and rates (Asian hubs and New York follow the sun; the "FX day" runs Sydney-Tokyo-Singapore/Hong Kong-London-New York).

### 2.5 Commodities (bank desks)

**Definition.** Bank commodities desks trade energy (oil, gas, power), metals (precious and base) and agricultural products, mainly via derivatives (futures, swaps, options), and sometimes physical. Clients: producers, airlines, utilities, miners, funds. Coalition reported a slight decline in commodities revenue at its 12 banks in 2025 (Coalition Greenwich, 7 May 2026). Many banks have shrunk commodity desks since 2010 because of capital rules and low returns; the large physical positions have gone to the independent trading houses (2.10). Bank desks that remain tend to be hedging and structuring businesses for corporate clients, plus precious metals.

### 2.6 Equities: cash equities, equity derivatives, delta one, prime brokerage and financing

- **Cash equities:** buying and selling shares for institutions. It has two halves. *High-touch* means a sales trader talks to a client and works a large order carefully; *low-touch* means the client sends orders through algorithms and electronic access with little human involvement. Revenue is commissions. Coalition reported cash equities revenue up 17.4% to US$12.2bn in 2025.
- **Equity derivatives:** options, structured notes, volatility trading, autocallables and similar products on shares and indices. Coalition: US$24.7bn, up 21.4% in 2025. This is among the most quantitative desks, with a structuring function (3.3).
- **Delta one:** products whose value moves one-for-one with an underlying asset: ETFs, index and total-return swaps, futures, and baskets. Margins are thin, volumes high, and the work is a mix of trading, financing and technology.
- **Prime brokerage and financing:** services to hedge funds: lending cash and shares (securities lending, margin), clearing, custody and reporting. It is a "balance-sheet" business that earns financing spread. Coalition: prime services and futures US$34.5bn, up 21.5% in 2025.

In 2025 equities revenue grew faster than FICC at the 12 big banks; Johnson Associates' May 2026 projections expected equity sales and trading incentives to rise 10-15% for 2026 and fixed income 5-10% (Johnson Associates, 7 May 2026).

### 2.7 Electronic and algorithmic trading, and what it did to headcount

Electronic trading means automated pricing, execution and risk management. Banks run "e-trading" or "digital markets" businesses (J.P. Morgan's markets programme lists "Digital Markets" next to Sales, Trading and Structuring & Origination, per job-board listings, unverified). Evidence on headcount:
- Banks' front-office staff overall: about 51,000 at the top investment banks at the end of H1 2025, per Crisil Coalition Greenwich figures cited in a news listing (headline only; the underlying article was not opened).
- Goldman Sachs International (London) reported FICC sales & trading headcount down 6% (694 to 655) in 2025 even though FICC revenue rose 13% to US$3.6bn; equities sales & trading headcount rose 1.1%; average pay per head at GSI was US$963k (up 21%) against US$399k for Goldman globally (eFinancialCareers citing GSI's annual report, 16 March 2026). This is one London entity and the pay average is skewed by seniors.
- Dealer-to-client trading's share of total volumes fell from 83.5% (2017) to 75.2% (2025 year to date), according to Crisil Coalition Greenwich (via a news summary; asset classes not stated), consistent with more volume going through all-to-all platforms and non-bank market makers.
- Electronic equities desks in 2024 expected to add staff in algorithmic sales and coverage (about one-third of respondents) with one participant expecting long-term cuts as AI replaces traders (The TRADE reporting Coalition Greenwich, 16 July 2024).
- Johnson Associates (May 2026) says firms are reducing analyst-to-manager ratios, outsourcing or offshoring some entry-level roles (e.g., to India) and treating AI fluency as a hiring prerequisite, and describes a "shrinking junior talent pipeline" (Johnson Associates, 7 May 2026).

The net effect (inference from the sources above): fewer voice traders per dollar of revenue, more technologists and quants on the desk, and a premium for people who can both understand markets and code. Human sales and trading roles remain in complex, large or illiquid products.

### 2.8 Sales vs trading vs structuring

- **Sales** covers clients: you call the portfolio manager at a fund, tell them what moved in the market, pitch ideas and get orders to the desk. You are paid on client revenue ("sales credits").
- **Trading** prices and holds risk. A trader quotes prices when a client asks, decides how much inventory to take, hedges and is responsible for the desk's profit and loss (P&L).
- **Structuring** (also "solutions", "origination" or "strats" in some banks) designs customised products and prices them with quants and traders, e.g., a note that pays a coupon if an index stays above a level.
- **Sales trading** (equities, some credit) is a hybrid: it executes client orders and advises on timing and liquidity, closer to the client than a market-making trader, closer to the market than a salesperson.

Typically ~50-60% of desk staff are sales and sales-trading, 25-35% traders and 5-15% structurers and strats (practitioner estimate, unverified; mix varies by desk).

### 2.9 Sell-side equity research and credit research

Sell-side research analysts cover a sector (e.g., European banks) and publish models, notes and ratings (buy/hold/sell) for institutional investors, and they talk to clients, accompany management meetings and help the sales force. Credit research analysts cover bond issuers, focusing on whether the company can repay (downside) rather than upside. Research is paid for indirectly through trading commissions and, after the MiFID II unbundling rules (EU/UK, January 2018), had to be priced separately; budgets for externally purchased research fell 20-30%, according to the FCA estimate cited by the University of Bath (2024), and research analyst headcount at the 12 largest banks had already fallen 20% between 2012 and mid-2019 (Integrity Research, 2019). In July 2024 the FCA (PS24/9) allowed UK buy-side firms to pay jointly for research and execution again, effective 1 August 2024, and the EU's Listing Act (effective 4 December 2024) allows similar bundling under conditions. Industry commentary (CRISIL, 2024) argues the reversal is probably too late to bring back the old model. Rankings still matter: in the Extel 2025 All-America survey, J.P. Morgan again led the table by team positions (49) and Evercore ISI was ranked No. 1 for analysts on a weighted basis for the fourth year running.

### 2.10 Physical commodity trading houses and energy majors' trading arms

**Definition.** Trading houses buy, store, ship, blend and sell physical commodities (crude oil and products, gas/LNG, power, metals, grains and softs) and use futures and swaps to hedge. They earn margin from logistics, location, time and quality arbitrage, financing and optionality, plus trading profit. Big independents: Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore (trading plus mining), Gunvor (rebranded Centalion on 2 October 2026), Mercuria, plus Cargill, ADM, Louis Dreyfus, Bunge and COFCO in agriculture. Energy majors (Shell, bp, TotalEnergies, Equinor, ExxonMobil, Eni) run trading arms; The Economist (2026, via search summary) said the big three trade 40-50m barrels of oil equivalent per day.

**Scale (company-reported or press-reported):** Vitol earned US$4.2bn in 2025 (down from more than double that) and paid US$5.9bn to employee-owners (about 600) through share buybacks (Bloomberg, in The Star, 27 July 2026). Reuters sources put Vitol's 2025 net profit at about US$4.5bn and revenue at US$343bn (Reuters, via Euronext, 2026; the figures differ, so treat both as approximate). Trafigura reported FY2025 (to 30 September 2025) net profit of US$2.67bn and over 1,400 employee shareholders (Trafigura release, December 2025, via the project's earlier research file) and strong early-2026 results (US$4.1bn for October-March, Kitco/Reuters, 4 June 2026). Gunvor's 2025 net profit fell 85% to US$104m after writedowns; the firm, now Centalion, reports more than 2,000 staff and plans to move its corporate headquarters from Cyprus to Singapore; its trading offices stay in Singapore, Geneva and Houston with hubs in Calgary, London and Stamford (Centalion release, 2 October 2026).

**Hubs.** Geneva (Vitol, Trafigura, Gunvor, Mercuria all have major Geneva offices), London, Singapore (Asia desks; regional headquarters), Houston (US energy), Dubai (smaller but growing; Shell reportedly moving a Madrid gas trading team to Dubai, reported January 2025), Rotterdam (oil products/shipping), Stamford and Calgary. About the Swiss cluster: commodity trading contributed CHF 19.2bn of value added in 2024 (2.3% of GDP), 58% of it in Geneva (Swiss Federal Statistical Office via blue News, 18 November 2025, via earlier project research).

### 2.11 Discretionary trading at hedge funds, and how it differs from bank trading

Discretionary traders and portfolio managers (PMs) at macro funds and multi-manager "pod" shops (Millennium, Citadel, Point72, Balyasny, Schonfeld and others) take directional or relative-value positions on rates, FX, credit, equities or commodities with fund capital, based on judgment (as opposed to computer-driven "systematic" strategies). The key differences from a bank trader: (1) no client flow to serve, so no spread income, only price views; (2) pay is a share of your own P&L (typical PM payouts of 10-20% of P&L in the US, 5-20% in London for fund PMs, Selby Jennings 2024); (3) strict risk limits and drawdown stops at multi-manager funds, so a bad few months can end a seat; (4) smaller teams and faster career highs and lows. Detail on hedge fund investing roles is in the buy-side part.

### 2.12 Briefly: proprietary/electronic market makers and buy-side execution desks

Proprietary trading firms such as Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Optiver, IMC and Susquehanna (SIG) make markets with their own capital and highly automated systems; recruitment is quantitative and covered in the quant part. **Buy-side execution (dealing) desks** sit inside asset managers (e.g., BlackRock's global trading team executes for all its portfolio managers across asset classes, per BlackRock job postings) and place orders with banks and electronic venues; they are the "buyer" of the sell side's service.

## 3. Role families

### 3.1 Trader at a bank (market-making)

**What you actually do.** You price risk for clients and manage the book (the desk's inventory of positions). When a salesperson says a pension fund wants to sell GBP 200m of 10-year gilts, you decide the price, take the risk, then lay it off in the market or hold it. As a junior you build the daily P&L explanation, update pricing tools in Excel/Python, keep an eye on limits, execute small flows and shadow the senior trader; as a senior you set the quotes, size positions and deal with risk managers and regulators. Tools: Bloomberg terminals, bank pricing systems, trading platforms (e.g., MarketAxess, Tradeweb, Bloomberg), voice and chat; coding (Python, SQL, sometimes C++ or kdb+) is increasingly expected.

*Desk notes (what changes by desk):*
- **Rates:** high-volume, quantitative, driven by central banks and data releases; vanilla desks are electronic; options and exotics need strong maths. Typical start 6:30-7:00am London time.
- **Credit (IG/HY/distressed/securitised):** inventory-heavy and more analytical. IG is moving to electronic; HY and distressed need credit judgement and legal reading; securitised products need cash-flow modelling. Slower tempo but liquidity can vanish in stress.
- **FX:** the most electronic; spot traders manage a flow book with huge volume and tiny margins; options traders work on volatility. The day is long because FX runs around the clock; Asia-Pacific and London hubs start early.
- **Equities:** cash equities trading is increasingly algorithmic; derivatives traders ("vol traders") are the most quant; delta one traders work on baskets and ETFs. In practitioner opinion London cash equities has among the better hours (eFinancialCareers opinion piece, 10 June 2021).
- **Commodities:** fewer bank seats; hours follow the energy/metal exchanges; more hedging-oriented and weather/geopolitics-driven.

**A typical day and week.** (Practitioner consensus, not a survey.) 6:30-7:00am: read overnight news, check positions, prepare morning meeting; 7:30: desk meeting, view of the day; 8:00-4:30: quoting, hedging, talking to sales; after close: reconcile P&L, review risk, plan tomorrow. Weeks are structured around data releases (US payrolls, inflation), central-bank meetings, auctions, earnings and month/quarter-end. Evenings are generally free except in volatile markets; client entertainment is more a sales task.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle.** Hours: typically 50-65 per week, peak 70+ in crisis weeks or for juniors; an eFinancialCareers writer put S&T at 50-70 with a median nearer 50 (opinion piece, 10 June 2021) and another (15 May 2026) described the week as arriving a few hours before the open and staying a couple of hours after, roughly stable through the year with peaks around earnings and market shocks. The 2021 writer also claimed hours are longest in Hong Kong, then London, then New York (opinion). **Stress: 4/5** (5 in volatile markets: you are the person holding the risk and your P&L is posted daily). Lifestyle: office-based; the desk must sit together for compliance (recording of lines) so remote work is rare; travel is limited (some conferences and client visits); weekend work is rare except in crises.

**Human vs. quantitative profile.** People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. Traders talk constantly (to sales, other traders, brokers) but the core skill is probabilistic thinking, mental arithmetic under pressure and risk discipline; coding is a growing differentiator.

**Compensation (approximate).** All figures are dated and from recruiters or press, not payroll data.
- **US (New York), 2025:** Selby Jennings' NYC guide (2025, based on placement data) shows trading base salary of US$125k for analysts, US$175k associates, US$200-275k VPs, US$300-350k directors, US$400-600k MDs. Total pay: analyst US$175-250k (equity derivatives up to US$275k); associate US$250-425k (credit US$250-425k, rates US$250-450k, FX US$250-400k, equity derivatives US$250-450k); VP US$400-850k; director US$600k-1.2m; MD US$800k to around US$2.5-2.75m (the PDF prints "2.5k" and "2.75MM"; I read the "2.5k" as 2.5MM). A different source, Wall Street Prep (updated December 2023, so older), shows a first-year analyst on US$85k base with US$50-75k bonus (all-in US$135-160k) and associates at US$150k base with US$90-130k bonus. The two disagree by roughly US$40-50k at analyst level; the difference reflects the year, the firms covered and the way the numbers were gathered (recruiter placements vs an educational site).
- **UK (London):** I found no recruiter guide that I could open for London S&T pay. A 2025 London/Europe guide seen only as a search-result summary (a page that returned 404 when opened; treat as unverified) gives first-year analyst base of about £60-65k, bonus 25-60% and total of £75-105k, with bulge-bracket analysts at £90-140k total and VPs £280-400k, and says European banks pay 10-15% less. The closest verified London sell-side bands are Selby Jennings' 2024 quant guide (so for quant-heavy desks, not typical traders): Associate/AVP base £75-120k, total £85-150k; VP base £115-180k, total £135-250k; Director £240-450k total; MD base £250-400k, total £600k-1.2m. - **Hong Kong/Singapore:** Selby Jennings' 2024 guide (quant-heavy): sell-side Associate base US$85-100k, total US$120-140k; VP base US$150-170k, total US$220-250k; MD total US$550-700k. For banks' IB analysts in 2024, Bloomberg Intelligence/Michael Page data show Hong Kong analysts at about US$92k vs US$63k in Singapore (investment banking, not S&T). I found no reliable S&T-specific figure for Tokyo or Dubai.
- **Italy (Milan):** no reliable data found for trader pay. The only formal proxy is Michael Page Italy's 2026 salary study for "investment banking, capital markets, private equity": M&A analyst/associate <5 years €30-55k gross annual salary (RAL), bonus 50-100% of base; asset management analyst €30-55k, bonus 15-25% (Michael Page Italy, 2026). Because the EU bonus cap applies and Milan desks are smaller, expect lower total pay than London for the same title (inference).
- **Bonus outlook:** Johnson Associates projected 2026 year-end incentives for equity sales and trading at +10-15% and fixed income sales and trading at +5-10% (7 May 2026).

**Career path.** US-style ladder: Analyst (years 1-3) → Associate (years 3-6) → Vice President (about year 6-9) → Director/Executive Director → Managing Director, with promotion tied to P&L and risk ownership (practitioner consensus). Many bank trainees rotate through sales, trading and structuring during the internship and are placed on a desk at graduation. Only a minority of juniors become head-of-desk traders; many move into sales or structuring, or out. Bonuses are discretionary and depend on bank, desk and year performance, and a portion at senior level is deferred (typical).

**Exit opportunities.** After 2-3 years: another bank desk, a hedge fund analyst or junior-trader seat, an electronic market maker (uncommon from voice desks), buy-side trading, MBA or a master's in finance, a move into structuring, risk or strategy. After 7-10 years: portfolio manager at a hedge fund (eFinancialCareers found that about one-third of 15 star traders identified in 2016 had moved to hedge funds as senior PMs or unit heads by early 2026 (23 February 2026); a sample of 15 is anecdotal), head of desk, asset management, commodity house, fintech/electronic trading firm, or exit to corporate treasury.

**Tier list of employers.** *Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.*
- **Tier 1 (global franchises, the largest markets revenues):** Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Morgan Stanley, Citi, Bank of America (the five US "bulge brackets"). Coalition's 12-bank index (2025) also includes Barclays, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, HSBC, Société Générale, UBS and Wells Fargo. Differences: scale, balance sheet, technology investment, the strongest client franchises, higher pay.
- **Tier 2 (strong in specific products or regions):** Barclays (rates/credit/FX), Deutsche Bank (rates, FX, flow), UBS, BNP Paribas (equity derivatives, rates), Société Générale (equity derivatives, structured products), Nomura (Japan/Asia), HSBC (Asia, FX), Jefferies, RBC, Macquarie (commodities), Mizuho/MUFG/SMBC.
- **Tier 3 (regional, smaller or boutique):** national champions and local players, e.g., in Italy Intesa Sanpaolo IMI CIB, UniCredit, Mediobanca (now within the Monte dei Paschi group) and Banca Akros (Banco BPM group), Equita and Intermonte in equities brokerage; mid-sized banks in Germany, Spain and Asia. Differences: narrower products, fewer graduate seats, lower pay, but often more responsibility early and strong local clients.

**How to enter.**
- *Degrees/backgrounds:* maths-heavy degrees (economics, finance, engineering, maths, physics, computer science) are favoured; recruiters in London and New York also take any strong student who can show market interest and numeracy. Nothing replaces strong exam results.
- *Internships and programmes:* the pipeline is the summer analyst programme, converted to a full-time offer. Morgan Stanley's Sales & Trading Summer Analyst Programme in London ran 10 weeks starting in June and rotated across institutional equities and fixed income, sales, trading and structuring; the 2027 London posting went live around 7 September 2026 (job-board listings, unverified). J.P. Morgan's 2027 "Markets" summer analyst track lists Sales, Trading, Structuring & Origination and Digital Markets (job boards, unverified); several J.P. Morgan 2027 Corporate & Investment Bank listings show deadlines around 1 November 2026, but the Markets deadline itself was not confirmed. Barclays' 2027 London graduate roles (Banking and Capital Markets) closed on 25 September 2026 according to Gradcracker; a third-party guide says Markets roles may stay open into January 2027 (unverified; Barclays page not confirmed). Goldman took about 2,500 interns in 2026 with a sub-1% acceptance rate for the third year (Fortune, 8 June 2026, via earlier project research). Asia: Morgan Stanley Japan's 2027 summer analyst deadlines were 16 August and 27 September 2026; Nomura's Asia Global Markets internship applications are expected to open around January 2027 (third-party guide, unverified). Italy: Intesa Sanpaolo's "I'M IN" international graduate programme has posted credit-trader and rates-solutions seats in Milan (mid-2026, listings only; details not readable).
- *Recruiting calendar (general pattern):* apply in the autumn (September-November) of the year before the summer internship (UK/Europe), or in the August-October window of junior year (US); spring weeks and early-insight programmes for first-years in the UK; off-cycle internships exist but are fewer for markets.
- *Certifications:* none required to start. Licences (e.g., US FINRA Series 7/63 or 57, UK FCA certification) are sponsored by the employer (general knowledge, unverified). CFA is of marginal value for trading; it is more useful for research and asset management (CFA Level I pass rates were 39-45% across 2025-26 windows, per the project's earlier research). Skills: Excel, Python, market knowledge (follow rates/FX daily), probability and mental arithmetic, a stock/market pitch, a view on what moved yesterday. Practise brainteasers and market-sense questions.
- *Common mistakes:* applying only to one product; not following markets; pitching "I like adrenaline" without a view; underestimating the numeracy test; applying late; ignoring Tier 2 and local banks.
- *Alternative routes:* lateral entry from middle office, trade support, operations, risk or control; a one-year finance master's (LSE, Imperial, Bocconi, HEC and similar) to earn an interview at off-cycle or graduate programmes; starting at a smaller bank or brokerage or in a regional hub and moving to a bigger bank; MFE/maths master's for derivatives. For non-target students, networking and demonstrable market projects (a trade journal, a tracked paper portfolio) matter more.
Entry difficulty: 4/5.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for.** P&L is visible every day and stressful; bonuses fall in bad years (and are discretionary); a quiet year can mean a desk closes (headcount in FICC at GSI fell 6% in 2025 despite higher revenue); electronic trading and AI will keep pressuring junior voice-trading seats; the work is repetitive on slow days; the first years are mostly support work; pay at Tier 3 and in Italy is much lower; and trading careers can end suddenly. Not a good fit if you dislike public mistakes, early hours or being measured in dollars daily, if you are uncomfortable with maths under time pressure, or if you want predictable job security.

### 3.2 Institutional salesperson (by product)

**What you actually do.** You cover a list of institutional clients (fund managers, pension funds, insurers, corporates, hedge funds), relay market colour and the desk's ideas, take orders and relay prices, set up trades, and sometimes arrange meetings with analysts and strategists. Deliverables: morning notes/calls, trade ideas, client lists, order tickets, "sales credits" (the revenue attributed to you). Tools: Bloomberg chat, phones, CRM, pricing screens, bank research.

*Desk notes:* 
- **Rates/credit sales:** relationship-driven but increasingly supported by electronic platforms; credit sales cover many mid-sized accounts and push bond inventory; rates sales include "solutions" to pension and insurance clients.
- **FX sales:** two models: institutional (asset managers, hedge funds) and corporate (hedging treasury flows), the latter more consultative. Electronic execution is the norm for small tickets.
- **Equities sales and sales trading:** cash equities salespeople cover long-only funds and hedge funds; sales traders execute orders and are on the phone/chat all day; derivatives sales covers vol and structured products with a heavier quant element.
- **Commodities sales:** hedging solutions for corporates (airlines, utilities, miners).

**A typical day and week.** (Practitioner consensus.) 7:00am start: morning call with the desk, a note to clients; 8:00-4:30 calls and chats, order handling; late afternoon: client meetings or calls; evenings: client dinners/entertainment 1-3 nights per week for senior people; Fridays are usually lighter. Travel: conferences, client roadshows, in-person visits are part of the job (Europe/Asia more than for traders).

**Hours, stress and lifestyle.** Hours: 55-65 per week typically, 70+ at peak; a 2021 reader comment on eFinancialCareers noted evening client entertainment should be counted in hours (opinion). **Stress: 3/5** (4 in volatile markets and on bonus days); the pressure is relationship-driven and revenue-driven; you are expected to be "always on" for key clients. Remote/hybrid: mostly office-based; some regulatory limits on working from home on a desk.

**Human vs. quantitative profile.** People/communication: 5/5. Quantitative/technical: 2/5 (3/5 for derivatives sales). You succeed by being trusted, accurate and fast in explaining products and moves; you need enough market maths to explain pricing.

**Compensation (approximate).**
- **US (NY), 2025 (Selby Jennings):** sales base: analyst US$125k credit/rates/FX, US$100-125k cash equity and corporate FX/rates, associate US$150-175k, VP US$175-275k, director US$250-400k, MD US$350-600k. Total pay: analyst US$175-225k credit and FX, US$175-250k rates and equity derivatives, US$150-225k corporate FX/rates, US$120-170k cash equity; associate US$250-375k (cash equity US$225-350k); VP US$400-700k (rates and equity derivatives up to US$750k; cash equity US$300-450k); director US$575-900k (cash equity US$450-600k); MD US$775k-2m (cash equity US$650k-1.4m).
- **UK:** no reliable data found for sales specifically; use the trader ranges above as a rough guide, noting that sales pay is usually somewhat lower than trading pay at the same level in the same desk (inference from the NY table, where sales totals are usually a little below trading totals).
- **Hong Kong/Singapore and Tokyo/Dubai:** no reliable S&T-specific data found; see 3.1 for 2024 quant-heavy sell-side bands.
- **Italy:** no reliable data found. Proxy: Michael Page Italy 2026 "business development manager" in the banking section is €30-55k RAL for under five years with a 50-60% bonus (Michael Page Italy, 2026).

**Career path.** Analyst (years 1-3) → Associate → VP (a "coverage" role: you own accounts) → Director → MD (a senior franchise sales person). Equity sales and sales traders can also move to "head of sales" roles. Promotion depends on client revenue and relationships; star salespeople can be very well paid.

**Exit opportunities.** After 2-3 years: another bank, a sales role at an electronic platform or broker, buy-side relationship role, corporate treasury or fintech sales. After 7-10 years: head of sales, institutional sales at an asset manager or fund (capital-introduction/ investor relations at hedge funds), or a client-facing role at an exchange or data provider.

**Tier list of employers.** *Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.*
- **Tier 1:** Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Morgan Stanley, Citi, Bank of America: global clients and best products to sell.
- **Tier 2:** Barclays, Deutsche Bank, UBS, BNP Paribas, Société Générale, HSBC, Nomura, Jefferies, RBC, Macquarie, Mizuho.
- **Tier 3:** regional banks and brokers (Intesa Sanpaolo IMI CIB, UniCredit, Equita in Italy; Santander, ING, Natixis, Commerzbank, Banca Akros; Asian regional firms): narrower client base, local language and relationships matter.

**How to enter.** Same programmes as 3.1 (summer analyst programmes that rotate across sales, trading and structuring). Any degree works if you show numeracy and communication; languages are a real advantage in Europe and Asia (Tokyo: BNP Paribas's 2027 Global Markets graduate programme in Tokyo lists fluent Japanese and English as must-haves, per its posting, via search results). Skills: public speaking, concise market summaries, product knowledge, empathy for client needs. Alternative routes: junior roles in sales support/trade support, client service at a broker or fund administrator, or a regional bank. Certifications: none needed; employer sponsors licences. Mistakes: being all personality and no market knowledge; failing to track the news; poor listening. Entry difficulty: 4/5.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for.** Your revenue depends on client activity you do not control; sales is the first function to shrink when execution goes electronic; client entertainment and early starts can be exhausting; you are often the "face" blamed when the desk quotes badly; pay has less upside than trading at the top. Not a good fit if you dislike constant phone/chat contact, if you hate client entertainment, or if you want to be mostly analytical.

### 3.3 Structurer

**What you actually do.** You design and price customised products and the supporting ideas for sales and clients. A typical equity derivatives structurer designs payoffs such as barrier options, worst-of structures, cliquets, digitals and autocallables (a note that redeems early if the underlying stays above a level), sometimes hybrids that mix equity, rates and FX, or quantitative investment strategies (QIS); then prices them with quants, checks they can be hedged, sizes capital and funding costs, and produces term sheets and pitch material (Citi, Selby Jennings and other postings, via search results). Compliance with product regulation (PRIIPs, MiFID II, benchmark rules) is part of the job. In rates, credit and FX, "solutions" or "origination" teams do similar work for liability management (helping issuers), hedging and structured notes. J.P. Morgan's markets programme includes "Structuring & Origination" as a distinct track (job-board listings, unverified).

**A typical day and week.** Morning: check volatility, correlation, dividend and funding levels; respond to sales requests (price this note for a private bank); build or adjust payoffs in a pricing tool (Python/Excel/internal libraries); coordinate with traders on hedge costs and with quants on model changes; prepare term sheets and pitches; afternoon calls with clients or private banks; review with legal/compliance. Multiple live requests at once; pitch deadlines.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle.** Hours: 50-60 per week typically, 65+ near product launches or pitch deadlines (practitioner consensus, unverified). **Stress: 3/5.** Hybrid arrangements and some remote work are more possible than on a trading floor because it is less tied to the market clock. Travel: client visits, particularly in Europe and Asia where the retail structured product market is large (France, Italy, Switzerland, Hong Kong, Singapore).

**Human vs. quantitative profile.** People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. You need to explain complex payoffs simply and be rigorous in pricing and risk.

**Compensation (approximate).** No recruiter table for structurers alone could be opened. Selby Jennings' 2025 NYC guide has no separate structuring row; equity derivatives (trading and sales) are the nearest: trading analysts US$175-275k total, associates US$250-450k, VPs US$400-850k (Selby Jennings, 2025). UK/Italy/Asia: no reliable data found. The Selby Jennings 2024 quant guide shows sell-side quant bands for London (Associate/AVP total £85-150k; VP total £135-250k) and Hong Kong/Singapore (associate US$120-140k total, VP US$220-250k), which sit near structurer/strat pay but are not the same job (2024).

**Career path.** Analyst (2-3 years) → Associate → VP (about 6-8 years, often hired laterally from a quant or structurer role) → Director → MD (head of structuring for a product or region). Many structurers start as quants/strats, graduates of mathematics and engineering programmes, and move into structuring; others rotate from sales or trading.

**Exit opportunities.** After 2-3 years: trading, sales, quant roles at banks, insurance product teams, private bank product teams. After 7-10 years: head of structuring, derivatives sales at a hedge fund, product control, fintech/robo-advice product design, or asset management product development.

**Tier list of employers.** *Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.*
- **Tier 1:** Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Morgan Stanley, Citi, Bank of America and the strongest European derivatives houses: BNP Paribas and Société Générale (known for equity derivatives and structured products), Barclays, Deutsche Bank, UBS.
- **Tier 2:** Nomura, HSBC, Natixis, Crédit Agricole CIB, other global banks on selected products, large Italian banks (Intesa Sanpaolo, UniCredit) and Mediobanca for Italian retail certificates.
- **Tier 3:** regional banks, brokers and small specialist houses.

**How to enter.** Strong maths/finance background (mathematics, engineering, physics, quantitative finance MSc such as Bocconi's MAFinRisk, whose placement page lists Mediobanca, Intesa Sanpaolo and UniCredit among recruiters in 2023-25 and says most graduates go into trading, risk, sales or structuring). Entry points: summer analyst programmes (structuring track), graduate programmes, strats/quant teams. Skills: derivatives pricing (Black-Scholes, volatility surfaces), Python, a clear way of explaining payoffs. CFA is not a signal; an MFE/quantitative master's is. Common mistakes: focusing on the maths with no communication; ignoring regulation; applying only to the biggest firms. Alternative routes: quant analyst/strat first, then structuring; ex-trader/associate moves; product control/valuation roles with strong derivatives knowledge. Entry difficulty: 4/5.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for.** The seats are few; structured product demand depends on volatility, rates and investor risk appetite; pressure to produce "something new" for pitches; the role is in the middle (sales wants cheaper, risk wants safer); regulators scrutinise retail products; and pay is typically below top traders. Not a good fit if you dislike repetitive pricing work, if you want direct P&L ownership, or if you are not comfortable with derivatives mathematics.

### 3.4 Sell-side equity research analyst (and credit research)

**What you actually do.** You cover a sector (for example European banks, Italian utilities or US software), build and maintain financial models for 15-25 companies, write earnings notes, long reports (initiations) and thematic pieces, set a rating and target price, talk to company management, speak to clients and support the sales and trading desks. Credit research analysts cover bond issuers and focus on the ability to repay: leverage, cash flow, covenants, relative value between bonds, and recommendations for investment grade or high yield. They typically support the trading and sales desks and travel with sales to client meetings (sell-side postings, via search results). Associates (juniors) do the modelling and data gathering. Deliverables: models, notes, call notes, marketing presentations. Compliance and conflict-of-interest rules restrict what analysts can say and do, especially around investment banking deals.

**A typical day and week.** (Practitioner/secondary: ctacquisitions.com, 2026; undated.) Earnings season (about four two-week windows a year): review the results, update the model, write a short reaction note that goes through compliance, join the call, host client calls in the afternoon. Outside earnings: maintain models, publish one or two notes a week, run expert calls and channel checks, build initiation reports over weeks. Travel for client marketing: about 30% of the year at senior level (same source, unverified).

**Hours, stress and lifestyle.** Hours: typically 55-65, up to 70-80 in earnings season (ctacquisitions.com, 2026, a secondary source citing aggregated data; an eFinancialCareers opinion piece of 2021 claimed research has the longest hours of the S&T-type roles, also opinion). **Stress: 3/5.** Pressure peaks at earnings and in annual client rankings (Extel); you must be right in public. Hybrid is more possible than on trading floors, but analysts are on call during results.

**Human vs. quantitative profile.** People/communication: 3/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Modelling and accounting skills are core; writing and the ability to build a clear investment view matter equally.

**Compensation (approximate).**
- **US:** a 2026 secondary guide gives first-year analyst total pay of US$165-250k (2025 benchmarks), year 2-3 US$200-330k, senior associate US$275-550k, VP/sector head US$525k-1.2m, director US$0.9-2.2m, MD US$1.5-8m+ (ctacquisitions.com, 2026; cites recruiter sources; not independently verified). Bonuses depend on rankings, sector revenue and client votes. A different, older educational source (WallStreetMojo, undated) claims research bonuses are much smaller than IB (0-25% of base vs 50-100%); the two do not match, and I could not verify either.
- **UK, Italy, Asia:** no reliable data found. Michael Page Italy's 2026 study lists "asset management analyst/manager" at €30-55k RAL (<5 years) with a 15-25% bonus; equity research is not itemised (Michael Page Italy, 2026).
- Johnson Associates (May 2026) lists "Equities" (the traditional asset management category) up 5%, but gives no separate research figure.

**Career path.** Research associate (0-2 years) → senior associate (2-4) → analyst with own coverage (about 4-7) → senior analyst/sector head; ranking in the Extel survey drives pay and mobility. A common benchmark: analyst status takes about five years plus an MBA/finance degree or CFA (secondary guide). Research is a smaller industry than sales & trading, and the number of analysts has fallen (see 2.9).

**Exit opportunities.** After 2-3 years: the buy side (hedge fund analyst, long-only analyst), corporate development/investor relations, equity capital markets, consulting. After 7-10 years: portfolio manager, senior analyst at a long/short fund, head of research, CIO roles, or corporate IR/strategy. The ctacquisitions.com guide lists exits as: hedge funds about 45%, long-only 15%, corporate roles 10%, PE public-markets 8%, consulting 5%, operating companies 5%, staying in research 12% (its own estimates, unverified); a Euromoney poll reported that senior sell-side credit analysts were also leaving for the buy side. A 2024 academic study found high-quality analysts were more likely to leave the sell-side for the buy-side after MiFID II (as summarised in search results).

**Tier list of employers.** *Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.*
- **Tier 1 (top Extel/institutional rankings, global coverage):** J.P. Morgan (top the 2025 All-America research team table by number of positions), Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, Citi; Evercore ISI (No. 1 by weighted analyst rank for four consecutive years to 2025), Bernstein.
- **Tier 2:** Barclays, UBS, Deutsche Bank, BNP Paribas Exane, Jefferies, Wells Fargo, RBC, Mizuho, Redburn Atlantic and strong independents.
- **Tier 3:** regional brokers and local research houses (in Italy Equita, Intermonte, Banca Akros, Intesa and Mediobanca's research teams); in credit, the research team may be small or merged into the strategy team.

**How to enter.** Degrees: finance, accounting, economics, engineering/science for sector specialists. Programmes: research summer internships (smaller than S&T), graduate programmes (e.g., Barclays' 2027 Capital Markets graduate listing mentions research and economic analysis among teams, per a job listing). Skills: building a three-statement model, valuation, writing a stock pitch (one or two prepared), accounting. Licences: in the US, Series 86/87 for research analysts are sponsored by the employer (ctacquisitions.com; unverified). CFA: the charter is not required but passing Level 2 is what most hiring managers look for, according to that guide; CFA Level I pass rates were 39-45% across the four windows from November 2025 to August 2026 (CFA Institute, via the project's earlier research). Common mistakes: writing generic pitches; weak accounting; ignoring industry knowledge; submitting too late. Alternative routes: junior analyst at a regional broker; buy-side research assistant; industry experience (engineer, doctor, lawyer) plus modelling skills for a sector team; equity research associate at an asset manager and later switch. Entry difficulty: 3/5.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for.** A shrinking, structurally challenged business: MiFID II cut budgets 20-30% and many banks cut analysts; pay is lower than trading and banking at the same level (US junior pay sources agree it is lower than trading but disagree on how much); the job is constrained by compliance and by banking conflicts; you are judged in public; and much of the work is repetitive earnings-season modelling. Bonuses depend on client votes and rankings. Not a good fit if you want dealmaking, trading or fast promotion; if you dislike writing and detailed models; or if you cannot accept being wrong in public.

### 3.5 Trader/analyst at a physical commodity trading house or energy trading arm

**What you actually do.** Trading houses buy cargoes of crude oil, fuel, LNG, metals or grain, arrange shipping, storage, insurance and financing, hedge price risk with futures and swaps, and sell to refiners, utilities and manufacturers. A *trader* owns a book (e.g., naphtha in the Mediterranean, copper concentrate, Brent time spreads), negotiates deals, manages margin, and balances logistics. *Analysts* (junior) build position and P&L reports, curve and price analyses, fundamentals models, and support the trader; a Vitol-style "Commercial Analyst" maintains position and pricing reports, daily P&L, curve integrity and mark-to-market (search-result summary of Vitol's early careers page; unverified). Operators/logistics staff schedule ships and cargoes; risk and finance staff manage credit exposure and trade finance. Tools: Excel, Python, Bloomberg/Platts/Argus price assessments, internal trade systems, shipping platforms.

*Desk notes:* crude/products desks are the most senior and best paid; LNG/gas/power are growing; metals and agriculture have different supply chains and calendars; majors' trading arms (Shell, bp, TotalEnergies, Equinor) mix asset-backed trading with structured graduate rotations.

**A typical day and week.** Early morning review of overnight prices (Asia open) and shipping news; a morning meeting with the book; calls to brokers and counterparties through the London/Geneva day; nominations, credit-line checks, hedging; evenings or early mornings for time-zone overlap with Singapore or Houston. Week: bunker/loading windows and price benchmarks drive crunch points. Travel varies: junior analysts travel little, senior traders travel to counterparties, ports and conferences.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle.** Hours: 55-70 per week typical, 70+ in volatile markets (practitioner anecdote; I found no reliable survey). **Stress: 4/5** (large positions, 24-hour markets and operational risk). A forum thread said trading houses are much faster-paced than the majors, but that is hearsay. Lifestyle: office-based in Geneva, London, Singapore, Houston, Dubai; relocation between hubs is common; Singapore and Dubai attract people for lifestyle and tax (Dubai: zero personal income tax, general knowledge).

**Human vs. quantitative profile.** People/communication: 4/5. Quantitative/technical: 3/5. Trading is relational (counterparties, brokers, shipping, partners) and entrepreneurial, with solid numeracy and commercial judgement.

**Compensation (approximate).**
- **Geneva/Switzerland:** a recruiter (Upreer, 2026, method undisclosed) says graduate traders start at CHF 90-130k and established traders earn CHF 150-300k base with bonuses that can double it; ERI SalaryExpert estimates the average Geneva securities and commodities trader at CHF 103,862 base plus CHF 16,878 bonus (updated October 2025). An older recruiter table (2018, outside the two-year window) put analysts at CHF 70-95k. I found no verified graduate survey.
- **London:** Upreer gives approximately £70-100k at entry level (same caveat).
- **Singapore:** Payscale (2026) shows a SGD 87k median base for commodities traders (25th-75th percentile SGD 63-112k; bonus S$9-48k).
- **Ownership pay is the real money:** Reuters reported (2024) that most traders at Vitol and Trafigura are paid a percentage of their book's profit and that partners receive large dividends; Vitol paid US$5.9bn to roughly 600 employee-owners for 2025 on US$4.2bn profit (Bloomberg in The Star, 27 July 2026), Vitol paid US$6.5bn to employee-owners in 2022 (about 450 owners, Bloomberg via Swissinfo), and Trafigura has more than 1,400 employee shareholders. These are averages for a small group of partners and cannot be read as graduate pay. Majors pay less cash and more deferred stock, and struggle to match independents' bonuses (Reuters 2024, as summarised in search results). Hedge funds also hired oil traders with seven-figure signing bonuses (Bloomberg, 2022; outside the two-year window).
- **US, Italy, Dubai:** no reliable data found. Eni's paid master's in Milan (Politecnico di Milano/Torino) pays €1,500 gross per month for STEM graduates (Eni, earlier project research).

**Career path.** Typical at independents: junior analyst/trading assistant (years 1-3) → trader or "senior analyst" (3-6) → trader with own book (5-10) → senior trader/desk head → partner (in some houses, with equity). Graduate programmes: Trafigura offers two-year Commercial (towards junior trading), Development (operations, deals desk, trade finance) and Technical (trading IT, data science, research analysis) tracks in Geneva, Singapore, Houston, Calgary, Montevideo and Shanghai; TotalEnergies offers a two-year programme with four six-month rotations; Gunvor/Centalion has a graduate programme with rotations through trading, risk, operations and analytics (company pages via earlier project research). Equinor has a two-year market analysis and trading graduate programme (search summary). Those programmes lead toward junior trading roles, not guaranteed trader seats; no house publishes how many graduates become traders.

**Exit opportunities.** After 2-3 years: move to another house, a major's trading arm, a bank's commodities desk, a commodity-focused hedge fund, a producer/refiner commercial role, or risk/finance roles. After 7-10 years: head a book or desk at a trading house, run a commodity fund, move to a producer or utility as commercial director, or start a trading business. Many exits are into trade finance, shipping and risk rather than trading seats.

**Tier list of employers.** *Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.*
- **Tier 1 (largest independents, highest upside):** Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore, Mercuria, Gunvor/Centalion (the "big five" by trading volume and reputation); in agriculture Cargill, ADM, Louis Dreyfus, Bunge, COFCO.
- **Tier 2:** energy majors' trading arms (Shell, bp, TotalEnergies, Equinor, ExxonMobil, Eni) with structured graduate programmes and better predictability; strong regional houses (Hartree, Freepoint, Castleton, Concord, Sucden, Aurubis in metals); bank commodities desks at a few banks (J.P. Morgan, Goldman, Macquarie).
- **Tier 3:** smaller independents, utilities' trading units (Uniper, Axpo, EDF Trading, Enel), regional brokers; they offer less pay but sometimes more breadth early.

**How to enter.** Backgrounds: commodities/energy economics, finance, engineering, logistics and supply chain, maths/data science. Graduate programmes are small and annual: Trafigura's page on 30 September 2026 showed most 2027 intakes closed, with the China programme open to 23 October 2026; Gunvor's programme asks for a 40-minute maths/logic/Python test (company page, per earlier research). The University of Geneva MSc in Commodity Trading is effectively a hiring funnel: in 2024, 170 applied, 100 were shortlisted and 33 were placed in paid company traineeships (UNIGE, 2024, via earlier research). Common mistakes: expecting a trading seat at graduation; ignoring operations/finance entry; applying to one house only; unfamiliarity with how oil/gas/metals markets work. Certifications: none required; shipping/trade finance courses and industry reading (Platts, Argus, Energy Intelligence, FT Commodities) help. Alternative entry: operations, trade finance, risk or analytics roles, then move to the desk; junior roles at brokers; energy-major graduate programmes; experience in a physical industry (shipping, refining, mining) then lateral. Entry difficulty: 4/5 (small intakes; no published acceptance rates; heavy referral/hiring through headhunters for experienced staff, Upreer 2026).

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for.** Results are cyclical (Vitol's 2025 profit halved; Gunvor's fell 85%, then 2026 profits jumped with the Hormuz-related disruption, per Reuters/Kitco), so bonuses swing; the culture can be closed and politically complex; reputational and legal controversies exist (e.g., fines/pleas by Glencore, Trafigura and Vitol, per earlier research); you may be relocated, and hubs are small, expensive cities; opaque pay; and the best money is for partners. Not a good fit if you want transparency, regular hours, or a global brand name; if you dislike negotiation; or if you are uncomfortable working near fossil-fuel industries.

### 3.6 Discretionary trader/PM at a hedge fund or macro fund

**What you actually do.** You take positions, using the fund's capital, based on your judgement of macro, rates, FX, credit, commodities or equities. A junior "analyst" or "associate trader" at a macro or multi-manager fund supports a PM by building trade ideas, running risk, doing data work and executing; a PM runs their own book with risk limits and a P&L target. A discretionary macro trader reads central banks, economic data, positioning and flows and then expresses views through futures, swaps, options and FX. Pod-shop PMs usually have strict stop-loss limits; macro funds (Brevan Howard, Rokos, Caxton, Soros-type) allow larger views. Tools: Bloomberg, in-house risk tools, Python, execution through banks.

**A typical day and week.** Early start tracking Asia and Europe; reviewing positions and risk; research on themes; calls with sell-side salespeople and strategists; trades around data releases and central bank meetings; end-of-day risk review. Weeks revolve around data, central bank meetings and position management.

**Hours, stress and lifestyle.** Hours: 55-70 typical; 70+ in stress periods (practitioner consensus; no survey). **Stress: 5/5**: your P&L is your livelihood and hitting a drawdown limit can end the seat quickly. Pay is variable. Lifestyle: office-based (funds are hybrid for analysts, but PMs sit near risk), London, New York, Greenwich, Hong Kong, Singapore, Dubai and Geneva hubs.

**Human vs. quantitative profile.** People/communication: 2/5. Quantitative/technical: 4/5. The job is mostly decision-making under uncertainty and risk management; the network (to sell-side and to talk to) matters, but it is a solo-ish performance role.

**Compensation (approximate).**
- **US:** Selby Jennings (2024): fund PM base US$200-300k, payout 10-20% of P&L; quant trader at a prop/HFT firm base US$150-200k and 30-50% of P&L. Top hedge fund quant PhD hires US$300-750k total. Johnson Associates (May 2026) projects hedge fund incentives +2.5% to 10%+ in 2026 with wide variation; macro, commodities, prop and quant "outperforming," event-driven under; a "talent war for star PMs" and growing pass-through fees pushing pay up.
- **UK:** Selby Jennings (2024): fund PM base £120-250k, payout 5-20% of P&L.
- **Asia, Italy:** no reliable data found (Italy has few discretionary macro funds). Hong Kong/Singapore quant researcher bands in the 2024 guide are US$120-240k total for top hedge funds with PhDs, not typical for discretionary roles.
- **Entry level:** junior analyst seats at hedge funds are usually filled by people with 1-4 years at a bank, so there is no "entry" salary table; a London buy-side execution role was advertised at £70-90k and mid-to-senior traders at hedge funds at £150-250k in job ads (search results; unverified).

**Career path.** Typical path: bank analyst/associate (2-5 years in sales, trading or research) → hedge fund analyst or junior trader (2-4 years) → PM (with risk capital) → senior PM / CIO. Others start in prop trading or at a pod shop. Many do not make PM: seats are scarce and the attrition rate is high. eFinancialCareers' 2026 analysis of 15 star traders from 2016 found about a third had moved to hedge funds as senior PMs or unit heads; only four of the 15 still worked in banking (23 February 2026; small sample). In credit and distressed, bank MDs have moved to Verition and Schonfeld (same source).

**Exit opportunities.** After 2-3 years: another fund, a different strategy, the sell side, a prop firm or long-only manager. After 7-10 years: run your own fund (rare), family office CIO, senior PM at larger multi-manager, asset management or advisory, or leave finance. Non-competes in Europe can force 6-12 months (or up to three years in extreme cases) of gardening leave, according to Selby Jennings (2024).

**Tier list of employers.** *Tier groupings reflect industry consensus and practitioner perception, not objective fact.*
- **Tier 1 (multi-manager and top macro funds):** Millennium, Citadel, Point72, Balyasny, Brevan Howard, Rokos, Caxton, Bridgewater (systematic-macro hybrid), Soros Fund Management. Differences: capital, risk infrastructure, pay upside, strict limits.
- **Tier 2:** Schonfeld, Verition, ExodusPoint, Marshall Wace (macro and equity), Haidar, Qube (systematic), regional macro funds.
- **Tier 3:** smaller funds, family offices, emerging managers in Dubai, Hong Kong, Singapore, London.

**How to enter.** There is almost no direct graduate entry into discretionary PM roles. The route is: a strong sell-side desk seat (Tier 1 or Tier 2), strong P&L or an idea-generating track record, relationships with funds (via sales), and a headhunter. Some funds hire graduates as analysts (smaller intake; often via referral) or from investment banking/research. Skills: market view, risk discipline, scenario thinking, Python for analysis, and the ability to run a track record. Certifications: none required; CFA is helpful for fundamental equity. Mistakes: leaving a bank desk too early; building no P&L record; ignoring risk management. Alternative routes: prop trading, personal-account track record (rare), starting at a multi-manager as an analyst after a research or quant background. Entry difficulty: 5/5.

**Honest downsides and who it is NOT a good fit for.** High attrition; careers hinge on performance and capital allocators' patience; the 2025-26 pod-shop market has strict drawdown limits and long non-competes; compensation concentrates in a few stars; moves cost months on gardening leave; and Johnson Associates flags that AI firms and proprietary trading firms are competing for quantitative talent. Not a good fit if you want stable pay, work-life balance, or if you are uncomfortable with a personal P&L; also not a fit if you want a team environment or have little tolerance for losses.

**Commentary.**
- **Bank vs prop firm.** Prop and electronic market-making firms pay more at the start (self-reported estimates US$250-500k first-year total at top firms; investment banks about US$150-250k for strats and quant roles in the same dataset, Quantt Pay Index 2026, built on Glassdoor/Levels.fyi self-reports); summer intern pay is also high (e.g., Citadel and Balyasny about US$23k a month; Optiver over US$30k a month in Chicago and Austin; eFinancialCareers, 7 July 2026, citing Levels.fyi). eFinancialCareers also reported fixed income quant traders moving from banks to trading firms with an initial pay rise of about 20% (Hedgeweek, 29 November 2024). But a prop role is selected for quantitative problem-solving, not for client skills. The bank trader is a client-facing risk-taker; the prop trader is a market maker whose edge is in technology and models.
- **Bank vs hedge fund.** The bank trader's income is a salary plus a bonus pool decided by the bank; the hedge fund PM's income is a percentage of their own P&L but with much sharper job risk. Most hedge fund discretionary PMs come from banks.
- **Commodity house.** Higher upside and ownership (partners), but pay opacity and cyclical profits (Vitol 2025 profit halved; Gunvor down 85%; 2026 spike). Hubs are small; relocation is part of the career.
- **Asset manager desk.** Better hours and lower stress, lower pay, and a path to PM for some (e.g., a trader at Hermes who later headed credit trading, per The TRADE profile). Hours data for buy-side dealing desks: no reliable data found; London Asia night-desk postings run 7pm to the US equity close (HK/Singapore job ads).
- **Regional differences.**
  - **London:** the largest hub for FX, rates and European credit/equities, plus commodities. Pay is below New York for the same title, with a bonus cap removed since October 2023 for UK banks. London also hosts many prop firms and hedge funds.
  - **New York:** the largest US Treasury, credit and equities market; higher base salaries (analyst base US$125k at the average NYC firm, 2025); also the hub for US hedge fund PM jobs.
  - **Hong Kong/Singapore:** Asian time zone; trading, sales and structuring for Asian clients; Hong Kong pays more than Singapore on paper (2024 data, IB analysts, 46% higher), but a Reddit discussion suggested the gap is mostly for seniors (anecdote). Singapore is also the Asian hub for commodity trading houses (Gunvor/Centalion's corporate HQ move to Singapore announced 2 October 2026) and some banks relocate desks there (a gold-trading team move by J.P. Morgan was reported in late 2025 by a secondary aggregator; unverified).
  - **Tokyo:** large local brokers and banks (Nomura, Daiwa, Mizuho); graduate recruitment for foreign banks typically demands fluent Japanese (BNP Paribas Tokyo 2027 Global Markets programme lists fluent Japanese as a must-have); graduate programmes are fed by summer internships (Morgan Stanley Japan summer analyst deadlines passed on 16 August and 27 September 2026); Recruit Works Institute estimates about five graduate applicants per finance-sector position in Japan (2026, via search results).
  - **Dubai:** growing hedge fund and commodities hub, zero personal income tax, DIFC registered a record 1,924 new companies in 2025 (+28%) and 775 in Q1 2026 (+62%); bank sales-and-trading desk hiring is limited (I found no 2025-26 evidence of big-bank desk expansion), and Shell moved a gas trading team from Madrid to Dubai/London/Singapore (reported January 2025).
  - **Milan/Italy:** a mid-sized market; the main employers of traders and structurers are Intesa Sanpaolo (IMI CIB), UniCredit, Mediobanca (now under Monte dei Paschi after a 2025 takeover), Banca Akros and Equita; international banks have small Milan offices; pay is lower than London and the EU bonus cap applies. European banks' equity sales & trading revenue grew strongly in Q1 2026 (Teleborsa, 12 May 2026), but recruiting firm commentary describes Milan banks as cautious on hiring (Kitalent, unverified).
